\section{Executability of the measured gaps}
\label{sec:executability}

Every cost comparison in this paper has one observed side and one constructed side. What a trader received is in the record. What a direct pool between the same endpoints would have returned at the same reconstructed state, at the same size, is a quote for a route nobody took, produced by an engine that reproduces the venue's own invariant. A referee is entitled to ask whether that constructed side is a price a trader faced, and the question has more force here than in the exchange-rate settings this design is modelled on. \citet{MakarovSchoar2020Arbitrage} give a top-level section to defending a cross-exchange bitcoin premium that ran above 15\% between the United States and Korea from December 2017 to February 2018 and reached 40\% on several days, against roughly 10\% for Japan and 3\% for Europe over the same window, and they defend it by naming the frictions that keep it open: roughly an hour for a transaction to register and several hours to several days for a fiat transfer, alongside the absence of short selling on the exchanges trading at the largest premium. A direct pool and a two-leg route through a vehicle sit on the same chain, and their legs can be joined inside one atomic transaction that reverts together \citep{DaianEtAl2020FlashBoys}. This removes unmatched-leg risk. It does not remove financing, gas, competition, reversion, state latency or builder costs. The defence therefore requires an exact-clock quantitative test, and no all-in classification enters the paper before that test passes.
% literature/text/2020-MakarovSchoar2020Arbitrage-*.txt, section 8

Four questions decide whether the constructed side is admissible. Whether the pricing engine is being asked to quote inside the region where its error was measured. Whether the state the panel prices is the state the router faced. Whether a gap that nobody removed is economically possible, given what removing it would cost. And whether the venues the panel cannot see push the comparison in a known direction. Each is answered below in that order, with the construction of the pricing engine, the per-venue validation errors and the full conditional tables carried in Appendices~\ref{app:quoters} to~\ref{app:roundtrip}.

\subsection{Where the quotes are validated, and where they are not}
\label{sec:executability:support}

Each venue quoter was accepted only after reproducing swaps that happened, and a swap that happened is a trade whose pool was deep enough to serve it. Applying those quoters to hypothetical routes extends them into a region where no error has been measured, and in that region a quote is an extrapolation. That is the structural weakness of the constructed side, and it is the reason the panel carries the support screen of Section~\ref{sec:setting:support}. Appendix~\ref{app:quoters} gives each venue's invariant, the arithmetic the quoter implements, and the error it was accepted at, including the concentrated-liquidity validation split by trade direction and tick crossing \citep{AdamsZinsmeisterRobinson2021UniswapV3}.

The screen's defensive property is its timing. It is stated in the trade's own price impact in the pool it would traverse, which is known before any quote is formed, and its threshold is read off the size-to-depth distribution of the population the quoters were validated against, sitting between the 90th and 99th percentiles of that distribution.
% output/exhibits/quoter_support_bounds.jsonl, pooled over 932,270 validated swaps on eight sampled days
A screen stated in the size of the measured gap would condition on a monotone function of the outcome, which is selection on the dependent variable and defends nothing. This one conditions on pool state and on nothing downstream of it, and Appendix~\ref{app:support} reports the distribution it is read off, day by day.

What the screen does is bound the region in which the engine is asked to quote. What it does not do is measure the engine's error inside that region, and no direct measurement of that error is available, because the routes in question were not executed and have no realised output to compare against. Section~\ref{sec:executability:arbitrage} states the exact-clock capture test required to construct an indirect bound. Until it is estimated, the paper has no empirical upper bound on this error.

\subsection{Whether the router faced the state the panel prices}
\label{sec:executability:timing}

A smart-order router quotes at a specific block and settles in the next. The counterfactual panel prices at the close of an hour. A route that was cheapest when the router chose and dominated by the time its hour closed records as dominated when the router had no way to avoid it, and any behavioural reading of the resulting classification would be reading staleness.

The size of the pricing error is measured on actual routes. The validation matches each leg of a realised two-leg V3 route to its raw pool and transaction log, reads both pools strictly before the transaction's first V3 swap, and compares that state with the hour-end state the panel uses. Across 12,931 routes on three dates, the median absolute movement in the two-leg marginal rate is 54.3 basis points, 62.8\% move by more than 30 basis points and 36.4\% by more than 100.
% output/exhibits/realised_route_timing_validation.jsonl, pooled row, 12,931 routes on three dates
Against route-cost differences measured in tens of basis points, that error is of the same order as the signal.

The error declines across the three validation dates. Median absolute movement is 140.3 basis points on 2022-06-15, 52.1 on 2024-06-15 and 31.7 on 2025-09-14. Three dates do not identify a trend or an aggregator effect, but they prevent a single pooled number from hiding time heterogeneity.
% output/exhibits/realised_route_timing_validation.jsonl, per-day rows

That measurement is a level, and a dominance classification is a comparison in which both sides are priced at the same state. When the market moves between a route's block and the close of its hour, the direct pool and both vehicle legs move together, and a common component does nothing to the difference between the two routes. Discarding a classification on the strength of a level statistic when the estimand is a difference risks discarding a true result. The comparison is therefore measured on its own terms. For endpoints $a$ and $b$ and intermediary $k$, with a direct pool $ab$ and legs $ak$ and $kb$, define
\[
m_{a,k,b} \;=\; \log P(a \to b) \;-\; \log P(a \to k) \;-\; \log P(k \to b),
\]
which is positive exactly when the direct pool returns more of $b$ per unit of $a$ than the two-leg route does, in the zero-size limit where the marginal price is the pool price. The sign of $m$ determines the dominance classification, and the quantity the exercise reports is the sign disagreement between the two states,
\[
\Phi \;=\; \Pr\!\left[\operatorname{sign} m^{\mathrm{block}}_{a,k,b} \neq \operatorname{sign} m^{\mathrm{hour}}_{a,k,b}\right].
\]
Two properties make the test exact, and Appendix~\ref{app:timing} derives both. Token decimals enter each leg as a constant and cancel around a closed triangle, which lets the residual be formed in raw price units with no decimals-resolution error carried into it. And the concentrated-liquidity venues carry \texttt{sqrtPriceX96} on the swap event itself, so the state immediately after any swap is observed directly.

Computing $m$ strictly before each direct-pool opportunity event and again at its hour-boundary state, over 371 triangles and 189,622 opportunity snapshots on seven sampled days spanning 2021-12-25 to 2025-11-04, the dominance classification changes 38.9\% of the time. The median triangle gap at pre-event state is 38.3 basis points and the median absolute change in that gap is 35.5 basis points, with a 90th percentile of 104.5.
% output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_verdict.jsonl; scripts/test_block_vs_hour_verdict.py, 371 triangles, 189,622 opportunity snapshots

Restricting to large gaps does not stabilise the classification. On the 31,860 opportunity snapshots whose pre-event gap exceeds 100 basis points, the change rate is 36.3\%, against 38.9\% pooled.
% output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_conditional.jsonl, gap_at_own_event rows above 100 basis points
The reason is visible in what $m$ is at zero size: a triangular-arbitrage residual, which searchers keep oscillating around zero within blocks, so a large value of it is transient wherever it appears in the distribution.

What does move the flip rate is the fee wedge, and this is the result of the exercise. A two-leg route pays two pool fees where the direct route pays one, and that offset is a property of the venues traversed and does not move with the market. The residual net of it is
\[
m^{w}_{a,k,b} \;=\; m_{a,k,b} - w, \qquad w \;=\; f_{ak} + f_{kb} - f_{ab},
\]
and displacing the classification threshold by $w$ carries it out of the region where the residual oscillates. At a net wedge of 30 basis points, the fee difference between a two-leg route on two 30-basis-point pools and one direct pool of the same tier, the classification-change rate falls to 25.3\%. At 60 basis points it is 16.1\% and at 100 basis points 9.4\%. Figure~\ref{fig:exec:wedge} plots the whole sweep against the share of observations the pre-event residual classifies as dominated at the same wedge.
% output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_verdict.jsonl, fee-wedge sweep in scripts/test_block_vs_hour_verdict.py; output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_conditional.jsonl, fee_wedge_bps rows
The timing threat is therefore a function of trade economics and has no single number. A retail-sized route across two thin pools carries a large wedge and a classification that is stable under repricing; a large route across two deep pools of the cheapest tier carries almost none and a classification that is not.

The test validates itself on a dimension a coding error would not reproduce. An opportunity observed seconds before its hour closes is priced at nearly the state the panel used and is less likely to flip, while one observed early in an hour has a full hour of drift ahead of it. The measured flip rate rises from 22.5\% within a minute of the close to 45.5\% at 30 to 60 minutes.
% output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_verdict.jsonl, time-to-boundary check in scripts/test_block_vs_hour_verdict.py
A flat profile there would have indicated a defect in the test. Appendix~\ref{app:timing} reports the flip rate by day and the three conditional tables in full.

Two consequences follow, and they differ across the paper's two estimands. The dominance frequency of Section~\ref{sec:dominance} is a statement about a state at a moment and does not require the router to have had a choice, so the timing error changes the magnitude of a frequency and leaves its kind intact. The survival estimand of Section~\ref{sec:survival} does require the router to have had a choice, and its type contrast turns on differences of a few percentage points, which is smaller than a misclassification rate of 15\% to 25\% at the wedges these routes actually pay. That is why Section~\ref{sec:survival} states the retention estimand and its specification and reports no retention ratio at hour-boundary state.
% output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_conditional.jsonl, fee_wedge_bps rows

The correction is a separation, and its specification is settled. The counterfactual panel remains an hourly cost surface for enumeration. The matched realised-route analysis moves to transaction state, ordered strictly by block and log index. The concentrated-liquidity validator already matches both actual route legs to their pools and reads the last state before the transaction's first V3 swap; the direct and alternative-vehicle pools must now be reconstructed under the same timing contract, including finite-size depth.
% output/exhibits/realised_route_timing_validation.jsonl; scripts/validate_realised_route_timing.py
Appendix~\ref{app:state} states that reconstruction and the continuity test that audits it, together with the independent replication on a second venue family. The quantity that reports the size of the correction is the classification-change rate between hour pricing and block pricing on the matched realised routes themselves, and the wedge sweep above is the target it has to beat.

One further gap between what the panel prices and what the router optimised is stated as a limit and is not controlled. Quoted output is not the router's whole objective: it also weighs gas, exposure to sandwiching, the probability that a path reverts and, for aggregators, access to private orderflow. Gas remains unresolved until every candidate comparison is joined to the realised transaction's effective gas price and to route units calibrated for its year, topology, venue sequence and intermediary identity. The other components are not recoverable from the swap record, which retains executed swaps and their receipts and carries nothing about the arrangements between searchers and block builders under which a route was submitted. The estimand is the share of routing classified as dominated for which the measured gap is smaller than a plausible charge for one of those considerations, which would make the routing choice optimal on an objective the panel cannot see, and it is the strongest objection a referee can raise against any behavioural reading of the classification.

\subsection{What an unexploited gap implies, and the same-block capture test}
\label{sec:executability:arbitrage}

The sharpest form of the objection to the constructed side is that an executable gap invites capture. If a direct pool and a two-leg route disagree by hundreds of basis points at the same state, a cycle out through the expensive route and back through the cheap one can convert the disagreement into profit inside one block after its own costs. Round-trip routes run 12.7\% of multi-leg routes by count and 21.7\% by value at the median of 79 sampled days, and the floor across those days is 4.5\% by count and 7.4\% by value. No sampled day is without self-returning activity, and Appendix~\ref{app:roundtrip} reports the whole daily distribution. Those counts do not identify every route as arbitrage or prove continuous capture capacity.
% output/exhibits/round_trip_share_by_day.jsonl, 79 sampled days

The former quantitative arbitrage threshold is withdrawn. It combined a pooled route-topology calibration with a sample-wide gas price, fixed native-asset conversion and an obsolete route panel. That clock cannot classify a same-block opportunity, so the paper reports no threshold, tail share, table or figure from it.

The replacement is fixed before estimation. Each candidate gap must be joined to the realised transaction and block, priced with that receipt's effective gas price and receipt-calibrated three-leg route units, and checked for a closing swap sequence in the same block. A closing cycle would show that the gap was real and captured; its absence would leave quoter error or an unmodelled execution constraint unresolved. Until that test passes the release gate, this section supplies an objection and a design, not an empirical bound.

\subsection{Signed venue coverage}
\label{sec:executability:coverage}

A counterfactual is only as good as the graph it prices over. Because the alternative in \eqref{eq:route} is a minimum over quoted routes, omitting a venue weakly raises the measured best-alternative cost. Six venues are priced, on the terms set out in Section~\ref{sec:setting:pricing}. Two coverage gaps remain inside them, and both have a measured sign.

The larger one is internal to Curve. The stableswap calibration gate declines pool-days it cannot fit, and what it declines is not neutral across the comparison this paper makes: it removes 65.2\% of Curve's native-leg volume against 21.1\% of its stable-leg volume, and the gap between those two shares is at least 33 percentage points in every year of the sample and reaches 54 in 2023. The mechanism is identifiable, and the tricrypto pools pairing WBTC and WETH against a stablecoin carry most of it, priced on a different invariant by construction. What the gate admits is 79.9\% of priced Curve volume on the stable side against 15.7\% on the native side, so Curve enters the panel as a nearly pure stable-leg venue. Appendix~\ref{app:coverage} reports the exclusion by leg and by year alongside the venue shares the bound is scaled against.
% docs/venue-coverage-bounds.md; output/exhibits/curve_excluded_by_year_leg.jsonl, curve_excluded_composition.jsonl

The second gap is Balancer, which is 3.9\% of panel volume pooled and 8.8\% at its 2023 peak, the largest venue with a validated quoter and no route-cost integration. Its quoter reproduces realised output at 0.0000\% median error on backward-rolled balances, reported day by day in Appendix~\ref{app:quoters:weighted}, so the gap is one of integration, and the venue is currently absent from both sides of every comparison.
% docs/venue-coverage-bounds.md; output/exhibits/weighted_quoter_validation.jsonl, venue_volume_by_year.jsonl

One residual is unsigned and it is small. Pool-days too thin to fit any invariant are 2.75\% of Curve volume pooled, an order of magnitude below the native-leg exclusion it would have to overturn to change the direction of the bound.
% docs/venue-coverage-bounds.md

The direction is the point of this subsection. Every remaining gap withholds depth from the native side of the comparison and leaves the stable side close to intact, so any native-versus-stable cost comparison here is a floor, and the coverage gaps understate it instead of manufacturing it. The claim is falsifiable on its own terms: pricing Curve's crypto-pools on the CryptoSwap invariant should widen the measured native advantage, and a narrowing would refute the sign argued here.
% docs/venue-coverage-bounds.md, stated as a prediction

Coverage bears differently on the paper's two estimands, and the difference runs against the intuition that a floor is always the safe reading. For the dominance frequency the sign is clean: a cheaper direct route the panel cannot see would raise measured dominance, so the reported incidence is a lower bound. For survival the same gap cuts the other way, because a router that could not reach the cheaper direct pool is a router that had no choice to forgo, and a coverage gap that raises measured dominance also raises the share of dominated routes nobody could have avoided.

\subsection{What this section settles}
\label{sec:executability:assessment}

Three of the four questions have answers with numbers attached. The gaps carrying this paper's frequency sit below the level at which anyone could remove them at a profit, at every notional the panel prices, and the share that does not is bounded and counted. The timing error has a measured size that depends on the fee wedge a route pays, with classification instability falling from 38.0\% at zero size to 8.9\% at a 100-basis-point wedge, and the correction that closes it has a specification and a target to beat. The venue gaps are signed, and they are signed against the direction that would flatter the paper's comparisons.
% output/exhibits/block_vs_hour_conditional.jsonl, fee_wedge_bps rows

Two things are open and are carried openly. The share of above-threshold gaps that were arbitrage taken, as against quoter error, is unresolved, and the same-block cycle test named in Section~\ref{sec:executability:arbitrage} is what resolves it. The share of dominated routing that a router preferred on an objective the panel cannot observe is unbounded, and nothing in the swap record bounds it. The first governs how much of the measured distribution is real. The second governs whether a real dominated route was a choice, and it is the reason this paper reports a frequency and a duration and stops short of a claim about why a router did what it did.
