\section{Interpreting the rotation}
\label{sec:rivals}

The results point to a reallocation across endpoint markets; repeated stable-for-native substitution within a typical market contributes little. Several economic forces could produce that pattern. Liquidity may move slowly toward the pools used by an incumbent; routers may differ in the exchanges and paths they can reach; changes in backing or redemption risk may alter the appeal of an intermediary; and software or protocol architecture may direct trading toward particular routes. These forces can reinforce one another, so their implications must be read against the variation observed in the route data.

\subsection{Liquidity and router reach}
\label{sec:rivals:capital}

The classical vehicle-currency mechanism links trading volume to lower transaction cost \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies}. In a decentralised exchange, that feedback operates through the pools joining an intermediary to other assets. Trading through those pools can attract provider capital; deeper pools can then make the same intermediary cheaper to use. If providers move capital slowly, an established vehicle may retain deep routes after another intermediary becomes competitive.

The direction of timing is central to this explanation. Capital moving before vehicle use would support liquidity supply as a source of the change, whereas capital following route use would suggest that trading draws liquidity to the pools it already uses. The two quantities also differ. Provider capital is the amount invested in the relevant pools; executable liquidity is the amount those pools can trade at a given order size and price impact. Concentrated-liquidity providers can change the latter by moving positions within a pool without changing total invested capital.

The daily panel points in both directions. For WETH, WBTC, USDC, USDT, and DAI, a larger share of liquidity in vehicle-linked pools predicts a larger vehicle share 1, 7, 14, and 30 days later. Vehicle use also predicts the subsequent concentration of liquidity at every horizon. Its relation with the total amount supplied is weaker at short horizons but becomes positive at 14 and 30 days. These regressions absorb asset and date effects and condition on the lagged outcome. They establish temporal dependence, not a causal feedback loop: demand, volatility, or routing changes may move both series. Even with that qualification, the results are inconsistent with a purely one-way account in which liquidity placement evolves independently of vehicle use.

Router reach supplies a related explanation because a cheaper path affects route choice only when the trading system can use the exchange that offers it. Cross-exchange routing becomes much more common during the sample, while stablecoin use rises among both single-exchange and cross-exchange routes. The rotation therefore occurs within each broad route type as well as through changes in their relative importance. Because exchange span is chosen jointly with the route, estimating the effect of broader access requires comparisons within the same market and feasible route set. The present result establishes that the stablecoin gain extends beyond the cross-exchange portion of the sample \citep{ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation}.

\subsection{Asset risk and software architecture}
\label{sec:rivals:holding}

USDT's rise relative to USDC makes differences within the stablecoin category economically relevant. The two assets differ in issuance, redemption access, the distribution of liquidity across exchanges, and the risks borne by holders and liquidity providers. Inventory models imply that a deterioration in an intermediary can raise the cost of supporting routes through it \citep{HoStoll1981OptimalDealer,ComertonFordeEtAl2010Inventories}. A native-asset drawdown exposes providers to volatile inventory, while a stablecoin depeg changes the perceived safety of an asset valued for stability. The current route shares identify which stablecoin gains the vehicle role, but they do not isolate which of these attributes is responsible.

Software can preserve the same market allocation for a different reason. Routing systems search only the pools and exchanges they integrate, and pool-creation templates can make one intermediary the default path for new markets. The move from mandatory native-asset routing in the first protocol generation to arbitrary token pairs in the next shows that architecture can remove a routing requirement. Continued native-asset use after that change is compatible with both inherited software paths and the thick-market feedback described above. Protocol launches coincide with changes in users, liquidity, and available pairs, so their dates describe the market's chronology but do not identify an architecture effect.

Uniswap V4 changes the settlement of a route without necessarily changing its economic sequence. Its singleton can net token movements across the calls made within one transaction. We matched 500 V3 routes and 500 V4 routes by week, ordered endpoint pair, and intermediary token, then read the transaction receipts. Every matched V3 route records a transfer of the intermediary token, compared with 81.4\% of the V4 routes. The missing transfers are most common among smaller routes, and the V4 observations are smaller on average even within the matched groups. The comparison therefore does not estimate V4's effect on vehicle choice. It establishes the narrower mechanism: settlement netting can leave the economic vehicle route intact while removing one of its physical token movements.

The route evidence narrows these explanations without ranking them. A changing balance between single-exchange and cross-exchange routes is insufficient, and stablecoin adoption as an endpoint cannot explain USDT's increased intermediary use relative to its endpoint use. The lead--lag results make liquidity allocation empirically relevant, while the receipt comparison shows that architecture can change settlement without eliminating the vehicle role. Liquidity, router reach, asset risk, and software can nevertheless move together within the same endpoint market. Separating their effects requires comparisons of the same ordered pair and trade size when the router can reach the same alternatives, together with capital and liquidity measured independently of realised route choice.
