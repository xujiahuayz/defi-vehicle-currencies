\subsection{Routes and notation}
\label{sec:setting:route}

An automated market maker replaces a limit order book with a pool of tokens supplied by liquidity providers. A smart contract maps the pool's current state and the trader's input into an output, charging a fee according to the protocol's rule. Several pools may trade the same pair on different protocols or at different fee tiers, and pools that share a token can form an indirect path. A trader can exchange one asset for another in a single pool, split the order across direct pools, or pass through an intermediary when the indirect path offers better execution. \citet{LeharParlour2024Uniswap} describe the constant-product form and the economics of liquidity supply in this market.

Routers make these alternatives usable in one transaction. For an exchange from asset $a$ to asset $b$, a router can call a direct pool, divide the order among direct pools, or execute $a\rightarrow k\rightarrow b$ through an intermediary $k$. The calls settle atomically: all legs succeed or the transaction reverts. Atomic settlement removes unmatched-leg risk, but fees, price impact, gas, available pools, and the state seen by the router still vary across paths.

Blockchain records provide pool-level calls. Recovering an economic route requires joining them by directed token flow because one transaction can contain unrelated conversions and one conversion can contain parallel legs or a sequential path spanning several venues. A leg joins another when its output supplies the next leg's input, and each connected source-to-destination component forms one route. Parallel calls from the same source to the same destination form a direct split. A sequential route instead receives and pays out an internal token before reaching the destination.

One transaction on January 10, 2026 illustrates the difference between a transaction instruction and its component trades. A public explorer summarizes the instruction as a 460,000 PYUSD--USDe swap executed through 0x.\footnote{Transaction \texttt{0xbda1d07f17c503b5a9c8117c5d1383472006af8a9e22aa4e5e30804b9bf67ad9}.} Within that transaction, PYUSD is transferred to the market maker and USDC enters a connected sequence of pool swaps. The sequence sends 459,929.81 USDC to a Fluid pool, receives 460,331.05 USDT, and exchanges the USDT through Uniswap v4 for 460,104.28 USDe. Thus, the transaction begins with PYUSD, while the component observed in the pool events exchanges USDC for USDe through USDT. Our route measure treats USDC and USDe as the endpoints of this component and USDT as its vehicle. We apply the same flow decomposition to every transaction. The notation below formalizes this observed component; evaluating the availability and cost of a direct USDC--USDe route additionally requires pre-transaction market state.
% output/exhibits/route_replay.json; data/unified/20260110.parquet; Blockscout transaction page retrieved 2026-08-14

Write $t$ for a state index, $\mathcal{A}_{t}$ for the assets tradable at $t$, $\mathcal{P}_{t}$ for the pools live at $t$, and $\mathcal{V}$ for the priced venues, with $v(p) \in \mathcal{V}$ the venue of pool $p$ and $S_{p,t}$ the reconstructed state of $p$ at $t$. A route $r$ carries an ordered pool sequence $(p_{1},\ldots,p_{L})$ of length $L(r)$, an endpoint pair $(a,b)$ with $a$ paid in and $b$ received, an intermediary set $\mathcal{M}(r) = \{k_{1},\ldots,k_{L-1}\}$, and a size $s$ measured in dollars at the input token's price. Let $q_{p}(a \to b,\, x;\, S_{p,t})$ denote the venue's own exact-input quote, the units of $b$ paid out for $x$ units of $a$ at pool $p$ in state $S_{p,t}$. The quoted output of the route composes its legs,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:route}
Q_{r}(s,t) \;=\; q_{p_{L}}\!\left(k_{L-1} \to b,\; \cdots\, q_{p_{1}}\!\left(a \to k_{1},\, s;\, S_{p_{1},t}\right) \cdots;\; S_{p_{L},t}\right),
\end{equation}
and Section~\ref{sec:setting:pricing} gives the four functional forms $q_{p}$ takes across the priced venues. This notation describes both the route that executed and the hypothetical routes introduced below. Only the executed route is observed without reconstructing market state.

The observed route in \eqref{eq:route} maps the classical volume--cost mechanism into an automated market. Using a vehicle expands its two bilateral markets, lowers their costs, and can validate the original choice \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies}. \citet{KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium} make acceptability an equilibrium object: an agent accepts a good for indirect exchange when its marketability, generated by others' trading strategies and inventories, compensates for its storage cost. \citet{DowdGreenaway1993CurrencyCompetition} introduce fixed user-side costs of learning, reckoning, requoting prices and accounting in a new currency. On-chain route selection can likewise reflect persistent software defaults, venue access, and liquidity placement. Automated execution removes human dealer relationships but leaves both volume--cost feedback and software-mediated coordination available.

The dominant-currency literature studies complementary roles in invoicing \citep{GopinathEtAl2020DCP,AmitiItskhokiKonings2022Dominant,Mukhin2022InternationalPriceSystem}, funding \citep{GopinathStein2021Making,ErenMalamud2022DominantDebt}, and dealer trading volumes \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics} measure a currency's international role by the number of markets quoting it and estimate a feedback between use and cost in a historical trading network. Their degree statistic describes network position; the observed routes in \eqref{eq:route} reveal realised intermediary use. A route's settlement block is observed, but the state available when the routing decision was made is not and may precede settlement. Section~\ref{sec:executability} assesses how settlement-time and latent quote-time states differ in counterfactual comparisons.

\subsection{Measuring vehicle dominance}
\label{sec:setting:definitions}

\begin{definition}[Vehicle status]
\label{def:vehicle}
Asset $k$ has \emph{vehicle status} on route $r$ when it is an observed intermediary and is not an endpoint,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vehicle-status}
Z_{r,k} \;=\; \mathbf{1}\{k \in \mathcal{M}(r)\}.
\end{equation}
Vehicle status is therefore binary at the route level.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Vehicle dominance]
\label{def:role}
The \emph{vehicle dominance} of asset $k$ on date $t$ is its continuous share of realised intermediary use. We report a count share and a value share,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dominance}
S^{N}_{k,t} \;=\;
\frac{\sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}^{(2)}_{t}} Z_{r,k}}
     {|\mathcal{R}^{(2)}_{t}|},
\qquad
S^{V}_{k,t} \;=\;
\frac{\sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}^{(2),V}_{t}} \nu_{r} Z_{r,k}}
     {\sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}^{(2),V}_{t}} \nu_{r}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}^{(2)}_{t}$ contains exact two-leg routes, each with one observed intermediary, $\mathcal{R}^{(2),V}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{(2)}_{t}$ is the subset with value support, and $\nu_{r}$ is the supported dollar value of route $r$. Count dominance measures how frequently $k$ carries the intermediary role; value dominance measures the economic value routed through it. Multi-leg routes with several intermediary positions enter the broader episode diagnostics, not this one-vehicle dominance measure.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Graph position]
\label{def:graph-position}
Graph position describes the paths available through an asset; vehicle dominance describes the realised routes that use it. Betweenness centrality is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:role}
\beta_{k,t} \;=\; \sum_{a \neq k \neq b} \frac{\sigma_{ab,t}(k)}{\sigma_{ab,t}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{ab,t}(k)$ of the $\sigma_{ab,t}$ shortest paths joining $a$ and $b$ pass through $k$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Round trip]
\label{def:roundtrip}
A route is a \emph{round trip} when its first input token equals its last output token, $a = b$, so that the route returns the trader to the asset it started from.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Asset types]
\label{def:types}
The type map $\tau$ labels each asset by what it is a claim on. The five types are native, staked native, stable, imported, and other.
\end{definition}

Vehicle status records whether asset $k$ connects the endpoints of an observed route, and vehicle dominance aggregates those observations. Count dominance gives each route one vote; value dominance weights routes by dollars when the three legs can be valued consistently. An asset can appear frequently as an intermediary simply because traders also buy and sell it frequently. We separate these roles by comparing its share of intermediary positions with its share of route endpoints among the same transactions. Their difference is the share gap, and their ratio is excess use when endpoint share is positive. A positive gap means that the asset appears between other assets more often than its own trading activity would predict.

Let $n^{I}_{k,t}$ and $n^{E}_{k,t}$ denote asset $k$'s intermediary and endpoint counts among the same transactions. The two role shares are
\begin{equation}
I^{N}_{k,t}=\frac{n^{I}_{k,t}}{\sum_j n^{I}_{j,t}},
\qquad
E^{N}_{k,t}=\frac{n^{E}_{k,t}}{\sum_j n^{E}_{j,t}},
\label{eq:role-shares}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding share gap and excess-use ratio are
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{N}_{k,t}=I^{N}_{k,t}-E^{N}_{k,t},
\qquad
X^{N}_{k,t}=\frac{I^{N}_{k,t}}{E^{N}_{k,t}}\quad\text{when }E^{N}_{k,t}>0.
\label{eq:excess-use}
\end{equation}
The value-weighted measures replace counts with supported dollar values and retain the same denominator on each side of the comparison.

These realised-use measures differ from graph position and hypothetical cost. Betweenness describes $k$'s position in the trading graph and is the on-chain analogue of the quote-count statistic used by \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}; the cost gap in Section~\ref{sec:setting:pricing} compares hypothetical route outputs at a reconstructed state. Graph position and cost can diverge from realised use: a central or low-cost asset may carry few routes, while a heavily used vehicle may lie on few shortest paths or offer a more expensive route. Graph position and cost characterize the opportunity set; dominance measures realised intermediary use.

We exclude round trips because their starting and ending assets coincide. The endpoint rule classifies path geometry; trader intent remains unobserved. This population can contain cyclic arbitrage and other self-returning activity. \citet{DaianEtAl2020FlashBoys} establish that multi-trade arbitrage can be bundled atomically, while an exhaustive behavioural classification of closed paths would require additional evidence. Round trips run 12.7\% of multi-leg routes by count and 21.7\% by value on the median of 79 sampled days. On 2025-12-06, the most extreme day observed, they run 25.9\% and 91.3\%.
% output/exhibits/round_trip_share_by_day.jsonl, via scripts/measure_round_trip_share.py

The \emph{native} label of Definition~\ref{def:types} goes to the platform's settlement asset, independent of how many pools quote it, how much volume it intermediates, or how cheaply a route through it executes. Native ETH and wrapped ETH are treated as one currency for this purpose. WETH is redeemable one-for-one for ETH, and a router can perform the wrapping step mechanically; treating the zero-address form used by newer protocols and the WETH contract as separate vehicles would turn a representation change into a currency choice. Liquid-staking claims remain a separate type because they carry a claim on staking returns. The \emph{stable} label goes to tokens targeting a fixed fiat value, \emph{imported} covers wrapped claims on off-chain or cross-chain value, and \emph{other} is the residual.

Pairing degree, centrality, depth, and route cost are measured properties of these types, not ingredients in the taxonomy. The stable class spans heterogeneous designs. Reserve-backed stablecoins target par through backing and redemption, making confidence in those arrangements relevant to acceptance and run exposure \citep{GortonZhang2023Wildcat}. Peg-restoration mechanisms and solvency differ across fiat-backed, crypto-collateralized, and algorithmic designs \citep{CataliniDeGortariShah2022Stablecoins,LyonsViswanathNatraj2023Stablecoins}. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} therefore examines the major stablecoins and backing groups separately.

\subsection{The route panel}
\label{sec:setting:data}

The route panel covers swaps on eight Ethereum AMM deployments: Curve, Uniswap v2, v3 and v4, Balancer, SushiSwap v2 and v3, and Fluid. It spans 2,332 calendar dates from 2020-02-11 through 2026-06-30. The sample begins with 472,254,909 pool-level swap records and yields 471,616,269 directed legs after harmonising token direction, event identity, and units. The panel covers several major venues but not the universe of Ethereum trading. Each protocol enters after launch, so both the set of active venues and the routes available to traders change over the sample. Fifty-five early dates contain no swaps on the covered deployments. These dates represent no observed activity: an inactive or pre-launch venue offers no route, whereas a missing source-day would leave route use unknown.
% output/exhibits/unified_route_quality.jsonl; docs/research-workflow.md section 2

We assemble pool-level events from the eight exchanges and recover the complete exchange executed within each transaction. This requires protocol-specific token identification, unit conversion, and event ordering because a transaction may contain unrelated calls, parallel order splits, and sequential trades across exchanges. We join the ordered pool events and follow the directed token flows from the asset sold to the asset received. This identifies single-pool trades, parallel splits, and multi-leg routes; disconnected event groups are not classified as routes. Appendix~\ref{app:route-construction} describes the reconstruction and quantifies the small set of token units that cannot be verified historically.

A route remains in the count measure whenever its token sequence is observed. It enters the value-weighted measure only when the amounts at the source, intermediary, and destination can be valued consistently, so a noisy dollar value does not erase an otherwise observed intermediary choice. Keeping route incidence separate from dollar weighting also preserves the distinction between venue-level depth and aggregate allocation when orders split across exchanges \citep{ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation}. The panel follows the conversion executed across pools, although the public record generally does not reveal the software or trader that selected it.

\subsection{Pricing the counterfactual}
\label{sec:setting:pricing}

We construct two counterfactual benchmarks at a common reconstructed settlement state and trade size. The direct benchmark is the best single-hop execution between the observed endpoints. The vehicle benchmark is the best two-hop execution through intermediary $k$, allowing each leg to use its best priced venue,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:direct}
Q^{\ast}_{ab}(s,t) \;=\; \max_{p \,\in\, \mathcal{P}_{ab,t}} \; q_{p}\!\left(a \to b,\, s;\, S_{p,t}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vehicle}
Q^{k}_{ab}(s,t) \;=\; \max_{p' \,\in\, \mathcal{P}_{kb,t}} \; q_{p'}\!\left(k \to b,\; \max_{p \,\in\, \mathcal{P}_{ak,t}} q_{p}\!\left(a \to k,\, s;\, S_{p,t}\right);\; S_{p',t}\right).
\end{equation}
The vehicle-route shortfall relative to direct execution is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap}
G^{k}_{ab}(s,t) \;=\; \frac{Q^{\ast}_{ab}(s,t) - Q^{k}_{ab}(s,t)}{Q^{\ast}_{ab}(s,t)},
\end{equation}
reported in basis points as $10^{4}G^{k}_{ab}(s,t)$.

\begin{definition}[Cost-dominated route]
\label{def:dominated}
A route with endpoints $(a,b)$, intermediary $k$ and size $s$ is \emph{cost dominated} at reconstructed state $t$ when $G^{k}_{ab}(s,t)>0$: the direct benchmark returns more output than the vehicle benchmark before gas.
\end{definition}

Let $\mathcal G^{D}_{ab}(s,t)$ and $\mathcal G^{I}_{ab,k}(s,t)$ denote the direct and indirect routes' dollar gas expenditures evaluated at the realised transaction's settlement block. Gross and all-in cost-dominance indicators are
\begin{align}
\label{eq:dom}
D^{\mathrm{g}}_{ab,k}(s,t) &\;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left\{ G^{k}_{ab}(s,t) > 0 \right\}, \\
\label{eq:domallin}
D^{\mathrm{a}}_{ab,k}(s,t) &\;=\; \mathbf{1}\!\left\{ G^{k}_{ab}(s,t) > -\gamma(s,t) \right\}, \qquad
\gamma(s,t) \;=\; \frac{\mathcal G^{I}_{ab,k}(s,t)-\mathcal G^{D}_{ab}(s,t)}{Q^{\ast}_{ab}(s,t)}.
\end{align}
Each $\mathcal G$ combines gas units calibrated by year, topology, venue sequence and intermediary identity with the transaction's effective gas price and the native asset's dollar price at settlement. The maximisations in \eqref{eq:direct} and \eqref{eq:vehicle} cover the priced venue set. These indicators measure costs at settlement. An interpretation about route choice additionally requires the router's quote time, venue access and any costs outside the observed perimeter. Section~\ref{sec:executability} evaluates those conditions before using the counterfactual to interpret route choice.

Pool design determines how much liquidity is available to a route of a given size. Constant-product pools spread liquidity over the price curve, so price impact rises smoothly as a trade consumes reserves. Concentrated-liquidity pools place capital inside chosen price ranges; a trade that crosses a range boundary encounters a different amount of available depth. StableSwap flattens the curve near parity and is used disproportionately for stable assets, while weighted pools permit asymmetric and multi-asset portfolios. These differences make a common marginal price insufficient. The benchmark must quote the full trade through each pool's own state transition.

The six priced deployments span four pool families. For each family, we reconstruct the pool state immediately before an executed swap, apply the pool's own pricing rule to the observed input, and compare the resulting quote with the amount actually received. The validation applies to the directions, trade sizes, and states represented by executed swaps. Accuracy on unexecuted paths or under other pool rules requires separate evidence. Table~\ref{tab:venues} reports the resulting errors and coverage; Appendix~\ref{app:quoters} gives the pricing equations, parameter recovery, event ordering, and validation samples.

The counterfactual uses the same input size for the direct and vehicle paths and searches only the declared priced venue set. Its gross gap measures output foregone at that reconstructed state. Gas adds a path-specific cost that is most important for small trades and routes activating more pools. Both quantities belong to the stated benchmark; neither reveals the route set available to a particular router or the state at which it formed its decision. SushiSwap v3 is observed in the route panel but is not priced because historical tick state is unavailable. Its 0.02\% pooled venue share makes the aggregate omission small, although the effect can still be material for a pair served nowhere else.

\label{sec:setting:support}

Each pricing routine is validated on realised swaps, which necessarily lie within the pool's executable depth. A hypothetical route can extend the same routine beyond the region in which its error has been measured. We restrict hypothetical legs to price impacts represented among realised trades. Smart-contract proxy execution can bundle trades across exchanges into one all-or-nothing transaction and revert the batch when one leg fails \citep{DaianEtAl2020FlashBoys}. Atomic execution removes unmatched-leg risk, while financing, gas, competition, state latency, and builder frictions remain. Coarser-time cost gaps consequently describe reconstructed price differences rather than executable arbitrage.

The restriction is applied before comparing routes. Filtering on the measured cost gap would condition on a monotone function of the outcome in \eqref{eq:gap} and would leave extrapolation unresolved. We instead retain each leg according to its own price impact relative to its own marginal price, with the threshold calibrated to realised trades,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:support}
\pi_{\ell}(x,t) \;=\; \frac{\tilde{q}_{\ell}(x,t) - q_{\ell}(x,t)}{\tilde{q}_{\ell}(x,t)} \;\leq\; \bar{\pi},
\qquad
\frac{x}{R_{a,p,t}} \;\leq\; \bar{\pi},
\qquad \bar{\pi} = 0.05,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{q}_{\ell}$ prices leg $\ell$ at the pool's marginal rate and the second form is the constant-product special case. Eligibility therefore depends on the state and size of the hypothetical leg before the outputs of competing routes are compared. Pooled over 932,270 validated swaps across eight sampled days, the median trade moves 0.34\% of the pool's input-side reserve, the 90th percentile 3.3\%, and the 99th 14.9\%, which places $\bar{\pi}$ between the 95th and the 99th percentile of realised behaviour.
% output/exhibits/quoter_support_bounds.jsonl

The restriction is applied across every venue-specific quoter. A reserve ratio alone is insufficient because most counterfactual quotes come from concentrated-liquidity pools, where executable depth depends on the active tick map. The price-impact condition in \eqref{eq:support} supplies one comparable support criterion across pool families.

\label{sec:setting:validation}

Counterfactual costs are distinct from vehicle dominance. Equations~\eqref{eq:dominance} measure observed intermediary use; Equation~\eqref{eq:gap} compares hypothetical outputs at a reconstructed state. A cost gap does not identify the route available when the decision was made unless quote-time state and venue access are also established. For that reason, Section~\ref{sec:executability} states the additional conditions required before the comparison can be used to interpret route choice. Reported magnitudes use the continuous gap $G^{k}_{ab}$; a coefficient on the threshold indicator $D^{\mathrm{g}}$ has no basis-point interpretation without the density at the threshold.
