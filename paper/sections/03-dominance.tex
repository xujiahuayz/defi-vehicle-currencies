\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Stablecoins carry a much larger share of vehicle activity in 2026 than in 2024. Comparing the same days of the calendar year, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count. The paired-day change is \StableCountChange{}, with a standard error of \StableCountSE{}. Their dollar-weighted share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, an increase of \StableValueChange{} with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. The count series gives equal weight to each exact two-leg route. The dollar-weighted series uses routes for which the values at the source, intermediary, and destination agree within 20\%. Table~\ref{tab:rotation} reports both. Each figure is an average over the markets traded in its year, and those markets differ between the two years. Section~\ref{sec:dominance:pairs} shows that the difference is what moves the aggregate: within a source--destination pair traded in both years, the intermediary carrying the route barely changes.

\subsection{Composition of source--destination pairs}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

A vehicle's aggregate share can rise in three ways. It can be chosen more often within a source--destination pair traded in both years; routed activity can move toward continuing pairs that already use it; or pairs traded in only one of the two years can route through it. Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} shows that the first way accounts for little of the stablecoin increase and that the other two carry it.

Table~\ref{tab:rotation} averages daily shares, whereas Panels A and B of Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} pool route count or value across the matched January--June windows. The different weighting explains why the headline changes of \StableCountChange{} and \StableValueChange{} differ slightly from the decomposition totals of \MarketBridgeTotal{} and \PairValueTotal{}.

By route count, pairs observed in only one comparison year contribute \MarketSupportBridge{} of the increase, and shifts in activity across continuing pairs contribute \MarketActivityReweight{}. Changes in how often those pairs use any intermediary add \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}, while the appearance or disappearance of the vehicle role adds \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}. Direct stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use a vehicle in both years contributes only \WithinPairStableShare{}. The dollar-weighted decomposition is sharper still: of the \PairValueTotal{} increase, \PairValueReweight{} comes from activity shifting across continuing pairs and \PairValueExclusive{} from pairs that use a native or stablecoin intermediary in only one comparison year. Within-pair substitution contributes \PairValueWithin{}.

Conditional on the same ordered source--destination pair, calendar date, and single- or cross-exchange route type, stablecoin share changes by \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketCountSE{}, and by \MatchedMarketValueChange{} by value, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketValueSE{}. The corresponding 95\% confidence intervals rule out within-pair changes larger than \MatchedMarketCountCIUpper{} by count and \MatchedMarketValueCIUpper{} by value. The matched-pair estimates are therefore small relative to the aggregate rotation.

Individual pairs make the accounting concrete. Among pairs whose two assets carry a recognised ticker, the largest contribution to the reweighting term comes from \MarginReweightPair{}, which is stablecoin-intermediated in both years and grows from \MarginReweightRoutesBase{} to \MarginReweightRoutesEnd{} routes, or from \MarginReweightWeightBase{} to \MarginReweightWeightEnd{} of routed activity, and adds \MarginReweightContribution{} on its own. The largest such contribution among pairs absent from 2024 comes from \MarginNewPairPair{}, which supplies \MarginNewPairRoutesEnd{} stablecoin-intermediated routes in 2026 and adds \MarginNewPairContribution{}. The largest such contribution within a continuing pair, from \MarginWithinPair{}, adds \MarginWithinContribution{} as its stablecoin share moves from \MarginWithinStableBase{} to \MarginWithinStableEnd{}; the within-pair term summed over all pairs is \MarginWithinTotal{}. Because most newly traded assets have no such ticker, each named pair understates its margin, which is why the margin totals accompany it. New listings or changes in pool access can bring a pair into the sample; trading activity can shift across continuing pairs; and direct-market depth or router access can change the use of indirect execution. Through these margins, routed activity reallocates across source--destination pairs. This reallocation carries most of the stablecoin increase. Stable-for-native substitution within continuing vehicle-using pairs contributes far less.

A margin of a given size can come from one corridor or from thousands of small markets, and the totals do not distinguish the two. It is therefore natural to ask whether a few large markets carry the reallocation. The two composition margins answer differently. Pairs traded only in 2026 contribute through \MarginNewPairGainPairs{} ordered pairs, the largest of which supplies \MarginNewPairTopShare{} of that margin, and \MarginNewPairNinetyPairs{} of them are needed for nine-tenths of it. Reweighting among continuing pairs is top-heavy instead: \MarginReweightHalfPairs{} pairs supply half of its \MarginReweightGrossUp{} gross gain, and the margin nets to \PairPooledReweight{} only because \MarginReweightLossPairs{} continuing pairs lose activity weight, \MarginReweightGrossDown{} between them. Dollar weighting sharpens the same contrast, with \MarginReweightValueHalfPairs{} pairs behind half of a \MarginReweightValueGrossUp{} gross gain. Within continuing pairs, intermediary choice moves in both directions: \MarginWithinGainPairs{} of them raise their stablecoin share and \MarginWithinLossPairs{} lower it, \MarginWithinGrossUp{} against \MarginWithinGrossDown{} by count and \MarginWithinValueGrossUp{} against \MarginWithinValueGrossDown{} by value. The near-zero net term is an offset between markets moving in both directions, not an absence of movement, and it stays small beside the weight that moves across pairs. The rotation therefore travels along two different edges of the trading network: the network extends into many new and mostly small markets, while activity weight concentrates in a few corridors that were already large. This reorganisation of where trading happens carries the aggregate change; the growth of a single corridor does not. The two edges also separate what a currency network measures: a matrix recording which pairs trade at all, as in the historical exchange structure of \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}, registers the first margin and is silent on the second.

The vehicle role is not contestable everywhere in the pair space. A route whose source or destination is wrapped ether cannot also use wrapped ether as its intermediary, so within the native-versus-stablecoin comparison such a route is stablecoin-intermediated by definition, in 2024 and in 2026 alike. Since the pairs that grew fastest are concentrated among exactly those corridors, a question of interest is whether the two composition margins record anything more than the growth of markets whose vehicle was never in doubt. We split each margin according to whether wrapped ether is an endpoint of the ordered pair. The split is decided by one contract address rather than by a ticker, so the long tail of newly traded assets is classified as reliably as the majors. \LockedPairs{} of the \LockedCommonPairs{} continuing pairs, or \LockedPairShare{}, have such an endpoint, and their share of routed activity rises from \LockedWeightBase{} to \LockedWeightEnd{}. By route count they supply \LockedReweight{} of the \PairPooledReweight{} reweighting margin and \LockedNewPair{} of the \MarginNewPairTotal{} new-pair margin, so most of the count rotation does run through corridors that had no intermediary to choose. Dollar weighting reverses the reading. The same corridors supply \LockedValueReweight{} of the \PairValueReweight{} value reweighting margin, or \LockedValueReweightShare{} of it, and \LockedValueNewPair{} of the value new-pair margin, or \LockedValueNewPairShare{}. Once trades are weighted by the value they move, the rotation is carried by pairs that could have been routed either way and were routed through a stablecoin. The same identity disciplines the within-pair term: a pair with a wrapped-ether endpoint contributes exactly zero to it under both weightings, so the near-zero total is not an average diluted by markets that were frozen at a stablecoin share of one. Every unit of it comes from pairs whose intermediary was a live choice, and among those pairs the change is still near zero.

So far the split has been taken over all routes at once, which does not say where in the venue structure the corridors without a vehicle choice sit. Taking it separately for routes that stay on one exchange and routes that cross exchanges answers that, and the two weightings answer it differently. Within a single exchange, reweighting among continuing pairs contributes \PairSingleReweight{}, of which wrapped-ether-endpoint corridors supply \LockedSingleReweightShare{}. Across exchanges the same margin is \PairCrossReweight{}, and those corridors supply \LockedCrossReweightShare{} of it. What survives the split is the part contributed by pairs that had a choice, and it is the same size in both: \OpenSingleReweight{} within an exchange and \OpenCrossReweight{} across exchanges. The entire cross-exchange excess in the count margin is therefore corridors that were stablecoin-intermediated by definition, and the corridors that could have gone either way reallocate activity at the same rate wherever the route runs. Weighting by the value moved removes the contrast. The margin is \PairValueSingleReweight{} within an exchange against \PairValueCrossReweight{} across exchanges, and eligible corridors supply \LockedValueSingleReweightShare{} and \LockedValueCrossReweightShare{} of them. In route counts, then, the rotation looks like something that happened to cross-exchange trading, and what it records there is which pairs became active across exchanges; in value terms it is no more a cross-exchange event than a single-exchange one. \citet{MakarovSchoar2020Arbitrage} decompose bitcoin price dispersion the same way and find the across-region component both the larger one and, once traded volume is taken into account, the one that carries the economic magnitude. The across-venue excess here does the opposite: it is visible in unweighted route counts and gone from the dollar-weighted margin.

That split was taken of continuing markets, whose endpoints are fixed and whose intermediary can change. The larger composition margin comes from pairs that traded only in 2026, and it is there that a reader should most expect eligibility to be the whole story: a market appearing for the first time is disproportionately a newly listed asset quoted against wrapped ether, which is to say a corridor that arrives with its intermediary already settled. By route count that expectation is met, and the route scope barely moves it. Entry contributes \PairSingleNewPair{} within a single exchange and \PairCrossNewPair{} across exchanges, and wrapped-ether-endpoint corridors supply \LockedSingleNewPairShare{} of the first and \LockedCrossNewPairShare{} of the second. Dollar weighting separates the two scopes instead of reversing them. The entry margin is \PairValueSingleNewPair{} within an exchange against \PairValueCrossNewPair{} across exchanges, twice the size, while the part from corridors that had no vehicle to choose is \LockedValueSingleNewPair{} and \LockedValueCrossNewPair{}, almost the same in both. What differs is the part that had a choice, \OpenValueSingleNewPair{} within an exchange against \OpenValueCrossNewPair{} across it, so eligible corridors account for \LockedValueSingleNewPairShare{} of the margin within an exchange and \LockedValueCrossNewPairShare{} across it. The entry margin is therefore the mirror image of the reweighting margin. There the cross-exchange excess was carried entirely by corridors whose vehicle was never in question; here it is carried entirely by corridors that had one and were routed through a stablecoin anyway. Read together, the two say that a market already trading across exchanges gains stablecoin share mainly where no other intermediary was available, while the value arriving with markets that begin to trade across exchanges comes from corridors that could have been routed through the native token.

That entry margin is gross. The decomposition nets it against the corridors that stopped trading, and the netting is not an accounting nuisance: it is the comparison the term makes. Averaged across the two years, corridors observed in only one of them carry \CohortMass{} of route activity and \CohortValueMass{} of the value moved, and the netted term is that mass multiplied by the gap between how two populations were routed---the corridors that left and the corridors that arrived. The two are not alike. The departing corridors sent \CohortExitShare{} of their routes and \CohortValueExitShare{} of their dollars through a stablecoin; the corridors that replaced them send \CohortEnterShare{} and \CohortValueEnterShare{}. Removing the wrapped-ether-endpoint corridors, whose vehicle was settled before they opened, leaves \CohortExitOpenShare{} against \CohortEnterOpenShare{} by route count and \CohortValueExitOpenShare{} against \CohortValueEnterOpenShare{} by value, and across exchanges the arriving corridors that could have chosen anything route \CohortValueCrossEnterOpenShare{} of their dollars through a stablecoin. The largest margin in the count decomposition is therefore not adoption inside a settled set of markets. It is the replacement of one population of markets by another that routes differently from the day it opens. \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics} estimate the incumbency advantage of an international currency across a stable roster of trading relationships and find persistence without lock-in; here the roster is what turns over, and the challenger's gain is concentrated in the corridors that did not exist when the incumbent's network was formed.

Replacement on that scale moves activity between the two populations as well as changing how each is routed, and the decomposition prices the two separately. Corridors observed in both years fell from \BlockWeightBase{} of route activity to \BlockWeightEnd{}, a shift of \BlockShift{}, and from \BlockValueWeightBase{} of the value moved to \BlockValueWeightEnd{}, a shift of \BlockValueShift{}; across exchanges the dollar shift is \BlockValueCrossShift{}. Roughly a fifth of the value in the comparison therefore changed which population of corridors carried it. Against those magnitudes the term itself is \BlockTerm{} by route count and \BlockValueTerm{} by value, the smallest of the four and the only one no other margin overlaps. It is small not because little activity moved but because the two populations are priced almost alike: averaged over the two years, corridors alive in both route \BlockCommonMid{} of their routes and \BlockValueCommonMid{} of their dollars through a stablecoin, against \BlockExclusiveMid{} and \BlockValueExclusiveMid{} for corridors alive in only one, gaps of \BlockGap{} and \BlockValueGap{}. The term is negative in every weighting and every route scope: holding both populations at those midpoint routing rates, the activity that changed population lowered the stablecoin share rather than raising it. The rotation is thus not activity migrating into corridors that already used the challenger. It is that the population receiving the activity routes differently from the one it replaced. \citet{AmitiItskhokiKonings2022Dominant} find invoicing currency to be close to a fixed attribute of an exporter, switching for a small minority of observations over almost four years, with the composition of destinations explaining little of the cross-sectional variation. Choice is similarly sticky inside a continuing corridor here; the difference is that the population of corridors turns over fast enough for composition to carry the whole aggregate change.

\subsection{USDT's intermediary role}
\label{sec:dominance:excess}

USDC and USDT together account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stablecoin count share. We examine whether USDT's changing role is specific to intermediation by comparing its share of intermediary use with its share of appearances at observed route endpoints. Direct routes contribute to the endpoint denominator; longer routes contribute once for each intermediary they use. A ratio above one means that the currency appears more often as an intermediary than at either end of an observed pool route.

On matched January--June dates, the difference between USDT's intermediary and route-endpoint shares rises from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}. The change is \USDTEndpointGapChange{}, with a standard error of \USDTEndpointGapSE{}. The ratio of the same two roles, shown as a descriptive full-period comparison in Table~\ref{tab:usdt-transition}, rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} in 2024 to \USDTCountExcessEnd{} in January--June 2026 by count; the dollar-weighted ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. The matched-window gap is the basis for the transition claim.

The expansion of cross-exchange routes accounts for \USDTVenueMixCountShare{} of USDT's count-share increase and \USDTVenueMixValueShare{} of its dollar-weighted increase. The remaining \USDTVenueWithinCountShare{} by count and \USDTVenueWithinValueShare{} by value occurs within the single- and cross-exchange groups. This decomposition conditions on the route that was executed; it does not reveal every alternative available to the router.

\subsection{Venue span and route complexity}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

Cross-exchange routing expands sharply during the sample. Access to pools on several exchanges can favour an intermediary with liquidity dispersed across venues, while also making direct execution easier to find. Cross-exchange routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} of route count in 2020 to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} in 2026 and account for \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of dollar-weighted route value in 2026.

Intermediation becomes less common over 2022--2026 even as cross-exchange routing grows. Its count share falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} to \FullIntermediationEnd{} across the full exchange set and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} among five exchanges observed throughout the comparison. The changes are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Stablecoin share nevertheless rises within every observed group defined by exchange span and route complexity. Its count-share increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across exchanges than within one exchange, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}; the dollar-weighted estimates rise in both groups.

Taken together, the estimates separate cross-exchange trading, the incidence of intermediation, and the composition of intermediaries. Wider venue span coincides with less intermediation overall, while stablecoins gain among the indirect routes that remain in both single- and cross-exchange groups. Section~\ref{sec:rivals:complexity} turns that stratification into a test of whether the aggregate rotation is a by-product of how routes were built. Isolating the effect of router access requires comparisons among the same ordered source--destination asset pairs, trade sizes, and feasible paths.
