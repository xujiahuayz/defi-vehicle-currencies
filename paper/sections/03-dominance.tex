\section{Rotation in the vehicle role}
\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Stablecoins carry a much larger share of vehicle activity in 2026 than in 2024. Comparing the same days of the calendar year, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count. The paired-day change is \StableCountChange{}, with a standard error of \StableCountSE{}. Their dollar-weighted share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, an increase of \StableValueChange{} with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. The count series gives equal weight to each exact two-leg route. The dollar-weighted series uses routes for which the values at the source, intermediary, and destination agree within 20\%. Table~\ref{tab:rotation} reports both. Each figure is an average over the markets traded in its year, and those markets differ between the two years. Section~\ref{sec:dominance:pairs} shows that the difference is what moves the aggregate: within a source--destination pair traded in both years, the intermediary carrying the route barely changes.

\subsection{Composition of ultimate pairs}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

A vehicle's aggregate share can rise in three ways. It can be chosen more often within an ultimate (source--destination) pair traded in both years; routed activity can move toward continuing ultimate pairs that already use it; or ultimate pairs traded in only one of the two years can route through it. Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} shows that the first way accounts for little of the stablecoin increase and that the other two carry it.

Table~\ref{tab:rotation} averages daily shares, whereas Panels A through C of Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} pool route count or value across the matched January--June windows. The different weighting explains why the headline changes of \StableCountChange{} and \StableValueChange{} differ slightly from the decomposition totals of \MarketBridgeTotal{} and \PairValueTotal{}.

Those three ways form an accounting identity once the third is split in two, because pairs traded in only one year both route their activity differently and carry a changing share of it. The rest of this section reads the resulting four terms one at a time. Fix one of the measures in Equation~\eqref{eq:dominance} and let $p$ index ordered source--destination pairs. A pair is continuing when it carries native-or-stable routes in both comparison years and year-specific when it carries them in only one. Let $W_y$ be the share of year-$y$ native-plus-stable mass carried by continuing pairs, $\omega_{p,y}$ the weight of continuing pair $p$ within that mass, and $s_{p,y}$ its stablecoin share, so continuing pairs route $S_{\mathrm{com},y}=\sum_{p}\omega_{p,y}s_{p,y}$ of their activity through a stablecoin and year-specific pairs route $S_{\mathrm{exc},y}$ of theirs. The aggregate share is $S_{y}=W_{y}S_{\mathrm{com},y}+(1-W_{y})S_{\mathrm{exc},y}$. Writing $\Delta x=x_{2026}-x_{2024}$ and $\bar{x}=(x_{2024}+x_{2026})/2$,
% docs/specifications/confirmatory.json, e1_2_conditional_pair_decomposition,
% formula_id midpoint_common_exclusive_support_v1. The displayed identity is the
% frozen formula, not a restatement of it: the four labelled terms are the
% within_common, common_pair_reweighting, common_support_mass and
% exclusive_pair_contribution components in that order, and the audit holds the
% residual to 1e-12. Refresh this display if formula_id changes.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pair-decomposition}
\Delta S=
\underbrace{\bar{W}\sum_{p}\bar{\omega}_{p}\,\Delta s_{p}}_{\text{within continuing pairs}}
+\underbrace{\bar{W}\sum_{p}\bar{s}_{p}\,\Delta\omega_{p}}_{\text{reweighting}}
+\underbrace{\bigl(\bar{S}_{\mathrm{com}}-\bar{S}_{\mathrm{exc}}\bigr)\Delta W}_{\text{support mass}}
+\underbrace{(1-\bar{W})\,\Delta S_{\mathrm{exc}}}_{\text{year-specific pairs}}.
\end{equation}
Midpoint weights make the identity exact by splitting each product's interaction evenly between its two factors instead of charging it to one year. Panels B and C report the four terms. Within continuing pairs, the stablecoin share moves \PairPooledWithin{} by route count and \PairValueWithin{} by value. Reweighting across those pairs contributes \PairPooledReweight{} and \PairValueReweight{}; the migration of mass between the continuing and year-specific populations, \PairPooledSupportMass{} and \PairValueSupportMass{}; and the pairs traded in only one year, \PairPooledExclusive{} and \PairValueExclusive{}. The first of the three ways is worth almost nothing on either measure, and the other two carry the rotation between them. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} decomposes dollar dominance in the other direction, separating the volume of a given dollar pair into the part that exists to route two other currencies and the part that does not. That split runs inside a pair; the one that carries the rotation measured here runs across them.

Panel A factors the same route-count total a second way. Equation~\eqref{eq:pair-decomposition} takes a pair's vehicle-routed activity as given and asks only which vehicle carried it; Panel A asks first how much the pair traded, then how much of that trading passed through any intermediary at all, and only then which intermediary it was. Let $M_{p,y}$ be the routes observed in pair $p$ in year $y$, let $I_{p,y}$ be the fraction of them that passed through a native or stablecoin intermediary, and let $s_{p,y}$ be the stablecoin share of that intermediated activity, the same object the identity above assigns to a continuing pair. For any set of pairs $\mathcal{Q}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:incidence-functional}
F_{y}(\mathcal{Q})=\frac{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}M_{p,y}I_{p,y}s_{p,y}}
{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}M_{p,y}I_{p,y}}
\end{equation}
is then the stablecoin share those pairs deliver in year $y$. Write $\mathcal{N}$ for every ordered pair, $\mathcal{N}_{M}\subseteq\mathcal{N}$ for the pairs that traded in both years, and $\mathcal{N}_{V}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{M}$ for the pairs that carried an intermediary in both years, so that $S_{y}=F_{y}(\mathcal{N})$ and $\mathcal{N}_{V}$ is the continuing population of Equation~\eqref{eq:pair-decomposition}. Bridging the aggregate to the narrower population twice and allocating what is left inside $\mathcal{N}_{V}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:market-incidence}
\Delta S=
\underbrace{\Delta\bigl[F(\mathcal{N})-F(\mathcal{N}_{M})\bigr]}_{\text{markets start or stop trading}}
+\underbrace{\Delta\bigl[F(\mathcal{N}_{M})-F(\mathcal{N}_{V})\bigr]}_{\text{vehicle role starts or stops}}
+\underbrace{\phi_{M}+\phi_{I}+\phi_{s}}_{\text{movement inside }\mathcal{N}_{V}},
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{M}$, $\phi_{I}$, and $\phi_{s}$ give each factor's average marginal contribution to $F(\mathcal{N}_{V})$ over the six orders in which market activity, vehicle incidence, and stablecoin share can be carried from their 2024 to their 2026 values. Averaging over orders is what lets the three exhaust $\Delta F(\mathcal{N}_{V})$ without charging an interaction to whichever factor happens to move first. Read in this order, the classical vehicle-currency question of which intermediary minimises the cost of exchanging a given pair of assets \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies} is the last of the five terms. The four before it record where trading happens; only the fifth records which intermediary a trade selects.

Pairs entering or leaving the sample contribute \MarketSupportBridge{}; established markets that gain or lose a vehicle route, \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}; market activity shifting across the pairs that carry a vehicle in both years, \MarketActivityReweight{}; and changes in how often those pairs use a vehicle at all, \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}. Direct stable-for-native substitution among them contributes \WithinPairStableShare{}. The two factorisations agree on which pairs continue and on nothing else below that. The three allocated terms sum to the change in $\mathcal{N}_{V}$'s own stablecoin share, while the identity's within-pair and reweighting terms sum to that same change multiplied by the share of choice mass $\mathcal{N}_{V}$ carries, averaged over the two years. That relation holds exactly, and it is why a component of one panel is never the same object as the similarly named component of the other. Equation~\eqref{eq:market-incidence} also cuts finer. It splits the pairs traded in only one year into markets that stop or start trading altogether and established markets that keep trading and stop or start using a vehicle, and it splits the reweighting of continuing pairs into how much those pairs traded and how often they used an intermediary at all. The distance this opens is small where it matters most: the within-pair term is \WithinPairStableShare{} under the finer factorisation and \PairPooledWithin{} under the identity, and both sit inside the confidence interval of the matched estimate reported in Panel D, whose upper limit is \MatchedMarketCountCIUpper{}. Whichever way the count total is cut, comparable trades did not switch intermediary.

The bridge's smallest term is the one that says the most once its population is opened. Pairs that gain or lose a vehicle route contribute \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}, which invites the reading that established markets barely changed whether they used an intermediary at all. Panel A partitions each year's choice mass into three classes, and the class behind that term is both narrow and closing: markets traded in both years whose vehicle role appears in only one carry \IncidenceRoleTurnoverWeightBase{} of 2024 choice mass across \IncidenceRoleTurnoverPairsBase{} ordered pairs, and \IncidenceRoleTurnoverWeightEnd{} of 2026 choice mass across \IncidenceRoleTurnoverPairsEnd{}. Its routed activity passes through a stablecoin more often than either other class does in either year, \IncidenceRoleTurnoverStableBase{} in 2024 and \IncidenceRoleTurnoverStableEnd{} in 2026, against \IncidenceCommonStableBase{} and \IncidenceCommonStableEnd{} for the \IncidenceCommonPairsBase{} pairs that carry a vehicle in both years and \IncidenceMarketTurnoverStableBase{} and \IncidenceMarketTurnoverStableEnd{} for pairs whose market is observed in only one. The margin on which an established market starts or stops using an intermediary at all is therefore narrow, shrinking, and routed through the challenger where it is used. That bounds a third account of the rotation, in which a stablecoin gains aggregate share because markets that had been trading directly begin routing indirectly and use a stablecoin when they do. The account is confined to a population never larger than \IncidenceRoleTurnoverWeightBase{} of routed choice mass and cannot carry a change of \MarketBridgeTotal{}. Membership in the class turns over by construction: a pair belongs to it only if its vehicle role is present in one year and absent in the other, so no pair in it carries an intermediary in both years and none can register a change of intermediary. The distance between the class's two stablecoin shares records which markets occupied it in each year. Appearance in the class and disappearance from it answer to the same forces as the routing decision itself.

Conditioning this finely has a price, and the price is coverage. Of the \SamplePairs{} ordered pairs that carry a native-or-stable route in either year, \BlockPairs{} carry one in both and make up the continuing block of Equation~\eqref{eq:pair-decomposition}. Requiring the same calendar date and the same route scope as well leaves \MatchedPairs{} pairs by count and \MatchedValuePairs{} by value. Those pairs carry \MatchedCoverageBase{} of 2024 routed activity and \MatchedCoverageEnd{} of 2026, but \MatchedValueCoverageBase{} and \MatchedValueCoverageEnd{} of the dollars moved. Measured inside the continuing block alone, the same four figures are \MatchedBlockCoverageBase{} and \MatchedBlockCoverageEnd{} by count, \MatchedValueBlockCoverageBase{} and \MatchedValueBlockCoverageEnd{} by value. The matched estimate therefore covers a minority of route counts and a much larger part of the value, because dollars concentrate in the pairs and days observed on both sides of the comparison. \citet{AmitiItskhokiKonings2022Dominant} read the same gap between a currency's share of transactions and its share of traded value as evidence about which trades are the small ones; here it says which trades the matched comparison retains. The identity supplies the complement, since its within-pair term is taken over the whole continuing block under no date or scope condition: \PairPooledWithin{} by count and \PairValueWithin{} by value, beside the matched \MatchedMarketCountChange{} and \MatchedMarketValueChange{}. Each matched interval contains the identity term of its own weighting. The narrow conditioned comparison and the wide accounting term put within-pair substitution in the same place, so its absence is not a property of the matched sample.

These entry, endpoint, architecture, and identity screens are the entry analogue of the selection margin in \citet{AmitiItskhokiKonings2022Dominant}: composition can move an aggregate while choices inside continuing units remain sticky. This is not evidence that the first route causes later routing. It says that new markets tend to arrive already attached to a vehicle regime, and that the regime is highly persistent over the first four months.

The same identity stickiness appears in a stress episode. During the March 2023 USDC/SVB window, stable-vehicle route activity rose by \StressEventStableActivityRise{} to \StressEventStressRoutesPerDay{} routes per day, but USDC's share inside the stable-vehicle class was \StressEventStressUsdcShare{}, against \StressEventPreUsdcShare{} in the prior window, a change of \StressEventUsdcShareChange{}. USDT's share rose only afterward, to \StressEventPostUsdtShare{} in the post-window. The capital side diverged from the route shock: inside the five-candidate set, stablecoins' route share rose by \StressEventStableRouteShareChange{} during the window while their deposited-capital share moved \StressEventStableCapitalShareChange{}, and after the window WETH's capital share was \StressEventPostWethCapitalShareChange{} higher than before. Stablecoin pegs depend on arbitrage and redemption arrangements \citep{LyonsViswanathNatraj2023Stablecoins,AnaduEtAl2023StablecoinRuns}, so this peg shock remains observational. Even so, the episode is informative about identity under stress: vehicle identity stayed with USDC while its cash-like promise was under pressure, and LP capital stayed with the native reserve stock rather than chasing the temporary stable-route surge.

That entry margin is gross. The decomposition subtracts the corridors that stopped trading, and the netting is not an accounting nuisance: it is the comparison the term makes. Averaged across the two years, corridors observed in only one of them carry \CohortMass{} of route activity and \CohortValueMass{} of the value moved, and the netted term is that mass multiplied by the gap between how two populations were routed---the corridors that left and the corridors that arrived. The two are not alike. The departing corridors sent \CohortExitShare{} of their routes and \CohortValueExitShare{} of their dollars through a stablecoin; the corridors that replaced them send \CohortEnterShare{} and \CohortValueEnterShare{}. Removing the wrapped-ether-endpoint corridors, whose vehicle was settled before they opened, leaves the departing corridors at \CohortExitOpenShare{} by route count and \CohortValueExitOpenShare{} by value, and the arriving ones at \CohortEnterOpenShare{} and \CohortValueEnterOpenShare{}. Across exchanges the arriving corridors that could have chosen anything route \CohortValueCrossEnterOpenShare{} of their dollars through a stablecoin. The largest margin in the count decomposition is therefore not adoption inside a settled set of markets. It is the replacement of one population of markets by another that routes differently from the day it opens. \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics} estimate the incumbency advantage of an international currency across a stable roster of trading relationships and find persistence without lock-in; here the roster is what turns over, and the challenger's gain is concentrated in the corridors that did not exist when the incumbent's network was formed.

Two different economies produce that gap, and the two weightings do not agree on which one is at work. Corridors are of three kinds: those with wrapped ether at an endpoint, which cannot use it to intermediate and route through a stablecoin by construction; those with a stablecoin at an endpoint, which may still be carried either way; and those with some other asset at each end. Evaluated at the two cohorts' midpoints, the gap separates exactly into the mix of kinds each cohort brings and the rate at which each kind routes. By route count the mix carries \ReplaceCompositionShare{} of the \CohortNet{} netted term, and nearly all of that is the first kind: wrapped-ether-paired corridors are \ReplaceLockedEnterWeight{} of arriving activity against \ReplaceLockedExitWeight{} of departing activity, worth \ReplaceLockedComposition{} by themselves. They enter the margin through their weight alone, because their routing rate is one in both cohorts. The remaining \ReplaceRate{} is a rate difference among corridors that could have been carried either way. By value the ordering reverses: the mix contributes \ReplaceValueComposition{} and the rates \ReplaceValueRate{}, or \ReplaceValueRateShare{} of \CohortValueNet{}. Arriving corridors with a stablecoin at an endpoint send \ReplaceValueStableEndEnterRate{} of their dollars through a stablecoin where the departing corridors of the same kind sent \ReplaceValueStableEndExitRate{}, and arriving corridors with some other asset at each end send \ReplaceValueOtherEndEnterRate{} against \ReplaceValueOtherEndExitRate{}. No corridor appears on both sides of those comparisons, so none of these figures describes an intermediary being changed; the dollars now moving through corridors that opened after the challenger was established were never routed the old way. \citet{DowdGreenaway1993CurrencyCompetition} make an agent's willingness to switch depend on the expectation that others will switch too, and the excess inertia that follows is a claim about the agents already holding the incumbent. Their model is silent on the agents who arrive later, and it is those the data separate: inside a continuing corridor the intermediary barely moves, while the corridors that open once the challenger is available carry it from their first trade.

\subsection{The cross-section within a day}
\label{sec:dominance:withinday}

Every comparison so far reads across years, which invites the reading that the calendar is doing the work. If the vehicle role is simply whatever the sample period happens to do, then the rotation is a description of 2020 through 2026 and not of how an intermediary is chosen. The construct itself does not require the calendar. Excess use compares two roles of the same asset on the same day, so it can be asked entirely within a day, of the assets that were available that day, with the year absorbed into a fixed effect.

We therefore write Equation~\eqref{eq:excess-use} as a coefficient. The outcome is a currency's share of the day's intermediary episodes in percentage points; the regressor of interest is its own share of that day's route-endpoint episodes in the same units; and each specification above the pooled baseline absorbs a date fixed effect, so the estimate comes from the cross-section of currencies observed on one day. This adds two quantities the ratio cannot express. The slope on endpoint share says whether intermediary use rises more or less than one for one with an asset's own trade demand, and the class intercepts say how much of the role an asset class takes beyond what its demand implies. \citet{AmitiItskhokiKonings2022Dominant} identify currency choice the same way, absorbing the macroeconomic environment in fixed effects so that what remains is the differential response of firms facing the same conditions. The ratio remains the primary extent measure; the regression is the same comparison with the calendar demoted from the identifying variation to a control. The sample is the \DateFETokenDays{} currency-days on which \DateFETokens{} currencies were quoted at a route endpoint with at least twenty route units, spanning \DateFEDays{} days. Inference clusters on date and currency, and on the few-currency cuts on date alone with a thirty-day Bartlett lag, since there the repeated sampling unit is the day.

Absorbing the calendar changes almost nothing. The native premium moves from \DateFEPooledNative{} pooled to \DateFEDayNative{} within day, and the stablecoin premium from \DateFEPooledStable{} to \DateFEDayStable{}; both are estimated imprecisely across currencies, with intervals of \DateFEPooledNativeCI{} and \DateFEPooledStableCI{}. The differences between asset classes are cross-sectional, not a period effect. What does move them is conditioning on the currency's own trade demand. The native intercept falls to \DateFEDemandNative{} with a standard error of \DateFEDemandNativeSE{}, while the stablecoin intercept holds at \DateFEDemandStable{} (\DateFEDemandStableSE{}). The apparent intermediary dominance of the native asset is accounted for by its own demand; the stablecoin's is not. That is the comparison the search-theoretic account of a medium of exchange asks for, since in \citet{KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium} an asset is used to intermediate because of what the other assets available at that moment can do, not because of its own turnover. The difference between the two, \DateFEStableMinusNative{} with an interval of \DateFEStableMinusNativeCI{}, is not one this cross-section can separate from zero.

The pass-through of demand into the intermediary role is \DateFESlope{} across currencies within a day (\DateFESlopeSE{}), and \DateFESlopeWithinToken{} (\DateFESlopeWithinTokenSE{}, \DateFESlopeWithinTokenP{}) within a currency and within a day once \DateFEWithinTokenAbsorbed{} currency and day effects are absorbed. A currency that gains a point of trade demand gains about a point of intermediary use; the excess above one in the cross-section is composition between currencies. The slope also differs by class, but not everywhere. Native pass-through exceeds imported by \DateFESlopeNativeMinusImported{}, with an interval of \DateFESlopeNativeMinusImportedCI{}; the stablecoin slope exceeds the native slope by \DateFESlopeStableMinusNative{}, with an interval of \DateFESlopeStableMinusNativeCI{} that the data cannot separate from zero. We report the second because it is the comparison the rotation would predict, and it does not hold.

Restricting the cross-section to the five currencies that carry the vehicle role sharpens it. With the stablecoin class as the base, the native asset intermediates \DateFECandidateNative{} relative to a stablecoin on the same day and at the same trade demand, with an interval of \DateFECandidateNativeCI{} over \DateFECandidateTokenDays{} currency-days. Over all \DateFEClassifiedTokens{} classified currencies the same contrast is \DateFEClassifiedNative{}, with an interval of \DateFEClassifiedNativeCI{}. The two magnitudes answer one question at two scales, and quoting either alone would misstate its size: the candidate set concentrates the day's share mass in five currencies, so its coefficients are large, and the classified set spreads the same question across a long tail whose currencies hold almost none of it, so its coefficients are small and the contrast is not separable there. Inside the stablecoin class the role is not uniform either. Against a fiat-reserve claim on the same day and at the same demand, an on-chain-collateralised stablecoin intermediates \DateFEBackingOnChain{} (\DateFEBackingOnChainCI{}) and a synthetic one \DateFEBackingSynthetic{} (\DateFEBackingSyntheticCI{}), while USDT intermediates \DateFETether{} relative to USDC (\DateFETetherCI{}). The class that gains the role over the sample is not homogeneous in it.

Three features of the design have to be measured before the estimates can be read economically. First, both variables are shares of the same day's route universe, and a currency cannot intermediate a route it terminates, so a mechanical crowd-out channel exists; it is negligible here, since the mean currency-day uses \DateFECeilingMean{} of the routes it does not itself terminate and only \DateFECeilingHalfShare{} of currency-days exceed half of that ceiling, and in any case crowd-out can only push the slope down. Second, each currency's own episodes enter the denominator of both of its shares. Recomputing both against the day's episodes excluding the currency's own raises the slope to \DateFELeaveOutSlope{} (\DateFELeaveOutSlopeSE{}), so the association is not the shared denominator. Third, the twenty-unit floor is a choice, and the slope is \DateFEFloorFiveSlope{} at five units and \DateFEFloorHundredSlope{} at one hundred. Calendar time returns here only as a sample split: refitting on each half of the sample gives slopes of \DateFESlopeEarly{} and \DateFESlopeLate{}, and native intercepts of \DateFENativeEarly{} \DateFENativeEarlyCI{} and \DateFENativeLate{} \DateFENativeLateCI{}, both intervals covering zero. One result does not survive reweighting. Weighting currency-days by route units turns the native intercept to \DateFEWeightedNative{}, so its sign depends on whether each currency-day or each route counts, and we read it as descriptive.

\subsection{Venue span and route complexity}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

Cross-exchange routing expands sharply during the sample. Access to pools on several exchanges can favour an intermediary with liquidity dispersed across venues, while also making direct execution easier to find. Cross-exchange routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} of route count in 2020 to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} in 2026 and account for \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of dollar-weighted route value in 2026.

Intermediation becomes less common over 2022--2026 even as cross-exchange routing grows. Its count share falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} to \FullIntermediationEnd{} across the full exchange set and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} among five exchanges observed throughout the comparison. The changes are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Stablecoin share nevertheless rises within every observed group defined by exchange span and route complexity. Its count-share increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across exchanges than within one exchange, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}; the dollar-weighted estimates rise in both groups.

Those estimates compare two endpoint years. The calendar supplies the variation that identifies them. A second reading of the same stratification removes that reliance. Each trading day contributes one observation for its single-exchange routes and one for its cross-exchange routes, and a day effect absorbs everything the two halves share, from gas prices to market direction. The remaining difference measures how far apart the two halves of one day's route universe stand.
