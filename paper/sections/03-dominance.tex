\section{Rotation in the vehicle role}
\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Each exact two-leg route $i\rightarrow k\rightarrow o$ contributes one observed intermediary. Count-weighted shares give every route equal weight. Value-weighted shares instead capture economic size and use the subset for which source, intermediary, and destination dollar values agree within 20\%. I report both because route frequency and routed value need not rank vehicle currencies in the same way.

The shift has progressed further in routed value than in route counts. Stablecoins lead by supported value before they sustain a lead in the number of routes. This difference does not imply a common switch within larger individual trades; the endpoint-pair results below show that shifts in activity across markets are central. The ordering remains silent on mechanism.

Native intermediation rebounds during 2023 and 2024. Stablecoins regain the value lead in 2025, whereas their count lead remains short-lived. I therefore describe a \emph{rotation}; persistent replacement would require a longer post-change lead.

\subsection{The cross-section of endpoint pairs}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

The aggregate stable share can rise in two ways. Stable vehicles can gain share within an endpoint pair that carries vehicle routes in both years, or aggregate activity can move toward pairs on which stable vehicles are already prevalent. I separate pairs with native-or-stable vehicle activity in both endpoint years from those with activity in only one. Among the continuing pairs, I further separate changes in stable share within a pair from changes in the pair's weight in total vehicle activity. The four terms add to the aggregate change by construction. They describe where the change occurs, not why it occurs.

By route count, the pooled stable share rises from \PairPooledBase{} in 2024 to \PairPooledEnd{} in 2026. Year-specific endpoint pairs account for \PairPooledExclusive{} of the \PairPooledTotal{} increase, and changing activity weights among continuing pairs account for \PairPooledReweight{}. Within-pair changes contribute \PairPooledWithin{}, while the changing share of activity carried by the two pair sets contributes \PairPooledSupportMass{}. The count rotation is therefore a change in the cross-section of endpoint markets carrying vehicle routes, not a common switch from the native asset to a stablecoin within the same market.

Supported value has a different allocation. Its stable share rises by \PairValueTotal{}. Changes in activity weights among continuing pairs account for \PairValueReweight{}, while year-specific pairs account for \PairValueExclusive{}; changes in the relative mass of the two pair sets contribute \PairValueSupportMass{}. Changes within continuing pairs again contribute only \PairValueWithin{}. Across venues, year-specific pairs account for more of the value change; within a single venue, changes in continuing-pair weights are the larger component. This difference is useful descriptively, but it does not identify a routing-technology effect.

Pair turnover and changing activity weights are accounting terms, not mechanisms. Endpoint demand, route feasibility, liquidity placement, venue reach, and router search can all alter which pairs carry vehicle routes and how heavily those pairs are used. A fixed-opportunity test must separate these possibilities.

\subsection{Intermediation and endpoint demand}
\label{sec:dominance:excess}

A stable asset can become common because traders want to acquire or sell it, without becoming a vehicle between other assets. The excess-use ratio separates the two margins. It divides the asset's share of intermediary positions by its share of route endpoints on the same perimeter. A ratio above one means that its intermediary share exceeds its endpoint share.

The aggregate shift is concentrated in one stablecoin design. USDC and USDT jointly account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stable count share. USDC already has excess vehicle use at the start of the comparison and changes little. USDT drives the shift within the stablecoin category. Its count excess-use ratio rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{}, while its strict-value ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. The strict-value intermediary-minus-endpoint gap moves from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}, a \USDTEndpointGapChange{} change with a \USDTEndpointGapSE{} standard error.

The result is specific to USDT. It becomes disproportionately more common in the intermediary position after accounting for its endpoint use. The available contrast leaves the relevant feature of USDT unidentified.

\subsection{The integration rival}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

The strongest mechanical rival is the expansion of the trading network. As routers span more venues, they can reach liquidity that was previously fragmented. A stable intermediary might then gain share because its pools become reachable, not because monetary coordination has strengthened. Conversely, the same integration can make direct paths easier to find and reduce the need for an intermediary altogether.

The data show both forces. Cross-venue routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} of route count, reaching \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of value. Yet the share of economic routes that use a true intermediary falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} in 2022 to \FullIntermediationEnd{} in 2026. On a balanced five-venue perimeter it falls to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{}. The declines are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Integration expands while intermediation contracts.

Within the routes that remain indirect, stable share rises in every observed integration-by-complexity count cell. The increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across venues than within one venue, with a \CrossVenueRotationSE{} standard error. Thus, the changing mix of observed venue span and route length does not account for the result. Endpoint-pair composition nevertheless changes sharply inside those broad strata. The comparison shows where the rotation is larger; it does not hold the route opportunity set fixed.

Better search may still activate dispersed stablecoin liquidity within the observed venue and complexity strata. Distinguishing that channel requires a fixed-opportunity comparison; the cross-venue interaction alone describes where the rotation is strongest.

\subsection{Time, architecture, and reversal}
\label{sec:dominance:architecture}

Protocol architecture changes during the same period, but protocol launches coincide with other market changes. The observed entry and exit windows also vary the comparison set. Entry often coincides with role appearance and exit with role disappearance, so part of the measured change follows mechanically from crossing zero. Their estimand combines architecture and composition, leaving an architecture effect unidentified.

Stablecoin vehicle dominance therefore rises sharply but non-monotonically. USDT accounts for the principal change within the stablecoin category, but the market-wide increase does not take the form of stablecoins repeatedly displacing the native asset within a fixed endpoint pair. It occurs mainly because the set and weight of endpoint markets carrying vehicle activity change. The rotation appears among both single- and cross-venue routes; its cause remains open.
