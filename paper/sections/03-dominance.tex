\section{Rotation in the vehicle role}
\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Stablecoins carry a much larger share of vehicle activity in 2026 than in 2024. Comparing the same days of the calendar year, their share among native and stable vehicles rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count. The paired-day change is \StableCountChange{}, with a calendar-HAC standard error of \StableCountSE{}. Their share of supported routed value rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, an increase of \StableValueChange{} with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. These measures capture different dimensions of intermediation. The count series gives equal weight to each exact two-leg route and uses full route-topology support; the value series weights routes by dollar size when the values recorded along the route agree within 20\%. Table~\ref{tab:rotation} reports both.

\subsection{Composition of source--destination pairs}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

The aggregate rise can come from substitution within an established ordered source--destination pair or from changes in the pairs across which routed trading takes place. Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} separates these possibilities. By route count, source--destination pairs entering or leaving the sample contribute \MarketSupportBridge{} of the increase, activity shifting across continuing pairs contributes \MarketActivityReweight{}, and changes in how often those pairs use an intermediary contribute \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}. The appearance or disappearance of a native-or-stable intermediary contributes \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}. Direct stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use a vehicle in both years contributes only \WithinPairStableShare{}, even though stablecoin share rises from \ObservedBothYearsBase{} to \ObservedBothYearsEnd{} among pairs observed in both comparison years. The value-weighted decomposition makes the allocation result still sharper: of the \PairValueTotal{} increase, \PairValueReweight{} comes from activity shifting across continuing pairs and \PairValueExclusive{} from pairs that carry a native-or-stable vehicle route in only one comparison year, while within-pair substitution contributes \PairValueWithin{}.

Matching the ordered source--destination asset pair, day of the calendar year, and single- or cross-exchange route type yields the same economic conclusion. Stablecoin share changes by \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketCountSE{}, and by \MatchedMarketValueChange{} by value, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketValueSE{}. The corresponding upper confidence bounds, \MatchedMarketCountCIUpper{} and \MatchedMarketValueCIUpper{}, allow modest substitution inside continuing pairs but exclude a change large enough to account for the aggregate increase.

s^{(m)}_{c,y}=\alpha^{(m)}_{c}+\beta^{(m)}\mathbf{1}\{y=2026\}+\varepsilon^{(m)}_{c,y},
\qquad y\in\{2024,2026\}.
\end{equation}
An observation is one comparison group in one year. Weighted least squares uses the native-plus-stable denominator for metric $m$; 2024 is the omitted year. The full count specification contains 188,520 observations, 94,260 fixed-effect groups, 5,432 ordered-pair clusters, and 362 calendar-date clusters. The two supported-value specifications each contain 182,834 observations, 91,417 fixed-effect groups, 5,278 ordered-pair clusters, and 362 calendar-date clusters. These regressions are descriptive because the observed route type and the pairs traded can respond to the same forces as intermediary use.

New listings or endpoint demand can bring a pair into the sample; demand or liquidity can redirect activity across continuing pairs; and direct-market depth or router access can change the use of indirect execution. Through these margins, routed activity reallocates across source--destination pairs. This reallocation, rather than stable-for-native substitution within continuing vehicle-using pairs, carries most of the stablecoin increase.

\subsection{USDT and USDC}
\label{sec:dominance:excess}

To distinguish vehicle use from ordinary demand for a token, we compare each stablecoin's share of intermediary episodes with its share of endpoint appearances on the prespecified currency perimeter. Direct routes contribute to the endpoint denominator, and routes with several intermediaries contribute one episode for each intermediary. An excess-use ratio above one means that the token's intermediary share exceeds its endpoint share. USDC and USDT together account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stablecoin count share. Using the available observations in each endpoint year---the full 2024 calendar year and January--June 2026---USDT's count ratio rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{}, while its value ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. On the paired January--June dates used for transition inference, the value-weighted difference between USDT's intermediary and endpoint shares rises from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}, a change of \USDTEndpointGapChange{} with a standard error of \USDTEndpointGapSE{}. USDT thus becomes more prevalent between other assets even after accounting for its growing use as an endpoint.

Reweighting from single- to cross-exchange routes accounts for \USDTVenueMixCountShare{} of USDT's count-share increase and \USDTVenueMixValueShare{} of its supported-value increase. The remaining \USDTVenueWithinCountShare{} by count and \USDTVenueWithinValueShare{} by value occurs within the two venue-scope categories. The decomposition conditions on observed venue span; the feasible alternatives available to the routing system remain unobserved.

The rise in USDT's intermediary use relative to its endpoint use could reflect issuance, redemption access, venue coverage, or the distribution of liquidity across pools, and some of these variables may themselves respond to routed activity. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} returns to these explanations after measuring the contribution of observed venue span.

\subsection{Venue span and route complexity}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

Cross-exchange routing expands sharply during the sample, raising the possibility that broader venue access contributed to the rotation. Access to previously fragmented pools can favour an intermediary with liquidity on several venues; it can also reveal a direct path and remove the need for an intermediary. Cross-exchange routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} of route count in 2020 to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} in 2026 and account for \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of supported value in 2026. Over 2022--2026, intermediation becomes less common: its count share falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} to \FullIntermediationEnd{} across the full venue set and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} on the balanced five-venue perimeter, changes of \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{} with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. In the count-based classification, stablecoin share rises in every observed category defined by venue span and route complexity. Stablecoin share also rises in the separate supported-value estimates for single- and cross-exchange routes. The count-share increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across exchanges than within one exchange, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}.

Taken together, the estimates separate cross-exchange trading, the incidence of intermediation, and the composition of intermediaries. Wider venue span coincides with less intermediation overall, while stablecoins gain among the indirect routes that remain in both single- and cross-exchange groups. Isolating the effect of router access requires comparisons among the same ordered source--destination asset pairs, trade sizes, and feasible paths.
