\section{Rotation in the vehicle role}
\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Stablecoins carry a much larger share of vehicle activity in 2026 than in 2024. Comparing the same days of the calendar year, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count. The paired-day change is \StableCountChange{}, with a standard error of \StableCountSE{}. Their dollar-weighted share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, an increase of \StableValueChange{} with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. The count series gives equal weight to each exact two-leg route. The dollar-weighted series uses routes for which the values at the source, intermediary, and destination agree within 20\%. Table~\ref{tab:rotation} reports both. Each figure is an average over the markets traded in its year, and those markets differ between the two years. Section~\ref{sec:dominance:pairs} shows that the difference is what moves the aggregate: within a source--destination pair traded in both years, the intermediary carrying the route barely changes.

\subsection{Composition of source--destination pairs}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

A vehicle's aggregate share can rise in three ways. It can be chosen more often within a source--destination pair traded in both years; routed activity can move toward continuing pairs that already use it; or pairs traded in only one of the two years can route through it. Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} shows that the first way accounts for little of the stablecoin increase and that the other two carry it.

Table~\ref{tab:rotation} averages daily shares, whereas Panels A and B of Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} pool route count or value across the matched January--June windows. The different weighting explains why the headline changes of \StableCountChange{} and \StableValueChange{} differ slightly from the decomposition totals of \MarketBridgeTotal{} and \PairValueTotal{}.

By route count, pairs observed in only one comparison year contribute \MarketSupportBridge{} of the increase, and shifts in activity across continuing pairs contribute \MarketActivityReweight{}. Changes in how often those pairs use any intermediary add \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}, while the appearance or disappearance of the vehicle role adds \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}. Direct stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use a vehicle in both years contributes only \WithinPairStableShare{}. The dollar-weighted decomposition is sharper still: of the \PairValueTotal{} increase, \PairValueReweight{} comes from activity shifting across continuing pairs and \PairValueExclusive{} from pairs that use a native or stablecoin intermediary in only one comparison year. Within-pair substitution contributes \PairValueWithin{}.

Conditional on the same ordered source--destination pair, calendar date, and single- or cross-exchange route type, stablecoin share changes by \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketCountSE{}, and by \MatchedMarketValueChange{} by value, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketValueSE{}. The corresponding 95\% confidence intervals rule out within-pair changes larger than \MatchedMarketCountCIUpper{} by count and \MatchedMarketValueCIUpper{} by value. The matched-pair estimates are therefore small relative to the aggregate rotation.

Panel C estimates the 2024--2026 difference after absorbing variation specific to an ordered source--destination pair, calendar date within the year, and observed single- or cross-exchange route type. Let $c$ index each such combination and let $s^{(m)}_{c,y}$ denote its stablecoin share under measure $m$. We estimate
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pair-fe}
s^{(m)}_{c,y}=\alpha^{(m)}_{c}+\beta^{(m)}\mathbf{1}\{y=2026\}+\varepsilon^{(m)}_{c,y},
\qquad y\in\{2024,2026\}.
\end{equation}
An observation is one pair--calendar-date--route-type combination in one year. Weighted least squares uses the number or dollar value of native-plus-stable routes as weights; 2024 is the omitted year. Standard errors are clustered by ordered pair and calendar date. The comparison remains descriptive because the pairs traded and the use of one or several exchanges can respond to the same forces as intermediary choice.

Individual pairs make the accounting concrete. Among pairs whose two assets carry a recognised ticker, the largest contribution to the reweighting term comes from \MarginReweightPair{}, which is stablecoin-intermediated in both years and grows from \MarginReweightRoutesBase{} to \MarginReweightRoutesEnd{} routes, or from \MarginReweightWeightBase{} to \MarginReweightWeightEnd{} of routed activity, and adds \MarginReweightContribution{} on its own. The largest such contribution among pairs absent from 2024 comes from \MarginNewPairPair{}, which supplies \MarginNewPairRoutesEnd{} stablecoin-intermediated routes in 2026 and adds \MarginNewPairContribution{}. The largest such contribution within a continuing pair, from \MarginWithinPair{}, adds \MarginWithinContribution{} as its stablecoin share moves from \MarginWithinStableBase{} to \MarginWithinStableEnd{}; the within-pair term summed over all pairs is \MarginWithinTotal{}. Because most newly traded assets have no such ticker, each named pair understates its margin, which is why the margin totals accompany it. New listings or changes in pool access can bring a pair into the sample; trading activity can shift across continuing pairs; and direct-market depth or router access can change the use of indirect execution. Through these margins, routed activity reallocates across source--destination pairs. This reallocation carries most of the stablecoin increase. Stable-for-native substitution within continuing vehicle-using pairs contributes far less.

\subsection{USDT's intermediary role}
\label{sec:dominance:excess}

USDC and USDT together account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stablecoin count share. We examine whether USDT's changing role is specific to intermediation by comparing its share of intermediary use with its share of appearances at observed route endpoints. Direct routes contribute to the endpoint denominator; longer routes contribute once for each intermediary they use. A ratio above one means that the currency appears more often as an intermediary than at either end of an observed pool route.

On matched January--June dates, the difference between USDT's intermediary and route-endpoint shares rises from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}. The change is \USDTEndpointGapChange{}, with a standard error of \USDTEndpointGapSE{}. The ratio of the same two roles, shown as a descriptive full-period comparison in Table~\ref{tab:usdt-transition}, rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} in 2024 to \USDTCountExcessEnd{} in January--June 2026 by count; the dollar-weighted ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. The matched-window gap is the basis for the transition claim.

The expansion of cross-exchange routes accounts for \USDTVenueMixCountShare{} of USDT's count-share increase and \USDTVenueMixValueShare{} of its dollar-weighted increase. The remaining \USDTVenueWithinCountShare{} by count and \USDTVenueWithinValueShare{} by value occurs within the single- and cross-exchange groups. This decomposition conditions on the route that was executed; it does not reveal every alternative available to the router.

The rise in USDT's intermediary use relative to its observed route-endpoint use could reflect issuance, redemption access, venue coverage, or the distribution of liquidity across pools, and some of these variables may themselves respond to routed activity. \subsection{Venue span and route complexity}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

Cross-exchange routing expands sharply during the sample. Access to pools on several exchanges can favour an intermediary with liquidity dispersed across venues, while also making direct execution easier to find. Cross-exchange routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} of route count in 2020 to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} in 2026 and account for \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of dollar-weighted route value in 2026.

Intermediation becomes less common over 2022--2026 even as cross-exchange routing grows. Its count share falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} to \FullIntermediationEnd{} across the full exchange set and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} among five exchanges observed throughout the comparison. The changes are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Stablecoin share nevertheless rises within every observed group defined by exchange span and route complexity. Its count-share increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across exchanges than within one exchange, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}; the dollar-weighted estimates rise in both groups.

Taken together, the estimates separate cross-exchange trading, the incidence of intermediation, and the composition of intermediaries. Wider venue span coincides with less intermediation overall, while stablecoins gain among the indirect routes that remain in both single- and cross-exchange groups. Isolating the effect of router access requires comparisons among the same ordered source--destination asset pairs, trade sizes, and feasible paths.
