Stablecoins gain more by supported value than by route count, and the annual series rules out a simple monotonic succession. Native intermediation rebounds in 2023 and 2024. Count and value therefore describe different parts of the rotation; neither series yet establishes persistent replacement.

\subsection{Endpoint-pair composition}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

Stablecoin dominance can rise in several ways. New endpoint pairs may enter with stablecoin routes. Trading may shift toward continuing pairs where stablecoins are already important, or those pairs may route through an intermediary more often. Stablecoins may also replace the native asset within the same continuing market. Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} separates these changes and avoids attributing the aggregate share to the choice of one representative pair.

By route count, stablecoin share rises from \MarketBridgeBase{} in 2024 to \MarketBridgeEnd{} in 2026. Pairs entering or leaving the sample account for \MarketSupportBridge{} of the increase; shifts in trading across continuing pairs account for \MarketActivityReweight{}; and changes in how often those pairs use a vehicle account for \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}. The appearance or disappearance of a native-or-stable vehicle route contributes \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}, while direct stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use a vehicle in both years contributes only \WithinPairStableShare{}. Stablecoin share still rises from \ObservedBothYearsBase{} to \ObservedBothYearsEnd{} among pairs traded in both endpoint years, so pair turnover is incomplete. The value-weighted accounting reaches the same economic conclusion. Of the \PairValueTotal{} increase, \PairValueReweight{} comes from trading shifting across continuing pairs and \PairValueExclusive{} from pairs that carry a native-or-stable vehicle route in only one endpoint year; substitution within continuing pairs contributes \PairValueWithin{}. Reallocation across markets is the largest value margin, while repeated currency substitution inside a typical continuing market contributes little.

The pooled increase disappears in a stricter comparison of like-for-like observations. When we compare the same ordered endpoint pair on the same day of the year and within the same broad route scope, stablecoin share changes by \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketCountSE{}. The corresponding value estimate is $-1.35$ percentage points, with a standard error of $2.19$ points. These estimates rule out a common drift within comparable realised markets. They instead locate the aggregate transition in changes in which markets are active and where routed activity takes place. The comparison still leaves trade size, liquidity, available routes, and router state free to change, so it does not identify why activity moved across markets.

The components describe different economic changes. New pairs can reflect listings or endpoint demand; trading can move across continuing pairs as demand or liquidity changes; and the use of an intermediary can respond to direct-market depth or to the routes a trading system can reach. Only the final component compares native and stable intermediaries within continuing vehicle-using pairs. Pooling these changes would make a shifting mix of markets look like currency substitution even when no continuing pair changes its vehicle. The accounting exactly divides the change among route components observed in the panel, including their interactions, but its population remains a subset of all trading activity. Explaining the reallocation requires comparisons that follow the same endpoint market while holding trade size and available routes alike.

\subsection{USDT and USDC}
\label{sec:dominance:excess}

A stable asset can become common because traders want to acquire or sell it, without becoming a vehicle between other assets. The excess-use ratio compares the asset's share of intermediary positions with its share of route endpoints on the same perimeter. A ratio above one means that the asset appears between other assets more often than its endpoint use would predict.

USDC and USDT jointly account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stable count share, but their paths differ. USDC begins the comparison with excess vehicle use and changes little. USDT's count ratio rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{}, while its strict-value ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. Its strict-value intermediary-minus-endpoint gap rises from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}, a \USDTEndpointGapChange{} change with a standard error of \USDTEndpointGapSE{}. USDT's intermediary use therefore grows faster than its endpoint use. These comparisons identify the token that drives the stablecoin rotation, but not the feature of USDT responsible for it.

The token-level split rules out an interpretation based on stablecoin adoption alone. If traders simply used more stablecoins as sources and destinations, intermediary and endpoint shares would rise together and the share gap would remain unchanged. USDT instead gains in the middle of routes relative to its own endpoint presence, while USDC starts from a stronger vehicle position and contributes little to the change. The aggregate result is therefore not a common movement of two dollar-pegged assets in vehicle use. It is a change in the division of roles within the stablecoin category, concentrated in the currency whose relative intermediary use was initially lower.

That concentration makes institutional differences relevant without identifying which one is decisive. USDT and USDC differ in issuance, redemption access, venue coverage, and the distribution of liquidity across pools. Each feature could affect the cost of using the token between other assets, and each may respond to the same routed activity the paper measures. The route evidence establishes the magnitude and location of the transition before imposing a mechanism: a particular stablecoin becomes disproportionately common as an intermediary, and the change remains after benchmarking its ordinary endpoint use. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} uses backing and redemption, liquidity, and venue reach to state the comparisons that would separate the competing explanations.

\subsection{Market integration}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

A widening trading network can reallocate activity across endpoint pairs without strengthening monetary coordination. As routers span more venues, previously fragmented pools become reachable, which may favor a stable intermediary already present across venues. The same expansion can also expose a direct path and eliminate the intermediary.

Cross-venue routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} of route count and reach \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of supported value. Over the same period, true intermediation falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} of economic routes in 2022 to \FullIntermediationEnd{} in 2026; on the balanced five-venue perimeter it falls to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{}. The corresponding changes are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Greater venue span has therefore coincided with less intermediation overall.

Among routes that remain indirect, stable share rises in every observed category defined by venue span and route complexity. The increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across venues than within one venue, with a \CrossVenueRotationSE{} standard error. Reweighting across these broad route categories cannot account for the stablecoin rotation, but each category still combines different endpoint pairs, trade sizes, feasible paths, and search conditions. Better search may therefore activate dispersed stablecoin liquidity within the same broad category.

Protocol launches do not resolve this alternative because their entry and exit windows also change the comparison set. Role appearance often coincides with entry and disappearance with exit, so the event coefficient partly reflects crossing zero. We therefore use protocol dates for chronology and leave the architecture effect unestimated. Separating search from vehicle coordination requires a transaction-state comparison within the same endpoint pair, size, and feasible route set.
