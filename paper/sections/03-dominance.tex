\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Stablecoins carry a larger share of exact two-leg routes in 2026 than in 2024, whether each route receives equal weight or is weighted by supported dollar value. Comparing the same days of the calendar year in the two years, their share among native and stable vehicles rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count. The paired-day change is \StableCountChange{}, with a calendar-HAC standard error of \StableCountSE{}. The value-weighted share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, a \StableValueChange{} increase with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. Each exact route $i\rightarrow k\rightarrow o$ identifies one intermediary, so the count measure requires only the observed token sequence. For the value measure, we retain routes whose source, intermediary, and destination values agree within 20\%; the tolerance allows for fees and slippage while excluding routes of ambiguous dollar size. Table~\ref{tab:rotation} reports both measures because frequent use need not coincide with the largest trades.

\subsection{Endpoint-pair composition}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

Stablecoin dominance can rise in several ways. New endpoint pairs may enter with stablecoin routes. Trading may shift toward continuing pairs where stablecoins are already important, or those pairs may route through an intermediary more often. Stablecoins may also replace the native asset within the same continuing market. Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} separates these changes and avoids attributing the aggregate share to the choice of one representative pair.

By route count, stablecoin share rises from \MarketBridgeBase{} in 2024 to \MarketBridgeEnd{} in 2026. Pairs entering or leaving the sample account for \MarketSupportBridge{} of the increase; shifts in trading across continuing pairs account for \MarketActivityReweight{}; and changes in how often those pairs use a vehicle account for \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}. The appearance or disappearance of a native-or-stable vehicle route contributes \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}, while direct stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use a vehicle in both years contributes only \WithinPairStableShare{}. Stablecoin share still rises from \ObservedBothYearsBase{} to \ObservedBothYearsEnd{} among pairs traded in both endpoint years, so pair turnover is incomplete. The value-weighted accounting reaches the same economic conclusion. Of the \PairValueTotal{} increase, \PairValueReweight{} comes from trading shifting across continuing pairs and \PairValueExclusive{} from pairs that carry a native-or-stable vehicle route in only one endpoint year; substitution within continuing pairs contributes \PairValueWithin{}. Reallocation across markets is the largest value margin, while repeated currency substitution inside a typical continuing market contributes little.

The pooled increase is nearly absent in a stricter comparison of like-for-like observations. Within observations that share the ordered endpoint pair, day of the calendar year, and single- versus cross-exchange route category, stablecoin share changes by \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketCountSE{}. The corresponding value estimate is \MatchedMarketValueChange{}, with a standard error of \MatchedMarketValueSE{}. Both confidence intervals include zero. Their upper endpoints, \MatchedMarketCountCIUpper{} by count and \MatchedMarketValueCIUpper{} by value, remain far below the corresponding aggregate increases. The matched estimates therefore rule out a within-market change large enough to explain the aggregate transition; they do not establish that the within-market effect is exactly zero. Explaining why activity moved across markets requires controls for trade size, liquidity, available routes, and router state.

The components describe different economic changes. New pairs can reflect listings or endpoint demand; trading can move across continuing pairs as demand or liquidity changes; and the use of an intermediary can respond to direct-market depth or to the routes a trading system can reach. Only the final component compares native and stable intermediaries within continuing vehicle-using pairs. Pooling these changes would make a shifting mix of markets look like currency substitution even when no continuing pair changes its vehicle. The accounting exactly divides the change among route components observed in the panel, including their interactions, but its population remains a subset of all trading activity. Explaining the reallocation requires comparisons that follow the same endpoint market while holding trade size and available routes alike.

\subsection{USDT and USDC}
\label{sec:dominance:excess}

A stable asset can become common because traders want to acquire or sell it, without becoming a vehicle between other assets. The excess-use ratio compares the asset's share of intermediary positions with its share of route endpoints on the same perimeter. A ratio above one means that the asset appears between other assets more often than its endpoint use would predict.

USDC and USDT jointly account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stable count share, but their paths differ. USDC begins the comparison with excess vehicle use and changes little. USDT's count ratio rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{}, while its strict-value ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. Its strict-value intermediary-minus-endpoint gap rises from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}, a \USDTEndpointGapChange{} change with a standard error of \USDTEndpointGapSE{}. USDT's intermediary use therefore grows faster than its endpoint use. These comparisons identify the token that drives the stablecoin rotation, but not the feature of USDT responsible for it.

The token-level split separates vehicle use from stablecoin adoption at the endpoints. If traders used more stablecoins only as sources and destinations, intermediary and endpoint shares would rise together and the share gap would remain unchanged. USDT instead gains in the middle of routes relative to its own endpoint presence, while USDC starts from a stronger vehicle position and contributes little to the change. USDT's relative intermediary use rises while USDC changes little, so the aggregate rotation is concentrated in the stablecoin whose initial vehicle position was weaker.

Reweighting from single- to cross-exchange routes accounts for \USDTVenueMixCountShare{} of USDT's count-share increase and \USDTVenueMixValueShare{} of its supported-value increase. The remaining \USDTVenueWithinCountShare{} by count and \USDTVenueWithinValueShare{} by value occurs within the two venue-scope categories. The decomposition conditions on observed venue span; the feasible alternatives available to the routing system remain unobserved.
% output/exhibits/e0_usdt_integration_decomposition.jsonl; route-only midpoint decomposition, 2024--2026

The concentration of stablecoin rotation in USDT makes institutional differences relevant without identifying which one is decisive. USDT and USDC differ in issuance, redemption access, venue coverage, and the distribution of liquidity across pools. Each feature could affect the cost of using the token between other assets, and each may respond to the same routed activity the paper measures. The route evidence establishes the magnitude and location of the transition before imposing a mechanism: a particular stablecoin becomes disproportionately common as an intermediary, and the change remains after benchmarking its ordinary endpoint use. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} uses backing and redemption, liquidity, and venue reach to state the comparisons that would separate the competing explanations.

\subsection{Venue span and route complexity}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

A widening trading network can reallocate activity across endpoint pairs without strengthening monetary coordination. As routers span more venues, previously fragmented pools become reachable, which may favor a stable intermediary already present across venues. The same expansion can also expose a direct path and eliminate the intermediary.

Cross-venue routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} of route count in 2022 to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} in 2026 and reach \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of supported value in 2026. Over the same years, true intermediation falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} to \FullIntermediationEnd{} of economic routes across the full venue set; on the balanced five-venue perimeter it falls to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{}. The corresponding changes are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Greater venue span has therefore coincided with less intermediation overall.

Among routes that remain indirect, stable share rises in every observed category defined by venue span and route complexity. The increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across venues than within one venue, with a \CrossVenueRotationSE{} standard error. Reweighting across these broad route categories cannot account for the stablecoin rotation, but each category still combines different endpoint pairs, trade sizes, feasible paths, and search conditions. Better search may therefore activate dispersed stablecoin liquidity within the same broad category.

Protocol dates establish chronology because entry and exit also change the comparison set. Role appearance often coincides with entry and disappearance with exit, so an event coefficient partly reflects crossing zero. Identifying an architecture effect requires a transaction-state comparison within the same endpoint pair, size, and feasible route set.
