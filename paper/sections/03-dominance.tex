\section{Rotation in the vehicle role}
\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{A route-level transition}
\label{sec:dominance:transition}

Each exact two-leg route $i\rightarrow k\rightarrow o$ contributes one observed intermediary. Count-weighted shares give every route equal weight. Value-weighted shares instead capture economic size and use the subset for which source, intermediary, and destination dollar values agree within 20\%. I report both because route frequency and routed value need not rank vehicle currencies in the same way.

Stablecoins gain more by supported value than by route count, and the annual series rules out a simple monotonic succession. Native intermediation rebounds in 2023 and 2024. Stablecoins recover the value lead in 2025, whereas their count lead remains short-lived. Count and value therefore describe different parts of the rotation; neither series yet establishes persistent replacement.

\subsection{Endpoint-pair composition}
\label{sec:dominance:pairs}

The aggregate share can rise when stablecoins gain within the same endpoint pairs, but it can also rise when the pairs represented in the data, their activity, or their use of an intermediary changes. Panel A of Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} separates these margins for pooled route count. The first two factors record whether a pair appears in the admitted route-component ledger and, for pairs observed in both endpoint years, whether it carries a native-or-stable vehicle route. For pairs carrying those routes in both years, the remaining factors record admitted route-component activity, realised vehicle incidence, and stable share among vehicle routes. A Shapley allocation distributes interaction terms across the five factors, so their contributions add exactly to the aggregate change.

The pooled stable share rises from \MarketBridgeBase{} in 2024 to \MarketBridgeEnd{} in 2026. Pairs observed in only one endpoint year contribute \MarketSupportBridge{}, and differences in vehicle-role support contribute \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}. Among pairs carrying native-or-stable vehicle routes in both years, activity reweighting contributes \MarketActivityReweight{}, realised vehicle incidence contributes \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}, and stable share within the pair contributes \WithinPairStableShare{}. The final term is small relative to the \MarketBridgeTotal{} aggregate increase. The count rotation is therefore spread across changes in observed pair representation, activity, and vehicle use, with little stable-for-native substitution on common-role pairs.

Panel B gives the complementary accounting for supported value. Stable share rises by \PairValueTotal{}. Changing activity weights among continuing vehicle-role pairs contribute \PairValueReweight{}, and pairs carrying native-or-stable vehicle routes in only one endpoint year contribute \PairValueExclusive{}. Changes in the relative mass of the two pair sets contribute \PairValueSupportMass{}, while stable share within continuing pairs contributes \PairValueWithin{}. Activity reallocation among continuing pairs is the largest value component, and within-pair substitution again contributes little.

The decomposition treats dominance as an allocation across markets, not as the choice of one representative endpoint pair. Pair representation is an extensive margin: it determines which markets enter the aggregate at all. Activity is an intensive margin: it determines how heavily the observed markets are weighted. Vehicle incidence then records whether those routes use an intermediary, and the final share records which currency fills that role. Conflating these margins would make a change in the set of traded pairs look like stable-for-native substitution even if no continuing pair changed its preferred vehicle.

The observed-both-year comparison shows that pair turnover is important but not the whole result. Stable share rises from \ObservedBothYearsBase{} to \ObservedBothYearsEnd{} among pairs represented in both endpoint years, a change of \ObservedBothYearsTotal{}. Within that comparison, admitted activity and realised vehicle incidence account for much more of the aggregate rise than stable share conditional on a common vehicle role. The economic change is therefore not confined to the arrival of new endpoint markets. Existing markets also become differently weighted and route indirectly at different rates, even though direct stable-for-native replacement within common-role pairs remains small.

These margins describe different equilibrium objects. Pair representation can respond to listing and endpoint demand; activity weights can respond to trading demand or liquidity; vehicle incidence can respond to direct-market depth and router reach; conditional stable share can respond to the relative attractiveness of the intermediary currencies themselves. The decomposition shows where the aggregate movement enters, but it does not show why a pair appears in the ledger, why its activity changes, or why an observed route uses an intermediary. Distinguishing among those explanations requires a comparison that holds the available route opportunity fixed.

\label{sec:dominance:excess}

A stable asset can become common because traders want to acquire or sell it, without becoming a vehicle between other assets. The excess-use ratio compares the asset's share of intermediary positions with its share of route endpoints on the same perimeter. A ratio above one means that the asset appears between other assets more often than its endpoint use would predict.

USDC and USDT jointly account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stable count share, but their paths differ. USDC begins the comparison with excess vehicle use and changes little. USDT's count ratio rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{}, while its strict-value ratio moves from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. Its strict-value intermediary-minus-endpoint gap rises from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}, a \USDTEndpointGapChange{} change with a standard error of \USDTEndpointGapSE{}. USDT's intermediary use therefore grows faster than its endpoint use. These comparisons identify the token that drives the stablecoin rotation, but not the feature of USDT responsible for it.

The token-level split rules out an interpretation based on stablecoin adoption alone. If traders simply used more stablecoins as sources and destinations, intermediary and endpoint shares would rise together and the share gap would remain unchanged. USDT instead gains in the middle of routes relative to its own endpoint presence, while USDC starts from a stronger vehicle position and contributes little to the change. The aggregate result is therefore not a common movement of two dollar-pegged assets in vehicle use. It is a change in the division of roles within the stablecoin category, concentrated in the currency whose relative intermediary use was initially lower.

That concentration makes institutional differences relevant without identifying which one is decisive. USDT and USDC differ in issuance, redemption access, venue coverage, and the distribution of liquidity across pools. Each feature could affect the cost of using the token between other assets, and each may respond to the same routed activity the paper measures. The route evidence establishes the magnitude and location of the transition before imposing a mechanism: a particular stablecoin becomes disproportionately common as an intermediary, and the change remains after benchmarking its ordinary endpoint use. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} uses backing and redemption, liquidity, and venue reach to state the comparisons that would separate the competing explanations.

\subsection{Market integration}
\label{sec:dominance:integration}

A widening trading network can reallocate activity across endpoint pairs without strengthening monetary coordination. As routers span more venues, previously fragmented pools become reachable, which may favor a stable intermediary already present across venues. The same expansion can also expose a direct path and eliminate the intermediary.

Cross-venue routes grow from \CrossVenueCountStart{} to \CrossVenueCountEnd{} of route count and reach \CrossVenueValueEnd{} of supported value. Over the same period, true intermediation falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} of economic routes in 2022 to \FullIntermediationEnd{} in 2026; on the balanced five-venue perimeter it falls to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{}. The corresponding changes are \FullIntermediationChange{} and \BalancedIntermediationChange{}, with standard errors of \FullIntermediationSE{} and \BalancedIntermediationSE{}. Greater venue span has therefore coincided with less intermediation overall.

Among routes that remain indirect, stable share rises in every observed cell defined by venue span and route complexity. The increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across venues than within one venue, with a \CrossVenueRotationSE{} standard error. Reweighting across these broad cells cannot account for the stablecoin rotation, but a cell does not hold fixed the exact endpoint pair, trade size, feasible paths, or search conditions. Better search may still activate dispersed stablecoin liquidity within a cell.

Protocol launches do not resolve this alternative because their entry and exit windows also change the comparison set. Role appearance often coincides with entry and disappearance with exit, so the event coefficient partly reflects crossing zero. I therefore use protocol dates for chronology and leave the architecture effect unestimated. Separating search from vehicle coordination requires a transaction-state comparison within the same endpoint pair, size, and feasible route set.
