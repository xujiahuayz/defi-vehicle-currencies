\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Vehicle currencies are usually observed through aggregate turnover, quotation patterns, or prices. Realised routes reveal the role directly. They also reveal why vehicle status and vehicle dominance are different objects: many assets can appear in the middle of a trade, while only a few carry a large share of those choices.

In decentralised exchange, vehicle use rotates sharply but not monotonically from the native asset toward fiat-backed stablecoins. Stablecoins lead by routed value in 2022, lose that lead in 2023 and 2024, and regain it in 2025. Their count share moves later. Within the stablecoin category, USDT accounts for the principal increase in intermediary use relative to its endpoint use.

The endpoint-pair results change the interpretation of the aggregate rotation. By count, \PairPooledExclusive{} of the \PairPooledTotal{} increase is associated with pairs carrying native-or-stable vehicle routes in only one endpoint year, and \PairPooledReweight{} with changing activity weights among continuing pairs. Stable shares within those pairs barely change. For supported value, changes in the weights of continuing pairs are the largest component. Vehicle leadership therefore changes mainly through a reallocation across endpoint markets. Repeated local substitution within fixed pairs contributes little.

The five-factor accounting gives this reallocation an economic structure. Some endpoint pairs are represented in only one endpoint year; continuing pairs receive different activity weights; routes on those pairs become more or less likely to use any vehicle; and the vehicle routes that remain divide between native and stable intermediaries. The first three margins concern which markets are active and how their trades are routed. The last margin is closest to direct currency substitution within a fixed market, and it is small. Stablecoin dominance thus rises mainly because the market places more weight on settings in which stable intermediation is used, not because a typical continuing pair repeatedly replaces the native asset with a stablecoin.

That distinction matters for the economics of a vehicle currency. A vehicle can gain aggregate share through broader market representation, heavier activity in markets where it is already used, or greater intermediary incidence on those markets. Each channel can reinforce future liquidity and software support even when the preferred intermediary within a fixed pair changes little. The observed-both-year increase shows that pair entry alone is insufficient, while the small common-role substitution term shows that a representative-pair story is also insufficient. The evidence therefore points toward an allocation across markets as the object to explain.

This pattern narrows the set of plausible accounts but does not select among them. Pair turnover can reflect endpoint demand, route feasibility, venue entry, liquidity placement, or data coverage. Activity weights among continuing pairs can respond to both demand and changes in accessible liquidity. The rotation appears separately among single- and cross-venue routes, but those comparisons do not fix trade size, feasible alternatives, or search quality. The route evidence establishes a change in vehicle leadership and where that change occurs. Identifying its cause requires the opportunity set to be held fixed.
