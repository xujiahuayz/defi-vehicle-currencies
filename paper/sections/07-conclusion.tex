\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Vehicle currencies are usually observed through aggregate turnover, quotation patterns, or prices. Realised routes reveal the role directly. They also reveal why vehicle status and vehicle dominance are different objects: many assets can appear in the middle of a trade, while only a few carry a large share of those choices.

In decentralised exchange, vehicle use rotates sharply but not monotonically from the native asset toward fiat-backed stablecoins. Stablecoins lead by routed value in 2022, lose that lead in 2023 and 2024, and regain it in 2025. Their count share moves later. Within the stablecoin category, USDT accounts for the principal increase in intermediary use relative to its endpoint use.

The endpoint-pair results change the interpretation of this rotation. Pairs that enter or leave the sample and shifts in trading across continuing pairs account for most of the increase. Stablecoin use also becomes more common among pairs traded in both 2024 and 2026, while direct stable-for-native substitution within continuing vehicle-using pairs is small. In the tighter matched-market comparison, stablecoin share changes by only \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count and by \MatchedMarketValueChange{} by value. Stablecoin dominance therefore grows mainly through a reallocation toward markets that use stable intermediation and a greater incidence of intermediation within those markets; repeated currency switching in a typical continuing market contributes little.

The reallocation across endpoint markets broadens the usual account of vehicle-currency formation. Theories built around repeated choice within a fixed market emphasize how lower trading costs reinforce an incumbent. The route evidence shows that dominance also changes through the entry and exit of endpoint pairs, the distribution of trading across continuing pairs, and the decision to route indirectly. These changes can deepen the pools and expand the software support associated with a currency even when the preferred intermediary within an existing market changes little. A theory of vehicle dominance must therefore explain how currencies become embedded across markets as well as how traders choose among them within a market.

The current evidence locates the rotation in a USDT-centred reorganisation of routed markets. Backing, redemption, liquidity, venue reach, endpoint demand, and routing software remain competing explanations. Distinguishing them requires capital and provider flows measured independently of realised route use and settlement comparisons that match routes on their economic sequence and trade size.
