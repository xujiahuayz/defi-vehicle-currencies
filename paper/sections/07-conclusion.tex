\section{Discussion and policy implications}
\label{sec:discussion}

We group stablecoins by their shared vehicle function while retaining the token that supplies each route. USDC, USDT, DAI, and other stablecoins can target the same unit of account while differing in issuer, backing, redemption, peg risk, and local pool liquidity. The family-level result measures the rise of tokenised-dollar intermediation; the issuer-level estimates show how that role is allocated within the family. Stable-to-stable endpoint pairs make the distinction especially visible because USDT supplies most of their increase in routed value.

A one-for-one peg aligns prices while preserving currency identity. The Bahamian dollar, for example, is conventionally pegged at one Bahamian dollar per U.S. dollar and remains a separately issued currency with its own monetary and exchange-control arrangements \citep{IMF2025BahamasArticleIV}. The same distinction disciplines our stablecoin aggregation. A currency-equivalent measure would consolidate dollar-pegged instruments by unit of account; our route measure instead records competition among separately issued dollar instruments and between that family and WETH.

This distinction may also affect liquidity supply. While two stablecoins hold their pegs, their relative price typically varies less than either token's price against WETH. In a constant-product pool, smaller relative-price movements imply smaller loss versus holding, commonly called impermanent or divergence loss. That risk profile may make stablecoin--stablecoin links attractive to liquidity providers and help tokenised dollars build dense cross-issuer markets. Peg stress reverses the comparison. Impermanent loss remains distinct from loss-versus-rebalancing, the running adverse-selection cost of stale \ac{amm} prices \citep{MilionisMoallemiRoughgardenZhang2022LVR}. Deposited capital reveals the pool stock; provider entry prices, fee income, hedges, and expectations determine profitability. We therefore use this channel as an economic interpretation of pool formation.

Vehicle use connects otherwise unrelated pairs to the liquidity and issuer conditions of the asset in the middle. This concentration can lower trading costs through deeper two-leg markets while transmitting a disruption in those markets across many endpoint pairs. The stablecoin stress comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-composition} shows that issuer identity can change quickly during a reserve-confidence event; the pair-entry estimates show why established relationships may adjust more slowly. We interpret these patterns as market-structure implications of observed route allocation. Welfare effects require additional evidence on user surplus, provider returns, and the incidence of stablecoin risk.

Payment-system design faces the same extensive margin. Nexus assigns foreign-exchange conversion to a provider, and Rialto uses a third currency when direct exchange is unavailable \citep{BISNexusFX2024,BISRialto2025}. The route evidence suggests that access policy affects more than the price quoted inside an existing relationship. It also affects which two-sided markets form early enough and deeply enough to become the bridge for new relationships.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Stablecoin vehicle dominance rises sharply on decentralised exchanges. Between matched January--June periods in 2024 and 2026, the equal-day stablecoin share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count and from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{} by routed value. In the pooled decomposition, gross substitutions offset inside continuing pairs; trading reallocation and period-specific pairs carry the rotation. Longer routes show the same movement.

The vehicle used at pair entry predicts later use. First-month stablecoin use rises from \BridgeTimingShallowMonthShare{} below one-tenth of WETH depth to \BridgeTimingCompetitiveMonthShare{} once that threshold is reached; capital on the weaker leg builds before first use. Opening all exact venues finds a better same-vehicle quote on \ExactFrontierSameVehicleShare{} of common-support routes. Adding other vehicles and the direct path raises that incidence by \ExactFrontierVehicleAddition{} and lowers stablecoin vehicle share by \ExactFrontierStableDecline{}.

Aggregate dominance combines use within continuing relationships with the vehicle identity of new ones. The large stablecoin rotation comes mainly from where trading grows and which pairs appear. Early vehicle use and two-sided depth tie new relationships to the markets around them. Currency competition therefore operates both inside established relationships and when new relationships form the markets that later support them.
