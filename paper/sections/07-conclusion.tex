\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Vehicle currencies are usually observed through aggregate turnover, quotation patterns, or prices. Realised routes reveal the role directly. They also reveal why vehicle status and vehicle dominance are different objects: many assets can appear in the middle of a trade, while only a few carry a large share of those choices.

In decentralised exchange, vehicle use rotates sharply but not monotonically from the native asset toward fiat-backed stablecoins. Stablecoins lead by routed value in 2022, lose that lead in 2023 and 2024, and regain it in 2025. Their count share moves later. Within the stablecoin category, USDT accounts for the principal increase in intermediary use relative to its endpoint use.

The endpoint-pair results change the interpretation of this rotation. Pairs that enter or leave the sample and shifts in trading across continuing pairs account for most of the increase. Stablecoin use also becomes more common among pairs traded in both 2024 and 2026, but direct stable-for-native substitution within continuing vehicle-using pairs is small. Stablecoin dominance therefore grows mainly because trading moves toward markets where stable intermediation is used and because those markets use intermediaries more often, not because a typical continuing market repeatedly switches currencies.

This finding broadens the usual account of vehicle-currency formation. Theories built around repeated choice within a fixed market emphasize how lower trading costs reinforce an incumbent. The route evidence shows that dominance also changes through the creation of markets, the distribution of trading across them, and the decision to route indirectly. These changes can deepen the pools and expand the software support associated with a currency even when the preferred intermediary within an existing market changes little. A theory of vehicle dominance must therefore explain how currencies become embedded across markets as well as how traders choose among them within a market.

The mechanism evidence is suggestive and leaves several explanations open. Vehicle use and liquidity concentration predict one another, which is consistent with the feedback at the centre of vehicle-currency theory but also with common changes in demand or volatility. V4 shows that architecture matters on a separate margin: settlement netting can remove the physical transfer of an intermediary without removing its economic role in the route. USDT and USDC still differ in backing, redemption, liquidity, and venue reach, while endpoint demand and routing software can move the same markets. The central result is therefore not a claim that one mechanism explains the transition. Stablecoin intermediation rises through a reorganisation of routed markets concentrated in USDT, and the evidence begins to show how liquidity and settlement design can support that organisation.
