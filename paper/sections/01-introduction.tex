\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vehicle currency is traded not because it is one side of the desired payment, but because it is the market's preferred way to move between two other currencies. This role is highly concentrated in foreign exchange. Theories of vehicle currencies attribute that concentration to a feedback between use and transaction cost: routing more trades through one currency deepens its markets, and deeper markets make it attractive to route still more trades through it \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies}. Monetary-search models generate a related coordination force because accepting an intermediary object is valuable when others are expected to accept it later \citep{KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium}. Modern dominant-currency models carry these complementarities into invoicing, safe-asset demand, and balance sheets \citep{GopinathEtAl2020DCP,GopinathStein2021Making,Mukhin2022InternationalPriceSystem}.

The empirical obstacle is that the vehicle route is usually latent. Foreign-exchange records reveal turnover and prices, but not the market-wide path a payment took or the paths it could have taken. A currency's international role can therefore be measured by the number of markets quoting it \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}, or inferred from price and volume responses \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}, but its realised use as the middle asset on individual routes is not generally observed. This makes the central transition difficult to study. A dominant currency can lose transaction share without losing its intermediary role, and a new currency can gain endpoint demand without becoming the object others trade through.

This paper measures that role in decentralised exchange. A swap transaction records an ordered path through a graph of automated markets. When a route exchanges asset $i$ for asset $o$ through asset $k$, the intermediary is observed directly. The same record identifies the endpoints, so vehicle use can be separated from ordinary demand for $k$ as a source or destination. The setting also follows one currency system from an architecture that required native-asset intermediation to designs in which arbitrary pairs and competing routes are available. That history makes vehicle choice observable. It does not make protocol date, or calendar time itself, a treatment.

The primary unit is an exact two-leg route, which gives each realised vehicle choice one intermediary and one vote. Vehicle dominance is the share of these choices carried by an asset or asset class. I also construct a vehicle excess-use ratio: an asset's intermediary share divided by its endpoint share on the same route perimeter. A value above one means that the asset appears in the middle of trades more often than its direct payment demand would predict. This distinction is important in a market where stablecoins can become popular endpoints at the same time that they become useful bridges between other assets.

The first result is a sharp rotation toward stable num\'eraires. On calendar days observed in both endpoint years, the equal-weighted stable share among native and stable vehicles rises from \StableCountBase{} in 2024 to \StableCountEnd{} in 2026, a \StableCountChange{} change with a calendar-HAC standard error of \StableCountSE{}. On value supported within 20\% of the route's input value, the stable share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, a \StableValueChange{} change with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. The frequency and value margins therefore agree on the direction and disagree on how far the transition has progressed.

This is a late rotation, not a monotone trend in time. Native intermediation rebounds in 2023 and 2024. Stable value regains the lead from 2025-Q1, while stable count leadership is not sustained over a long post-transition window. The distinction rules out an interpretation in which the coefficient on a date trend stands in for a design change. The relevant scientific question is whether a particular opportunity set, routing technology, or liquidity state changed and whether vehicle use reverses when that state reverses.

The second result locates the transition within the stable category. USDC and USDT account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stable count share. USDC is already used disproportionately as a vehicle and changes little across the endpoint years. USDT drives the shift: its count excess-use ratio rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{}, and its strict-value ratio rises from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{}. Its strict-value intermediary-minus-endpoint share moves from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}. The result is therefore not a generic property shared by stablecoin designs. Fiat-reserve, on-chain-collateralised, and synthetic claims remain distinct candidate mechanisms.

The strongest rival is market maturation. Cross-venue execution expands rapidly over the sample, and an increasingly integrated router can change which liquidity is reachable even when no monetary coordination changes. Two facts prevent that rival from explaining the rotation mechanically. First, the share of economic routes using a true intermediary falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} in 2022 to \FullIntermediationEnd{} in 2026 on the full perimeter, and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} on a balanced five-venue perimeter. Integration expands while indirect routing contracts. Second, the stable rotation survives every observed integration-by-complexity count cell and is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across venues than within one venue, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}. These findings reject route integration and leg-count composition as complete explanations.

They do not dispose of routing technology. Better search can activate fragmented stable liquidity and make a stable asset more useful as an intermediary. In that case routing is part of the mechanism, not a nuisance. The decisive specification conditions vehicle choice on fixed pair, size, opportunity, and search-efficiency cells. If the stable rebound disappears there, the headline is a routing-technology transition. If it survives, liquidity composition and monetary coordination remain live. The final transaction-state frontier needed for that test is still under construction, so the paper states the falsifier before assigning the mechanism.

Architecture changes provide a second tempting design and a useful negative result. Realised use of the newest protocol design enters and exits vehicle cells, but every candidate substitution window currently fails the pre-specified isolated, complete, fixed-comparison-set contract. Role appearances and disappearances are sparse lifecycle endpoints, and their large changes are mechanical because the event is defined by crossing zero. I report that support failure without treating protocol adoption as exogenous or manufacturing a coefficient from calendar coincidence. A valid reversal design requires an ex ante feasible risk set and a state change that can turn in both directions.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it observes the vehicle on each realised route and therefore separates vehicle status, which is binary on a route, from vehicle dominance, which is a continuous market share. The distinction turns the making of a vehicle currency from a quotation or aggregate-turnover object into a sequence of realised intermediary choices.

Second, the vehicle excess-use ratio nets out endpoint demand directly. It operationalises the idea that a vehicle carries more transactions than bilateral payment needs alone would justify, without requiring an exclusion restriction or a structural model. The ratio also disciplines the stablecoin interpretation: growth in stablecoin payments is not enough; the asset must be used disproportionately between other endpoints.

Third, the evidence separates routing integration from intermediation. More cross-venue reach need not produce more indirect trade, and the same technology can either bypass a vehicle or activate dispersed liquidity around it. This distinction connects vehicle-currency theory to the market-design literature on fragmented trading and automated liquidity \citep{MakarovSchoar2022DeFi,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}. It also provides a falsifiable alternative to reading a six-year chronology as a causal time trend.

Two prominent quantities are absent from these contributions. Earlier incidence estimates for cost-dominated routes are retired pending the final exact-transaction state and gas-price rebuild. The preliminary turnover hazard has not been estimated on the final pair-candidate panel with candidate-count and fixed-effect controls. Those exercises can change the mechanism and persistence sections, but they are not needed to establish the route-level rotation documented here.
