\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The U.S. dollar appears on more than 85\% of foreign-exchange transactions, far more often than the United States' share of cross-border economic activity would suggest \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. Its use as a vehicle between other currencies helps explain this prominence. Vehicle use concentrates trading in dollar markets; deeper markets lower trading costs; and lower costs reinforce the dollar's intermediary role \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. This feedback explains why an established vehicle can persist. It says less about how dominance changes when new markets and trading technologies alter the routes available to traders.

Testing how dominance changes requires observing the full trading path. Standard foreign-exchange records report turnover by currency pair, not whether two reported trades belong to the same underlying exchange.\footnote{For example, see the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} A yen--euro exchange routed through dollars therefore appears as turnover in the yen--dollar and dollar--euro markets, with no link between the two legs. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} overcomes this limitation by using pair-level volume responses around non-overlapping holidays to infer vehicle trading. We take a complementary approach in a market where transaction logs preserve the sequence of pools and assets used in each exchange.

Decentralised exchanges split liquidity across smart-contract pools that a router can combine within one transaction. We reconstruct those combinations from 472 million swaps on eight Ethereum \ac{amm} deployments between February 2020 and June 2026. The reconstruction identifies the first and last assets in the observed pool sequence, every intermediary, and any movement across exchanges. It thus separates intermediary use from use at either end of the observed route. These pool-route endpoints need not coincide with the user's original instruction, a distinction we document with a transaction in Section~\ref{sec:setting}. This route-based measure complements measures of quoting, invoicing, and funding, which describe related but different currency choices \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,GopinathEtAl2020DCP,GopinathStein2021Making}.

Stablecoins become substantially more prominent vehicles during the sample, but their rise is not monotone. Comparing the same January-through-June dates in 2024 and 2026, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count and from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{} by dollar-weighted volume. Native assets regain share in 2023 and 2024; stablecoins recover the value lead in 2025; and their lead by route count is temporary. The change is especially pronounced for USDT, whose intermediary use rises markedly relative to its appearance at observed route endpoints.

The allocation of trading across source--destination pairs accounts for most of this rotation. Some pairs appear in only one endpoint year, and activity shifts sharply across pairs that remain. Among continuing pairs with comparable realised routes, direct stable-for-native substitution is much smaller: its confidence bound remains far below the aggregate increase. The same conclusion appears in the value decomposition. Stablecoins gain chiefly because a larger share of trading occurs in pairs that route through them. The typical continuing pair rarely replaces a native intermediary with a stablecoin. This pattern survives separate comparisons of single-exchange and cross-exchange routes. Cross-exchange routing grows rapidly, but stablecoin share rises in both groups.

This reallocation broadens the empirical question posed by vehicle-currency theory. Classical models ask which intermediary minimises the cost of exchanging a given pair of assets. Their volume--cost feedback explains why trading converges on a common vehicle and why an established vehicle persists \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies}. Our decomposition shows a second margin in the observed data: the distribution of routed activity across source--destination pairs. A vehicle can gain aggregate share as activity moves toward pairs routed through it even when intermediary choice changes little within matched pairs.

The distinction disciplines the search for mechanisms. Relative execution costs and routing software can change the intermediary selected for a given pair. Liquidity supply, asset risk, and protocol design can change which routes are feasible and where activity occurs. These forces may reinforce one another, but they imply different comparisons in the data. The route decomposition establishes where the stablecoin gain occurs. A mechanism test must explain that allocation; observed pair reallocation does not by itself measure market formation.

Our first contribution is direct measurement of a currency role that is usually inferred from aggregate turnover. Because transaction sequences link the legs of an exchange, they separate intermediary use from use at the observed route endpoints without an exclusion restriction for latent vehicle trades. This measurement complements the holiday-based inference of \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} and the historical turnover evidence of \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. It also lets us follow the same role across protocols and routes that span several exchanges.

Our second contribution is to show that aggregate vehicle dominance depends on the allocation of routed activity across observed source--destination pairs as well as intermediary choice within matched pairs. Theories of vehicle currencies emphasise the self-reinforcing depth of markets joined by a common intermediary. Research on fragmented trading and automated market making shows how market design and liquidity supply shape available trading opportunities \citep{ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}. The decomposition connects these literatures by identifying the empirical margin that prospective formation and liquidity tests must explain. In our setting, reallocation across pairs accounts for most of the stablecoin rotation; widespread replacement within matched pairs does not.

Section~\ref{sec:dominance} documents the rotation toward stablecoins and separates changes across pairs from substitution within matched pairs. Section~\ref{sec:executability} defines the counterfactual cost benchmark and its current coverage boundary. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} uses the route evidence to specify tests of liquidity supply, router reach, asset risk, and protocol design.
