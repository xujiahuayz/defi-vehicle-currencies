\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The U.S. dollar appears on more than 85\% of foreign-exchange transactions, far more often than the United States' share of cross-border economic activity would suggest \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. Its use as a vehicle between other currencies helps explain this prominence. Vehicle use concentrates trading in dollar markets; deeper markets lower trading costs; and lower costs reinforce the dollar's intermediary role \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. This feedback explains why an established vehicle can persist. It says less about how dominance changes when new markets and trading technologies alter the routes available to traders.

Answering that question requires the route used for an exchange, which standard foreign-exchange records generally do not report.\footnote{For example, see the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} A yen--euro exchange routed through dollars appears as turnover in the yen--dollar and dollar--euro markets, without a link between the two legs. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} uses pair-level volume responses around non-overlapping holidays to infer vehicle trading. We study a market in which ordered admitted pool calls reveal the exchange component.

Decentralised exchanges record pool calls in their execution order within a transaction. From 472 million swap records on eight Ethereum \ac{amm} deployments between February 2020 and June 2026, we recover the asset sold, the asset bought, and every intermediary between them, including routes that cross exchanges. We measure vehicle dominance from an asset's share of intermediary use and compare that share with its presence at the endpoints, where it is bought or sold. Observing both roles separates a growing intermediary role from a general increase in trading in the asset. Our sample spans the expansion of stablecoin markets, cross-exchange routing, and protocols that changed which assets could trade directly. The resulting path-based measure complements measures of quoting, invoicing, and funding, each of which records a different economic decision \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,GopinathEtAl2020DCP,GopinathStein2021Making}.

Stablecoins become substantially more prominent vehicles during the sample. Comparing the same January-through-June dates in 2024 and 2026, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count and from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{} by supported routed value. The path reverses twice: native assets regain share in 2023 and 2024, stablecoins recover the value lead in 2025, and their lead by route count is temporary. For USDT, intermediary use rises markedly relative to endpoint use.

The main change is in the allocation of trading across ordered source--destination asset pairs. Changes in which pairs trade, shifts in activity across continuing pairs, and changes in the incidence of indirect execution account for most of the aggregate increase. Stable-for-native substitution is much smaller among continuing pairs with the same route scope: its confidence bound remains far below the aggregate increase, even though stablecoin share rises among pairs observed in both endpoint years. Cross-exchange routing becomes more common over the same period, while the fraction of routes using any intermediary falls. Stablecoin share nonetheless rises among the indirect routes that remain, whether they use one exchange or cross several. The rotation therefore includes a within-group reallocation toward source--destination pairs that use stablecoins as intermediaries.

This allocation margin changes the empirical question posed by vehicle-currency theory. An explanation for the transition must account for why trading moved toward source--destination pairs connected through stablecoins and why continuing pairs changed their use of indirect execution. Relative execution costs, liquidity allocation, asset risk, routing software, and protocol design can all affect those choices. The route evidence locates the change in dominance; the mechanism tests ask what moved trading across pairs.

Our first contribution is to observe a currency role that is usually inferred from aggregate turnover. Transaction sequences distinguish intermediary use from endpoint demand without an exclusion restriction for latent vehicle trades. The second is to identify a margin that pair-level turnover conceals. Classical vehicle-currency mechanisms emphasise the reinforcing depth of the two markets joined by a vehicle; our evidence shows that aggregate dominance also changes when trading reallocates across source--destination pairs that use different intermediaries. This finding connects vehicle-currency theory to the formation of liquid markets, fragmented execution, and the design of trading protocols \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}.

The remainder of the paper develops the evidence in this order. Section~\ref{sec:setting} describes the exchanges, reconstructs transaction-level routes, and defines vehicle dominance. Section~\ref{sec:dominance} documents the rotation toward stablecoins and locates it across pairs, tokens, and route scopes. Sections~\ref{sec:executability} and~\ref{sec:rivals} set out the evidence needed to distinguish relative costs, liquidity, and market design as explanations for the transition.
