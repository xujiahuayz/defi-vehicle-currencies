\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Foreign-exchange trading is concentrated in a few currencies, above all the US dollar, whose international roles have proved persistent \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. Models attribute this pattern to self-reinforcing liquidity and coordination: a currency that intermediates more trades develops deeper markets and becomes more useful in subsequent exchange \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium}. Studying these mechanisms requires observing the currencies used along the complete trading path, such as a yen--euro exchange executed through dollars. The BIS Triennial Survey reports foreign-exchange turnover by currency pair, not the complete sequence of markets used for an underlying exchange.\footnote{See the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} Pair-level turnover therefore does not directly reveal whether volume in a dollar pair reflects demand for dollars or the dollar's use to exchange two nondollar currencies. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} estimates this distinction using non-overlapping holidays. Decentralised-exchange transaction logs instead record the ordered markets used for execution. We assemble these logs for eight Ethereum automated-market-maker deployments from 11 February 2020 through 30 June 2026. The resulting sequences identify the assets ultimately exchanged and any intermediary.

For exact two-leg routes, vehicle dominance is an intermediary's share of routes, with each realised route receiving equal weight. We also compare an asset's intermediary share with its frequency at either end of the same transactions. This comparison shows whether the asset appears disproportionately between other assets relative to its use as the asset bought or sold. Complete trading paths isolate this medium-of-exchange role; measures based on quoting markets, aggregate price and volume responses, invoicing, or funding capture different currency roles \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}.

Market design changed substantially over the period we study. Uniswap v1 required exchanges between two tokens to pass through ETH, whereas v2 allowed arbitrary ERC20 pairs; v3 subsequently introduced concentrated liquidity and v4 a singleton settlement architecture \citep{LeharParlour2024Uniswap,AdamsZinsmeisterRobinson2021UniswapV3}. Uniswap v1 is a separate institutional comparison because its exchange-to-token identities cannot yet be joined to the eight-venue route panel. The route panel therefore measures intermediary choice after arbitrary pairs became possible; it does not place the v1 mandate and later designs in one continuous route sample. Protocol launches also coincide with changes in users, liquidity, and available pairs, so calendar time alone cannot identify the effect of architecture.

Stablecoins gain vehicle share relative to native assets, although the path is not monotonic. Comparing the same days of the calendar year in 2024 and 2026, stablecoins' equal-weighted share among routes intermediated by native assets or stablecoins rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{}. The change is \StableCountChange{}, with a calendar-HAC standard error of \StableCountSE{}. Among routes whose source, intermediary, and destination values agree within 20\%, the stablecoin share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, a \StableValueChange{} increase with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. The longer time series contains reversals: native-asset intermediation rebounds in 2023 and 2024, and stablecoins recover the value lead in 2025 after losing it. Their lead by route count is temporary. These reversals leave the persistence of the 2026 value lead unresolved.

Most of the aggregate shift arises across endpoint markets, not from substitution within a continuing market. A five-part decomposition of the \MarketBridgeTotal{} increase assigns \MarketSupportBridge{} to pair entry and exit, \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{} to continuing pairs that gain or lose native-or-stable intermediation, \MarketActivityReweight{} to changes in activity across continuing pairs, \VehicleIncidenceReweight{} to changes in the frequency of indirect execution within continuing pairs, and \WithinPairStableShare{} to stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use an intermediary in both years. Stablecoin share nevertheless rises from \ObservedBothYearsBase{} to \ObservedBothYearsEnd{} among pairs present in both 2024 and 2026, so pair entry and exit do not explain the full increase. Within observations that also share the ordered endpoint pair and single- versus cross-exchange route category, stablecoin share changes by only \MatchedMarketCountChange{} by count (standard error \MatchedMarketCountSE{}) and by \MatchedMarketValueChange{} by value (standard error \MatchedMarketValueSE{}). The 95\% confidence intervals are [\MatchedMarketCountCILower{}, \MatchedMarketCountCIUpper{}] and [\MatchedMarketValueCILower{}, \MatchedMarketValueCIUpper{}]; their upper endpoints are far below the aggregate changes. Together, the decomposition and matched estimates locate the aggregate increase in which pairs trade, where routed activity occurs, and how often continuing pairs use indirect execution. Replacement of native assets by stablecoins within continuing markets contributes little.

USDT accounts for most of the increase within the stablecoin category. USDC begins with a high intermediary share relative to its endpoint share and changes little. The ratio of USDT's intermediary share to its endpoint share rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{} by count and from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{} by value. Consistently, its value-weighted intermediary share minus endpoint share moves from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}. These comparisons identify USDT as the principal contributor to the stablecoin increase, but do not identify why its intermediary use grows.

Router access to several exchanges is a plausible alternative to substitution between vehicles within one endpoint market. Cross-exchange execution becomes more common during the sample even as indirect execution becomes less common overall. The share of economic routes that use an intermediary falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} in 2022 to \FullIntermediationEnd{} in 2026 across all covered exchanges and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} on a constant set of five exchanges. Among indirect routes, stablecoin share rises for each observed combination of one versus multiple exchanges and route length. The increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger on cross-exchange routes than on single-exchange routes, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}. The aggregate increase is therefore not generated solely by a shift toward cross-exchange or longer routes. These comparisons do not hold the endpoints, trade size, available routes, or contemporaneous market state fixed, so they do not isolate the effect of venue access or router search.

Models of vehicle currencies emphasise coordination, liquidity, and persistence, while research on fragmented trading studies how execution depends on venue access and market design \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,GopinathStein2021Making,LeharParlour2024Uniswap,ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation}. Liquidity allocation is one possible explanation for the observed reallocation. We test whether pool capital and provider flows precede vehicle use, and whether vehicle use precedes subsequent capital allocation. These lead--lag relations establish timing, not causal feedback. Uniswap V4 raises a different question. Its settlement design may allow an economic route to retain an intermediary even when the contract records no transfer of that token. We compare routes with the same assets bought and sold, intermediary, direction, and trade size to test whether settlement changes the transfers used to implement an otherwise comparable route.
