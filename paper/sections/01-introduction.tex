\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vehicle currency can dominate trading even when no party wants to hold it. It is acquired only to be exchanged again because two liquid markets through the vehicle can outperform a thin direct market. The U.S. dollar appears on more than 85\% of foreign-exchange transactions, far more often than the United States' share of cross-border economic activity would suggest \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. Vehicle use concentrates trading in dollar markets; deeper markets lower trading costs and reinforce the dollar's intermediary role \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. This feedback explains persistence after a vehicle becomes dominant. It says less about how a challenger gains share as new trading relationships and market technologies appear.
% literature/text/2026-Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX-dollar-dominance-in-fx-trading.txt; dollar involvement exceeds 85 percent

Aggregate dominance combines two margins. A continuing relationship can replace its vehicle, or activity can move toward relationships that already use another vehicle and toward new relationships formed around it. The same aggregate change can therefore arise with pervasive switching or with no net switching inside established relationships. Separating these cases requires transactions that attach the intermediary to the exchange between its source and destination.

Standard foreign-exchange records report turnover in each currency pair without linking the legs of the customer's exchange.\footnote{For example, see the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} A yen--euro exchange routed through dollars appears as separate yen--dollar and dollar--euro trades. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} infers vehicle activity from currency-pair volume responses around non-overlapping holidays. In decentralised exchange, the pool calls inside one blockchain transaction preserve their execution order. We use those records to observe the connected route directly.

We call the ordered source and destination the \emph{endpoint pair}, shortened hereafter to \emph{pair}. Each directed pool execution is a \emph{leg}; the ordered sequence of connected legs is a \emph{route}; and an asset between the two endpoints is a vehicle. We reconstruct routes from \RoutePanelRawSwaps{} swaps on \RoutePanelDeploymentCount{} Ethereum \ac{amm} deployments from \RoutePanelSpan{}; Table~\ref{tab:panel} reports the resulting panel. The token sequences identify the pair, every intermediary, and whether the route spans exchanges. This transaction-level object reveals vehicle use without inferring a connection from turnover in separate markets.

Uniswap v1 allowed only ETH--token pools, so each token-to-token trade had to cross ETH; we recover \VOneForcedRoutes{} such routes. Pair-level estimates trace how that new vehicle use becomes established. Among pairs that trade again, a 10 pp higher entry-day stablecoin share predicts \EntryPersistenceEarlyPairEffectPerTen{} higher stablecoin share during days 1--30 and \EntryPersistenceLatePairEffectPerTen{} during days 31--120 (Table~\ref{tab:entry-persistence}, panel A, columns 2 and 5). At the first sampled exact contest, the entry family still carries \FirstContestRouteRetention{} of routes, while its exact-output advantage predicts retention and its separate prior-capital coefficient is imprecise (Table~\ref{tab:first-contestable-choice}, panels A and B). The evidence therefore separates bridge availability, first competition, and mature retention.

Competition becomes sharper inside established pairs. On the same route sample, a 100 bp incumbent output advantage predicts \ContestOutputAdvantageHundredBpEffect{} higher retention, and a 10 pp prior full-range capital-share advantage predicts another \ContestCapitalAdvantageTenPpEffect{} (Table~\ref{tab:contestable-vehicle-choice}, column 4). When exact-output leadership changes hands, incumbent share falls \RankCrossingPlaceboDrop{} more than during the same event's earlier movement (Table~\ref{tab:price-rank-crossing}, panel B, column 4). Prior challenger capital predicts whether the new price lead persists (column 2), while routes retaining a mature incumbent leave only \ContestRetainedForegoneInputValueWeightedBps{} of input-value-weighted gross output (Table~\ref{tab:contestable-vehicle-consequences}, panel B). Vehicle dominance is thus maintained through price competitiveness associated with two-sided depth, while exact-price rank reversals reallocate flow.

We contribute an extensive-margin account of currency dominance and evidence on the market structure that sustains an incumbent vehicle. The route panel complements the holiday-based inference of \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} and the historical network evidence of \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. The pair decomposition connects aggregate leadership to relationship formation; the post-entry and exact-price estimates connect formation to classical volume--cost feedback, constrained currency-market liquidity supply, and decentralised-exchange liquidity provision \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,HuangRanaldoSchrimpfSomogyi2025Constrained,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}. The price comparison extends optimal-routing work from parallel pools to sequential routes through alternative vehicles \citep{XiMoallemi2026Routing}. Together, the results show where a vehicle network expands, when a cheaper challenger takes flow, and how two-leg capital predicts the persistence of price leadership.

The extensive margin is also relevant for payment-system design. Project Nexus assigns currency conversion to a foreign-exchange provider, while Project Rialto uses a third currency when direct exchange is unavailable \citep{BISNexusFX2024,BISRialto2025}. Access policy can therefore affect the price inside an existing relationship and which two-sided markets become the bridge inherited by new ones.
