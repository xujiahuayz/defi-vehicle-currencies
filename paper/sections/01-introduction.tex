\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vehicle currency can dominate trading even when no party wants to hold it. It is acquired only to be exchanged again because two liquid markets through the vehicle can outperform a thin direct market. The U.S. dollar appears on more than 85\% of foreign-exchange transactions, far more often than the United States' share of cross-border economic activity would suggest \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. Vehicle use concentrates trading in dollar markets; deeper markets lower trading costs and reinforce the dollar's intermediary role \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. This feedback explains persistence after a vehicle becomes dominant. It says less about how a challenger gains share as new trading relationships and market technologies appear.
% literature/text/2026-Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX-dollar-dominance-in-fx-trading.txt; dollar involvement exceeds 85 percent

Aggregate dominance combines two margins. A continuing relationship can replace its vehicle, or activity can move toward relationships that already use another vehicle and toward new relationships formed around it. The same aggregate change can therefore arise with pervasive switching or with no net switching inside established relationships. Separating these cases requires transactions that attach the intermediary to the exchange between its source and destination.

Standard foreign-exchange records report turnover in each currency pair without linking the legs of the customer's exchange.\footnote{For example, see the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} A yen--euro exchange routed through dollars appears as separate yen--dollar and dollar--euro trades. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} infers vehicle activity from currency-pair volume responses around non-overlapping holidays. In decentralised exchange, the pool calls inside one blockchain transaction preserve their execution order. We use those records to observe the connected route directly.

We call the ordered source and destination the \emph{endpoint pair}, shortened hereafter to \emph{pair}. Each directed pool execution is a \emph{leg}; the ordered sequence of connected legs is a \emph{route}; and an asset between the two endpoints is a vehicle. We reconstruct routes from \RoutePanelRawSwaps{} swaps on \RoutePanelDeploymentCount{} Ethereum \ac{amm} deployments from \RoutePanelSpan{}; Table~\ref{tab:panel} reports the resulting panel. The token sequences identify the pair, every intermediary, and whether the route spans exchanges. This transaction-level object reveals vehicle use without inferring a connection from turnover in separate markets.

The sample begins with an institutional benchmark for how a vehicle network forms. Uniswap v1 allowed only ETH--token pools, so each token-to-token trade had to cross ETH; we recover \VOneForcedRoutes{} such routes. Uniswap v2 removed that rule and allowed any two ERC-20 tokens to form a pool. Six years later, however, \VTwoWethTradeShare{} of single-leg v2 trades still use a WETH pool and \VTwoWethNewPairShare{} of token combinations first traded in 2026 include WETH. Removing the mandate did not unwind the pool network formed around the native asset. We read this chronology descriptively because venue migration and available token combinations changed with the rule.

We decompose the pooled route activity into those margins. The route-count share rises from \PairPooledBase{} to \PairPooledEnd{}, or \PairPooledTotal{}. Moves toward and away from stablecoins within continuing pairs net to \PairPooledWithin{}. Trading reallocation across continuing pairs contributes \PairPooledReweight{}, while pairs present in only one comparison period contribute \PairPooledExclusive{}. Panel B of Table~\ref{tab:pair-composition} reports these route-count components. Panel C gives \PairValueTotal{} overall by routed value, with \PairValueWithin{} from within-pair switching, \PairValueReweight{} from reallocation, and \PairValueExclusive{} from period-specific pairs. We therefore locate the aggregate rotation in network expansion and the reallocation of trading across pairs; vehicle changes inside continuing pairs largely offset in aggregate.

The vehicle used at pair entry predicts later allocation: a 1 pp higher initial stablecoin share predicts \FormationPathEntryShareOneTwentyCoef{} higher stablecoin share over the next 120 days (Table~\ref{tab:formation-regressions}, second row). We read this estimate as persistence in an equilibrium allocation. The first vehicle reflects the depth, prices, and venue access available when the relationship forms, and those conditions can persist with it.

Local two-leg liquidity provides the corresponding market structure. A stablecoin bridge often becomes technically available while remaining shallow. First-month adoption is \BridgeTimingShallowMonthShare{} when its weaker leg has less than one-tenth of WETH depth and \BridgeTimingCompetitiveMonthShare{} once it reaches that threshold (Table~\ref{tab:bridge-establishment}, panel C). Among eventual adopters, deposited capital on the weaker leg rises before the first observed stablecoin route (Figure~\ref{fig:bridge-adoption-capital-path}). Broader exact price competition changes venue much more often than vehicle: opening other vehicles and the direct path adds only \ExactFrontierVehicleAddition{} to the share of improved routes and lowers stablecoin vehicle share by \ExactFrontierStableDecline{} (Table~\ref{tab:exact-vehicle-frontier}, panel B). We interpret these associations as equilibrium evidence that local depth, current execution prices, and vehicle use move together; a causal liquidity-supply effect requires an exogenous shift.

We contribute a directly observed vehicle measure and an extensive-margin account of currency dominance. Our route measure complements the holiday-based inference of \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} and the historical network evidence of \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. The two-leg depth and exact-price results connect the formation margin to classical volume--cost feedback, constrained currency-market liquidity supply, and decentralised-exchange liquidity provision \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,HuangRanaldoSchrimpfSomogyi2025Constrained,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}. Our price frontier extends optimal-routing comparisons across parallel pools to sequential paths through alternative vehicles \citep{XiMoallemi2026Routing}. We identify where the network forms and which observable prices support it. Trader intent, provider expectations, private flow, and omitted venues define the remaining empirical boundary.

The extensive margin is also relevant for payment-system design. Project Nexus assigns currency conversion to a foreign-exchange provider, while Project Rialto uses a third currency when direct exchange is unavailable \citep{BISNexusFX2024,BISRialto2025}. Access policy can therefore affect the price inside an existing relationship and which two-sided markets become the bridge inherited by new ones.
