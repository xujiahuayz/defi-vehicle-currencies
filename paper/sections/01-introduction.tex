\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The U.S. dollar appears on more than 85\% of foreign-exchange transactions, far more often than the United States' share of cross-border economic activity would suggest \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. One reason is the dollar's use as a vehicle between other currencies. Vehicle use concentrates trading in dollar markets; deeper markets lower trading costs; and lower costs reinforce the dollar's intermediary role \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. These mechanisms rationalise persistence. They leave open how vehicle dominance changes when new markets and trading technologies alter the routes available to traders.

The route used for an exchange is rarely observed. Standard foreign-exchange records report turnover by currency pair.\footnote{For example, see the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} A yen--euro exchange routed through dollars appears as turnover in the yen--dollar and dollar--euro markets, with no link between the two legs. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} infers vehicle trading from pair-level volume responses around non-overlapping holidays. We instead study a market in which the complete route is recorded.

Decentralised exchanges record the pool calls made within a transaction in their execution order. We assemble 472 million swap records from eight Ethereum \ac{amm} deployments between February 2020 and June 2026. From these records, we recover the asset sold, the asset bought, and each intermediary used between them, including routes that cross exchanges. The sample spans the expansion of stablecoin markets, cross-exchange routing, and protocols that changed which asset pairs could trade directly.

We measure vehicle dominance as a currency's share of observed intermediary use. The currency's share of the endpoints in the same transactions provides a benchmark for ordinary demand to buy or sell it. This comparison shows whether the currency is becoming more prevalent between other assets or is simply traded more often itself. Vehicle dominance complements measures based on quoting, invoicing, and funding, each of which captures a different economic decision \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,GopinathEtAl2020DCP,GopinathStein2021Making}.

Stablecoins become substantially more prominent vehicles during the sample. Comparing the same January-through-June dates in 2024 and 2026, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count and from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{} by routed value. The path reverses twice: native assets regain share in 2023 and 2024, and stablecoins recover the value lead in 2025. Their lead by route count is temporary. Within the stablecoin category, the transition is concentrated in USDT: its intermediary share rises markedly relative to its endpoint share, whereas USDC changes little.

The aggregate rotation occurs mainly through the allocation of trading across endpoint markets. Changes in which pairs trade, the amount of routed activity across continuing pairs, and the incidence of indirect execution account for most of the increase. Within continuing markets that share the same ordered endpoints and route scope, the estimated stable-for-native substitution is small and its confidence bound remains far below the aggregate increase. Stablecoin share also rises among pairs observed in both endpoint years.

The same pattern appears when routes are separated by whether they use one exchange or several. Cross-exchange routing becomes more common, while the fraction of all routes that uses an intermediary falls. Among routes that use an intermediary, stablecoin share rises within both the single- and cross-exchange groups. Its principal margin remains a reallocation of routed activity toward markets in which stablecoins, especially USDT, serve as intermediaries.

This result changes the empirical problem posed by vehicle-currency theory. Explaining the transition requires an account of why trading moved toward markets connected to stablecoin vehicles and why continuing markets changed their use of indirect execution. Relative execution costs, liquidity allocation, asset risk, software, and protocol design can all affect those margins. The route evidence locates the transition; the mechanism analysis asks which forces moved trading across markets.

The paper contributes direct evidence on a currency role that is usually inferred from aggregate turnover. Observed transaction sequences distinguish intermediary use from endpoint demand without relying on an exclusion restriction for latent vehicle trades. They also reveal a margin that pair-level turnover conceals. Classical vehicle-currency mechanisms emphasise the reinforcing depth of the two markets joined by a vehicle; aggregate dominance also changes when trading reallocates toward endpoint markets connected by another intermediary. This market-allocation margin links vehicle-currency theory to the formation of liquid markets, fragmented execution, and the design of trading protocols \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}.

The remainder of the paper develops the evidence in that order. Section~\ref{sec:setting} describes the exchanges, reconstructs transaction-level routes, and defines vehicle dominance. Section~\ref{sec:dominance} documents the rotation toward stablecoins and identifies where it occurs. Sections~\ref{sec:executability} and~\ref{sec:rivals} discuss the evidence required to distinguish relative costs, liquidity, and market design as explanations for the transition.
