\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A handful of currencies carry most of the world's indirect exchange. Trades between two minor currencies are routinely executed by going through a third, and the third is almost always the dollar. The standard explanation is a thick-market externality: a currency is cheap to trade because many others trade it, and many others trade it because it is cheap, so an incumbent's cost advantage and its incumbency are two descriptions of one equilibrium. The explanation is old and widely accepted, and it has a measurement problem that has never been solved. If the incumbent's cost advantage is a consequence of its role, then the data never contain the state that would let anyone measure how much the role is worth on its own, namely a currency holding the vehicle role at a moment when routing through it is not the cheapest way to trade.

This paper measures that state. Decentralised exchange on Ethereum is a currency system where every price, every available path, and every executed trade is recorded, and where the counterfactual path is computable. When a trader routes from one asset to another through a third, the alternative of going directly is a pool whose state at that moment is known, so the cost of the road not taken can be quoted with the same arithmetic the exchange itself used. The configuration that foreign exchange cannot observe is therefore observable here, and it is not rare. Of routing that actually executed and passed through an intermediary, 27.2\% would have done better going direct at the state it executed in, measured on 1,762 matched routes over four consecutively priced days.

The setting supplies a second thing the currency literature has lacked, which is the removal of habit. Routing is performed by deterministic graph optimisers that re-solve at every quote and hold no memory of yesterday. A human dealer's relationships, defaults and attention are absent by construction, so whatever incumbency remains has to work through state variables: where liquidity sits, which pairs exist, and what each path costs. This is a narrower and more testable object than the one the FX literature studies, and it is the reason a null here would be informative.

What the paper does with that state is ask how long the role outlives the advantage. A frequency of dominated routing is a statement about moments. The question the inertia literature actually poses is about duration, and it has a sharp form that observational currency data cannot reach: given that an incumbent has lost its cost advantage, does it hold the role longer than a challenger with an equivalent advantage takes to take it. Equal durations are \emph{persistence}, meaning slow adjustment that is symmetric in direction. Retention exceeding displacement is \emph{hysteresis}, meaning the incumbent enjoys a protection no symmetric friction can produce. Both arms are measurable here on the same pairs, at the same states, with the same pricing engine, and Section~\ref{sec:survival} sets them out.

Three descriptive findings frame the exercise, and each one is about how a vehicle role is made and unmade. The role migrated: value-weighted, the native platform asset's share of intermediation falls from 73.0\% to 14.8\% across six years while the stable numeraire's rises from 21.2\% to 50.1\%. The role also fragmented, with the effective number of vehicles rising from 1.42 to 8.43 by value and from 1.86 to 7.31 by transaction count, which makes the spreading common to both ways of counting. Reading those two together separates worlds the currency literature can state but not distinguish, because succession holds concentration fixed while changing the leader and fragmentation lowers concentration while the leader may survive, and the dollar against the euro and the renminbi is precisely this question. The third finding is that architecture is not destiny: when the platform removed a hard technical mandate to route through the native asset, native pairing did not fall, and the share of new pairs quoted against it rose from 84.1\% to 97.9\%.

Two rival accounts of survival are named here and run against each other in Section~\ref{sec:rivals}. Under \emph{liquidity supply as the slow state variable}, a vehicle keeps its role after losing its cost edge because the depth backing it is committed capital that moves slowly, so the role follows the capital with a lag and nothing about the asset itself matters. Under \emph{integration and reachability}, the role persists because routers, front ends and pair listings encode the incumbent as the default hop, so the constraint is the graph and not the price. The two make different predictions about which routes stop using a dominated vehicle first, and the paper reports which survives.

This paper's evidence is measurement and not identification, and it says so. Six of the nine empirical papers read to calibrate this exercise make no causal claim, and what they substitute is a data advantage together with an unusually heavy validation apparatus. The same choice is made here. Every venue's quoter is accepted only after reproducing swaps that actually happened, at 0.0000\% median error on three of the six venue families and 0.033\% on a fourth. Reconstruction of past pool state is done by unwinding backward from stored period-end values, because replaying forward returns a median 11.8\% error and does so silently. The panel refuses to quote any route whose own price impact would exceed 5\%, a bound read off the distribution of 932,270 realised trades and not chosen, which removes most quotable routes and moves the median measured gap from thousands of basis points to tens. Section~\ref{sec:executability} then asks whether the surviving gaps could have been taken, since a gap that pays after fees and gas should not persist and its presence would indicate measurement error and not economics.

The relation to three literatures is as follows. The theory of vehicle currencies explains why exchange concentrates on a small number of media and predicts multiple equilibria supported by thick-market externalities, and it is tested here on a system where the alternative paths are observable, which is what that theory has always needed and never had. The empirical work on currency persistence estimates feedback between a currency's use and its cost on historical networks and finds persistence without lock-in, and it measures a currency's international role by how many markets quote it, which is a degree statistic that this paper's data allow to be separated from realised use. The market microstructure literature on inventory and adverse selection explains dealer spreads through the risk a liquidity supplier bears, and it operates on a single asset's spread where the object here is the choice among paths, so it supplies the cost decomposition and not the estimand.

The remainder proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setting} defines the objects and describes the data, the six priced venues and the support screen. Section~\ref{sec:dominance} reports how often the vehicle role is held while dominated, and how the role migrated and fragmented over the sample. Section~\ref{sec:survival} sets out the survival estimand and its two arms. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} runs the rival accounts against each other. Section~\ref{sec:executability} defends the measured object against the objection that the gaps would have been arbitraged away. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
