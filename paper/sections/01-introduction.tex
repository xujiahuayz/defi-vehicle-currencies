\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vehicle currency is the market's preferred intermediary between two other currencies. The role is highly concentrated in foreign exchange, and the standard explanation is self-reinforcing liquidity: routing more trades through one currency deepens its markets, which lowers transaction costs and attracts more trades \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies}. Monetary-search models generate a related coordination force because an intermediary is valuable when others are expected to accept it later \citep{KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium}. The same logic can sustain a currency's place in invoicing, funding, and balance sheets \citep{GopinathEtAl2020DCP,GopinathStein2021Making,Mukhin2022InternationalPriceSystem}. Yet the trading role at the center of these theories is rarely observed. Foreign-exchange records reveal turnover and prices while leaving the market-wide path between two currencies hidden. A currency can lose trading share without losing its intermediary role, or gain demand as an endpoint without becoming the currency others trade through.

Decentralised exchange makes that distinction observable. A transaction records an ordered sequence of calls to automated markets, so a trade from asset $i$ to asset $o$ through asset $k$ identifies both the intermediary and the two endpoints. We define vehicle dominance as the share of exact two-leg routes carried by an asset or asset class, giving each realised choice one intermediary and one vote. To distinguish vehicle use from ordinary demand to buy or sell the same asset, we also compare its intermediary share with its endpoint share among the same transactions. This route-level observation complements measures based on the number of markets quoting a currency \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}, aggregate price and volume responses \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}, invoice shares, and funding claims. It reveals the particular monetary role that those aggregate measures usually leave latent.

The market also undergoes an unusual institutional transition. Early protocols required trades between many tokens to pass through the native asset; later designs allow arbitrary pairs and permit routers to search across pools and exchanges. Stablecoins enter this expanding network as both widely traded assets and potential intermediaries. This history shows how vehicle use changes after the original architectural requirement disappears. Protocol launches coincide with changes in users, liquidity, and the set of traded pairs, so their dates establish chronology. Identifying an architecture effect requires a separate comparison.

Stablecoins gain sharply in the vehicle role, with reversals along the way. On calendar days observed in both endpoint years, their equal-weighted share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} in 2024 to \StableCountEnd{} in 2026. The change is \StableCountChange{}, with a calendar-HAC standard error of \StableCountSE{}. Among routes whose source, intermediary, and destination values agree within 20\%, the stablecoin share rises from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{}, a \StableValueChange{} increase with a standard error of \StableValueSE{}. Native intermediation rebounds in 2023 and 2024; stablecoins regain the value lead in 2025, whereas their lead by route count remains short-lived. The episode is therefore a rotation toward stable vehicles, with persistence still open.

Where does this rotation occur? The usual account of currency competition suggests substitution within a market: for the same two endpoints, traders abandon one intermediary for another. The route data show a broader reallocation. Of the \MarketBridgeTotal{} increase in pooled route-count share, pairs entering or leaving the sample account for \MarketSupportBridge{}, trading shifts across continuing pairs account for \MarketActivityReweight{}, and changes in how often continuing pairs use an intermediary account for \VehicleIncidenceReweight{}. The appearance or disappearance of a native-or-stable route contributes \VehicleRoleSupportBridge{}. Direct stable-for-native substitution within pairs that use an intermediary in both years contributes only \WithinPairStableShare{}. Pair turnover is incomplete because stablecoin share still rises from \ObservedBothYearsBase{} to \ObservedBothYearsEnd{} among pairs traded in both 2024 and 2026. Even so, most of the aggregate change comes from which markets are represented, how much activity they attract, and whether they use an intermediary at all. Vehicle-currency formation therefore includes an allocation of trading across markets alongside repeated currency choice inside a fixed market.

The aggregate stablecoin category hides a second reallocation. USDC and USDT together account for \JointStableContribution{} of the increase in stablecoin route share, but USDC starts with disproportionate intermediary use and changes little. USDT's intermediary share relative to its endpoint share rises from \USDTCountExcessBase{} to \USDTCountExcessEnd{} by route count and from \USDTValueExcessBase{} to \USDTValueExcessEnd{} by supported value. Its value-weighted intermediary-minus-endpoint gap moves from \USDTEndpointGapBase{} to \USDTEndpointGapEnd{}. USDT becomes more prominent between other assets even after accounting for its growing use as an asset to buy or sell. This comparison locates the rotation in one stablecoin, while leaving differences in backing, redemption, liquidity, and venue reach as competing explanations.

Expanding access across exchanges can help a widely available intermediary, but the observed shift reaches beyond that margin. Cross-exchange execution grows rapidly during the sample, while the share of economic routes using any intermediary falls from \FullIntermediationBase{} in 2022 to \FullIntermediationEnd{} in 2026 on the full perimeter and to \BalancedIntermediationEnd{} on a balanced five-exchange perimeter. Among routes that remain indirect, stablecoin share rises within every observed combination of exchange span and route complexity; the increase is \CrossVenueRotationPremium{} larger across exchanges than within one exchange, with a standard error of \CrossVenueRotationSE{}. The aggregate rotation thus occurs within broad route types as well as through changes in their relative importance. Because those categories still combine different endpoints, trade sizes, available paths, and router states, identifying search and venue-reach effects requires comparisons within the same opportunity set.

These results connect the international-currency literature's emphasis on coordination, liquidity, and persistence with research on fragmented trading and automated execution \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,GopinathStein2021Making,LeharParlour2024Uniswap,ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation}. The economic choices behind vehicle formation remain: liquidity providers decide where capital sits, protocols determine which paths exist, and routing systems choose among the paths they can reach. Automation changes their observability because the realised path shows when an asset actually connects two other markets. In this setting, stablecoin dominance grows mainly because routed activity moves toward markets in which stable intermediation is present, and because one stablecoin increasingly occupies the middle of routes relative to its own endpoint use.
