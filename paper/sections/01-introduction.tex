\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The U.S. dollar appears on more than 85\% of foreign-exchange transactions, far more often than the United States' share of cross-border economic activity would suggest \citep{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX}. Its use as a vehicle between other currencies helps explain this prominence. Vehicle use concentrates trading in dollar markets; deeper markets lower trading costs; and lower costs reinforce the dollar's intermediary role \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies,KiyotakiWright1989MoneyMedium,FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. This feedback explains why an established vehicle can persist. It says less about how dominance changes when new markets and trading technologies alter the routes available to traders.

Testing how dominance changes requires observing the full trading path. Standard foreign-exchange records report turnover by currency pair, not whether two reported trades belong to the same underlying exchange.\footnote{For example, see the \href{https://www.bis.org/statistics/triennialrep/guidelines_cbanks.htm}{BIS 2025 Triennial Survey reporting guidelines and templates}.} A yen--euro exchange routed through dollars therefore appears as turnover in the yen--dollar and dollar--euro markets, with no link between the two legs. \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} overcomes this limitation by using pair-level volume responses around non-overlapping holidays to infer vehicle trading. We take a complementary approach in a market where transaction logs preserve the sequence of pools and assets used in each exchange.

Decentralised exchanges split liquidity across smart-contract pools that a router can combine within one transaction. The path is observable. We reconstruct those combinations from 472 million swaps on eight Ethereum \ac{amm} deployments between February 2020 and June 2026. The reconstruction identifies the first and last assets in the observed pool sequence, every intermediary, and any movement across exchanges. It thus separates intermediary use from use at either end of the observed route. These pool-route endpoints need not coincide with the user's original instruction, a distinction we document with a transaction in Section~\ref{sec:setting}. This route-based measure complements measures of quoting, invoicing, and funding, which describe related but different currency choices \citep{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics,GopinathEtAl2020DCP,GopinathStein2021Making}.

Stablecoins become substantially more prominent vehicles during the sample, although the transition is not monotone. Comparing the same January-through-June dates in 2024 and 2026, their share among native and stable intermediaries rises from \StableCountBase{} to \StableCountEnd{} by route count and from \StableValueBase{} to \StableValueEnd{} by dollar-weighted volume. Native assets regain share in 2023 and 2024, stablecoins recover the value lead in 2025, and their lead by route count is temporary. The change is especially pronounced for USDT, whose intermediary use rises markedly relative to its appearance at observed route endpoints. The allocation of trading across source--destination pairs accounts for most of this rotation: some pairs appear in only one comparison year, activity shifts sharply across pairs that remain, and, among continuing pairs with comparable realised routes, the estimated stable-for-native substitution is close to zero and its confidence interval rules out changes of the aggregate magnitude. The dollar-weighted decomposition assigns essentially none of the increase to within-pair substitution; shifts in activity across continuing and comparison-year-specific pairs carry the change. Stablecoin share also rises separately among single-exchange and cross-exchange routes, even as cross-exchange routing expands rapidly.

Classical vehicle-currency models ask which intermediary minimises the cost of exchanging a given pair of assets. Their volume--cost feedback explains why trading converges on a common vehicle and why an established vehicle persists \citep{Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies}. The route decomposition reveals an additional empirical margin: the distribution of activity across source--destination pairs. Relative execution costs and routing software can change the intermediary selected for a given pair, while liquidity supply, asset risk, and protocol design can alter the routes through which activity occurs. A satisfactory explanation for the stablecoin gain must account for the reallocation across pairs that carries most of it.

We make two contributions. First, transaction sequences directly measure a currency role usually inferred from aggregate turnover. Linking the legs of an exchange separates intermediary use from use at the observed route endpoints without an exclusion restriction for latent vehicle trades, complementing the holiday-based inference of \citet{Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX} and the historical turnover evidence of \citet{FlandreauJobst2009Empirics}. Second, we show that aggregate vehicle dominance depends on the allocation of routed activity across source--destination pairs as well as intermediary choice within matched pairs. This result connects the self-reinforcing depth mechanism in vehicle-currency theory to research on fragmented trading and automated market making, where market design and liquidity supply shape the opportunities available to traders \citep{ChenDuffie2021Fragmentation,LeharParlour2024Uniswap}.

Section~\ref{sec:dominance} documents the rotation toward stablecoins and separates changes across pairs from substitution within matched pairs. Section~\ref{sec:executability} defines the counterfactual cost benchmark and the liquidity it represents. Section~\ref{sec:rivals} relates the route evidence to liquidity supply, exchange reach, and protocol architecture.
