### Eren and Malamud (2022), “Dominant Currency Debt”

- **Status:** Full-text read complete; merge-ready published-source card; saved artifact and all 19 PDF pages (journal pp. 571–589), including exhibits, notes, conclusion, and references, were read sequentially. The separately cited Internet Appendix is not contained in the saved artifact and therefore was not audited.
- **Roles:** Load-bearing theory/mechanism source for a borrower-driven account of dominant-currency debt; empirical-premise source for the horizon-dependent dollar–equity covariance; published-versus-working-paper closure; JFE research-communication exemplar; independent second read.
- **Source:** Egemen Eren and Semyon Malamud, “Dominant Currency Debt,” *Journal of Financial Economics* 144 (2022), 571–589, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.06.023; saved file `2022-ErenMalamud2022DominantDebt-dominant-currency-debt.pdf`; 19 PDF pages, corresponding to journal pp. 571–589.
- **Version:** Final published article, not the 2019 BIS working paper: received February 10, 2020; revised May 5, 2021; accepted May 9, 2021; available online July 18, 2021; © 2021 authors/Elsevier, assigned to JFE volume 144 (2022). The published paper uses a partial-equilibrium baseline and notes that the earlier working paper solved a general-equilibrium version (Section 2, p. 575).
- **Uses:** Cite as support for the proposition that, among otherwise comparable major funding currencies, a large global borrower may prefer the currency whose value falls in the borrower’s long-horizon distress states, because this reduces effective default costs (Introduction, pp. 572–574; Theorem 2.1 and Eq. (8), p. 576; Proposition 1, p. 577). Cite as evidence—not causal identification—that the dollar’s equity beta is negative at short horizons but becomes positive at horizons closer to corporate debt maturity (Eq. (12) and Fig. 4, pp. 581–582; Eq. (13), Table 3, and Fig. 5, pp. 582–583), and that longer-maturity bonds are more likely to be dollar-denominated within issuer-month cells (Table 4, pp. 584–585). Use the final published artifact as a JFE exemplar for a theory-to-measure-to-multiple-prediction exhibit ladder (Sections 2–7, pp. 575–588). Do not use it to establish that the mechanism causally created overall dollar dominance, that the dollar is always risky, that it explains vehicle-currency use in trading or invoicing, or that its results apply without qualification to emerging-market firms choosing between dollars and local currency; the paper expressly narrows its main domain to large global firms choosing among major liquid currencies (Introduction, p. 572; Section 2.1, pp. 575–576; Section 8, p. 588).
- **Scientific:** The paper asks why the dollar, rather than the euro, yen, Swiss franc, or pound, dominates international debt denomination and why its debt share fell and then rose around the GFC (pp. 571–574, 588). Its contribution is a borrower-driven “debt view”: in a tax-shield/default-cost capital-structure model with nominal defaultable debt, currency choice is governed by the currency’s covariance with firm value over the debt horizon, independently of lenders’ stochastic discount factor; with equal issuance costs, dollar-only issuance obtains under Eq. (8), and aggregate equity is offered as a proxy for the relevant firm-value state variable in Proposition 1 (Theorem 2.1, Eqs. (6)–(8), pp. 576–577). Under restrictive relative-PPP, common-shock, and independence assumptions, Theorem 2.2 maps dominance to the highest inflation risk premium (Assumption 2, Eq. (9), Theorem 2.2, p. 577). A three-period extension yields a no-buyback leverage-ratchet result and makes the relative nominal-yield-curve slope an additional currency-choice statistic (Assumption 3, Proposition 4, pp. 584–585; Theorem 6.1 and Eq. (14), pp. 585–586). Measurement combines two-year quanto-implied EUR/USD–S&P 500 covariance from Kremens and Martin, two- and five-year inflation risk premia from Hördahl and Tristani, BIS international-debt issuance, FRED dollar indexes, S&P 500/MSCI returns, US/German yield curves, and Dealogic bond issues (Sections 3–7, pp. 577–588). Table 1 reports that a 0.01 (one-standard-deviation) fall in QRP is associated with about a 3-percentage-point higher quarterly dollar issuance share, while a 0.02 fall in euro-area IRP is associated with about a 4-point higher share; the dollar IRP coefficient is insignificant (pp. 579–580). Those aggregate regressions are predictive descriptions, not causal designs: QRP has only 24 quarterly observations (2009Q4–2015Q3), IRP 84 (1999Q1–2019Q4), and controls are limited to trend/total issuance; Table 2 retains QRP/euro-IRP signs after adding the corporate basis, but this does not rule out convenience yield, market access, or common post-GFC forces (pp. 580–581). Historical overlapping-return regressions use horizon-matched Newey–West corrections and show sign reversal (Fig. 4, pp. 581–582); the non-overlapping annual VAR has only 45 observations, its lagged S&P-to-dollar coefficient is 0.122 at 10%, and cumulative dollar depreciation is significant at 5% only at two and six years, with acknowledged small-sample sensitivity (Table 3 and Fig. 5, pp. 582–583). Dealogic covers 706,924 bonds from 61,910 firms in 121 countries during 1999–2019 (p. 584): maturity coefficients are 1.49 points for non-banks and 2.99 for banks in broad specifications; with firm-by-month fixed effects among multi-currency issuers, the non-bank estimate is 1.63 points and insignificant while the bank estimate is 7.70 points and significant; the developed-market restriction gives 3.50 and 7.34 points (Table 4, p. 585). This is a tighter within-firm/month comparison but still does not randomly assign maturity or currency. Yield-slope coefficients are positive at all seven horizon pairs (Table 5, p. 587), and post-April-2020 estimates range from 0.77 to 3.25 percentage points (Table 6, p. 588), but the latter is explicitly preliminary pre/post evidence, not a clean COVID event-study identification. Boundary conditions are material: comparable liquidity/issuance costs; large global borrowers; exogenous exchange rates and a one-period baseline interpreted as several years; tax shields as the debt motive; limited hedging; partial equilibrium; restrictive PPP/common-shock assumptions; and technical conditions for the dynamic result (pp. 572, 575–577, 584–586). Credible alternatives include investor safe-asset demand, trade invoicing/network effects, covered-interest-parity/convenience-yield incentives, issuance costs and market access, and omitted macro trends (Related literature, pp. 574–575; Sections 3.4 and 4, pp. 580–583). The evidence discriminates patterns distinctive to the mechanism but does not causally identify the debt view as the origin of dollar dominance.
- **Structure:** The abstract states the named view, two proposed dominance characteristics, empirical fit, historical application, and COVID implication (p. 571). Section 1 opens with the $12 trillion scale fact and fall/rise puzzle, narrows the comparison to large global firms and major currencies, contrasts the conventional investor view with the borrower view, previews every theoretical and empirical result, and positions the paper against trade, safe-asset, vehicle-currency, and related debt literatures (pp. 571–575). Section 2 builds the static capital-structure model, proves the covariance criterion, connects firm value to stock indexes, and adds inflation/monetary policy via PPP (pp. 575–577). Section 3 maps theory to forward-looking QRP/IRP measures, then relates them to aggregate debt shares and tests convenience yield as an alternative (pp. 577–581; Figs. 2–3; Tables 1–2). Section 4 supplies backward-looking long-horizon covariance and lead-lag evidence through rolling regressions, a covariance decomposition, and a VAR/impulse response (pp. 580–583; Eq. (12)–(13); Figs. 4–5; Table 3). Section 5 tests the model’s maturity implication in granular Dealogic issuance data, moving from broad fixed effects to within-firm/month comparisons and a developed-market restriction (pp. 583–585; Table 4). Section 6 relaxes static capital structure in a three-period model, derives the leverage-ratchet/yield-slope condition, and tests the US–Germany slope differential (pp. 584–587; Eq. (14); Fig. 6; Table 5). Section 7 treats COVID as a fresh qualitative and preliminary issuance check (pp. 586–588; Fig. 7; Table 6). Section 8 restates the mechanism, policy implications, abrupt-belief-change possibility, and extensions/declarations of incompleteness (p. 588); references occupy p. 589.
- **Depth:** Analytical effort is concentrated in the static covariance result and its dynamic extension (Sections 2 and 6, pp. 575–586), with explicit assumptions, theorem statements, intuition, and empirically measurable comparative statics; proofs and several extensions are deferred to an Internet Appendix absent from this saved artifact (pp. 575–576, 582–583, 585). Institutional and measurement depth is moderate: the paper explains quanto pricing, IRP interpretation, debt-share construction, dollar indexes, and Dealogic sample/variables, but much data construction is imported from cited sources and exact replication detail is not fully in the main article (Sections 3–5, pp. 577–585). Identification depth is light for the central dominance claim: aggregate time-series regressions and historical covariances establish alignment and prediction, not causation; the strongest design is the issuer-by-month comparison for firms issuing in multiple currencies, yet maturity remains endogenous and its non-bank estimate loses significance (Table 4, p. 585). Robustness depth is distributed across trend/issuance controls, corporate-basis controls, stock and dollar indexes, overlapping versus non-overlapping returns, sector splits, fixed-effects variants, developed-market restrictions, yield horizons, and COVID subsamples (Tables 1–6, pp. 580–588), but several bilateral, non-overlapping, emerging-market, hedging, and proof checks are only said to appear in the unavailable Internet Appendix or “available upon request” (pp. 575–576, 581–583).
- **Breadth:** The paper spans corporate capital structure, international currencies, asset pricing, inflation/monetary policy, safe assets, trade invoicing, global banking, covered-interest-parity deviations, debt deflation, and leverage dynamics (Related literature, pp. 574–575). It tests the mechanism through forward-looking prices, historical returns, maturity choice, yield-curve slopes, and COVID issuance, and directly engages convenience yield through the corporate-basis control (Sections 3.4–7, pp. 580–588). Its breadth is disciplined by declared exclusions: it is not primarily a local-versus-dollar theory for emerging-market firms; it abstracts from network effects, price stickiness, safety demand, most UIP frictions, endogenous exchange rates, and a joint model of trade/banking/reserves; these are complements or future work rather than rejected mechanisms (pp. 572, 574–575, 588). It covers multiple competing major currencies conceptually, but the main published-text forward-looking and issuance-share tests are predominantly dollar-versus-euro; several bilateral comparisons reside only in the unavailable Internet Appendix (pp. 578, 581–583).
- **Optics:** The short declarative title names the proposed object, while the abstract immediately labels the “debt view,” states two memorable conditions, asserts empirical fit, and ends on historical/COVID relevance (p. 571). The opening uses scale and a visible fall/rise exhibit before narrowing the estimand and staging three challenges to the conventional view (pp. 571–572; Fig. 1), then gives an unusually complete result-by-result roadmap before the literature review (pp. 572–575). Claims are memorable but sometimes more emphatic than the designs warrant—“perfectly in line,” “strong evidence,” and “quite remarkably” accompany mainly associational tests (pp. 573, 578, 586)—although the body also discloses restrictive assumptions, short samples, small-sample problems, proxy status, preliminary COVID evidence, and scope limits (pp. 575–578, 581–588). The exhibit ladder is coherent: prevalence (Fig. 1), forward-looking risks (Fig. 2), issuance quantities (Fig. 3; Tables 1–2), horizon mechanism (Figs. 4–5; Table 3), bond-level implication (Table 4), dynamic/yield-slope implication (Fig. 6; Table 5), and real-time application (Fig. 7; Table 6). Most captions report samples, sources, definitions, inference, and specification details, making Tables 1–6 and Figs. 4–5 comparatively auditable; Fig. 6’s caption is too terse to stand alone (p. 586). Notation is dense but theorem-to-regression mapping is repeatedly verbalized; prose is compact and confident. Page allocation is front-heavy—roughly four pages of introduction/literature, two of baseline theory, about nine of layered empirical tests plus two of dynamic theory, and one of conclusion—which makes the paper read as a mechanism paper seeking credibility through convergent evidence rather than a single identified empirical design. Reviewer-facing credibility comes from sharp scope, explicit assumptions, a competing-basis control, multiple independent measures, within-firm/month specifications, and candid caveats, offset by reliance on an unavailable Internet Appendix and unreported “available upon request” sensitivity checks.

- **Implication:** The admissible project claim is narrow but useful: Eren and Malamud provide a formal borrower-side mechanism under which a currency’s depreciation in firms’ long-horizon distress states can make it dominant for nominal debt, and they document multiple predicted associations for the dollar (Theorem 2.1/Eq. (8), pp. 576–577; Figs. 2, 4–5 and Tables 1, 4–5, pp. 578–587). This supplies a mechanism and calibrated empirical facts for debt denomination, not proof that borrower hedging caused the dollar’s system-wide dominance or that debt dominance transfers automatically to vehicle-currency status in exchange, trade invoicing, banking, or reserves; those spillovers are explicitly left for future work (p. 588). Any project sentence should say “consistent with,” “predicts,” or “provides evidence for,” preserve the debt-maturity horizon and major-currency/large-firm boundary, and separate the clean theoretical comparative statics from the paper’s largely associational empirical validation.
