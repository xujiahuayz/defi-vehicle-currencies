### Mukhin (2022), “An Equilibrium Model of the International Price System”

- Uses: Use as support for the proposition that price-setting complementarities and input-output linkages can generate coordination on a vehicle currency (Section II.A, pp. 660–662, Figure 1, Proposition 1); for the model-based large-economy and anchor-currency advantages of the dollar (Section II.B, pp. 662–665, Figure 2, Propositions 2–3); for path dependence in transitions between dominant currencies (Section II.C, pp. 665–667, Figure 3, Corollary 1); for quantitative invoicing benchmarks (Section III.D, pp. 676–681, Table 1, Figures 7–10); and for explicitly conditional dollar-renminbi scenarios (Section III.F, pp. 683–685, Figure 11). Do not cite it as causal evidence that dollar pegs cause dollar invoicing or as an unconditional forecast.
- Scientific: The paper asks why exporters coordinate on the dollar and whether it will remain dominant. Its contribution is a tractable multi-country general-equilibrium model in which sticky-price exporters choose the currency that minimizes variation in their desired price (Lemma 1 and equation 10, pp. 657–658); input-output and competitor-price linkages create strategic complementarities, multiple equilibria, and hysteresis (equations 11–15, pp. 658–662). Dollar dominance follows from US trade size and the dollar’s low idiosyncratic exchange-rate volatility as an anchor, while positive inflation discourages use of a currency dynamically (Propositions 2–4, pp. 663–668, equation 17). The quantitative model uses OECD ICIO matrices for 63 economies and 32 sectors, IMF exchange-rate and inflation series, externally calibrated price stickiness and price complementarities, and one targeted invoicing moment—the 1995 aggregate DCP share—to set currency-switching costs (Section III.B, pp. 669–671). For the countries with invoicing data, simulated versus observed export shares are 52% versus 53% for DCP and 31% versus 35% for ECP (Figure 7, pp. 677–678); trade-weighted cross-country fit is R² = 0.80 for DCP, 0.96 for ECP, and 0.91 for LCP (Figure 8, pp. 678–679). These are untargeted fit exercises beyond the single 1995 target, not causal identification or direct discrimination against transaction-cost and financial-friction theories. Results are point-calibrated without reported sampling, posterior, or counterfactual confidence intervals; robustness covers alternative currency sets, domestic invoicing, zero inflation, initial conditions, services, and flexible prices (Table 1 and Section III.E, pp. 677, 681–683), with fixed-cost sensitivity reported only over 0.5–1.2 × 10⁻⁴ (footnote 16, p. 671). Important boundaries include exogenous exchange-rate regimes; complete markets, stabilized nominal demand, and financial shocks in the baseline; sector-country aggregation; no Chinese invoicing observation; an assumed all-dollar pre-1995 initial condition; and scenario-specific future trade, inflation, and covariance assumptions (pp. 655–660, 669–671, 683–685). Credible alternatives are that fit partly reflects history dependence, calibrated trade/exchange-rate structure, or omitted transaction and financial channels rather than uniquely validating the proposed nominal mechanism.
- Structure: The abstract and introduction state the puzzle, mechanism, calibration, fit, and counterfactual claims, then position the paper against transaction-cost, financial-friction, and nominal-friction literatures (pp. 650–654). Section I builds the baseline environment, derives individual currency choice, characterizes equilibrium prices, and establishes block recursion in wages and exchange rates (pp. 654–660, equations 1–14, Lemmas 1–3). Section II derives vehicle-currency existence, dollar fundamentals, and transition hysteresis (pp. 660–667, Proposition 1 through Corollary 1). Section III.A extends the model to dynamic, multi-country, multi-sector Calvo pricing (pp. 667–669); III.B documents data and calibration (pp. 669–671); III.C visualizes the four calibrated determinants (pp. 671–676, Facts 1–4 and Figures 4–6); III.D evaluates aggregate, country, sector, and time-series fit (pp. 676–681, Table 1 and Figures 7–10); III.E conducts robustness and mechanism discussion (pp. 681–683); III.F presents future-dollar counterfactuals (pp. 683–685, Figure 11). Section IV summarizes results and limits and proposes extensions (pp. 685–686); references occupy pp. 686–688.
- Depth: Analytical depth is concentrated in Sections I–II and Proposition 4, where desired-price variance, equilibrium price feedback, strategic complementarities, and dynamic pass-through are formalized (pp. 654–668). Quantitative depth is concentrated in constructing country-sector input-output exposure and exchange-rate covariance inputs, sequentially solving 1995/2005/2015 equilibria, and comparing predictions across several margins (pp. 669–681). Measurement and institutional detail are lighter: invoicing data are inherited from Georgiadis et al., some observations derive from settlement rather than customs data, services are excluded in the benchmark, and China is missing (pp. 670–671). Identification is light because only switching costs are tied to an invoicing target, external parameter estimates are treated as fixed, and no empirical shock isolates the proposed mechanism. Robustness is substantial within the model class but cannot be fully audited until the online appendix is read.
- Breadth: The article spans international pricing, open-economy macroeconomics, input-output trade, monetary regimes, dominant currencies, and historical transition. It considers PCP, LCP, DCP, other vehicle currencies, regional-currency equilibria, inflation, price flexibility, domestic invoicing, alternative initial conditions, and exchange-rate anchors. It distinguishes its nominal-friction account from transaction-market thickness and financial-contracting explanations (Related Literature, pp. 653–654), but does not jointly estimate or test those mechanisms. Declared exclusions include endogenous exchange-rate-regime choice, realistic financial intermediation and reserve-currency demand, firm-level network granularity, direct estimates of switching costs, and several policy experiments (Conclusion, pp. 685–686).
- Optics: The title accurately advertises an equilibrium model rather than causal evidence. The abstract follows a disciplined question–mechanism–calibration–fit–counterfactual sequence (p. 650), while the introduction opens with globally salient dollar-invoicing facts and policy questions before stating ingredients and results (pp. 650–654). Claims are generally marked as model predictions, though phrases such as “surprisingly good fit” and future-status conclusions are stronger than the reported uncertainty warrants (pp. 652, 676–680). The exhibit ladder is effective: Figure 1 diagrams the pricing mechanism; Figures 2–3 develop equilibrium regions and transition; Figures 4–6 expose calibrated inputs; Figures 7–10 validate fit; and Figure 11 delivers counterfactuals. Table 1 efficiently consolidates baseline magnitudes and robustness. Captions are informative, notation is dense but consistently indexed, and analytical statements are packaged as lemmas, propositions, facts, and a corollary. Roughly half the article is devoted to theory and half to data, fit, and counterfactuals; transparency about the sole invoicing target, initial-condition assumption, and robustness alternatives supports reviewer confidence, while relegating derivations and many validation exhibits to an unread online appendix limits standalone auditability.

- Implication: The durable contribution is a quantitatively disciplined mechanism showing how production and pricing networks can turn currency choice into a coordination problem, making dollar dominance depend jointly on fundamentals and history. It supports interpreting dollar invoicing as an equilibrium outcome that can persist despite declining US trade weight, but its strongest policy claims remain conditional structural counterfactuals: continued dollar pegs preserve dominance; a renminbi-centered anchor system produces regionalization; and 10% US inflation produces a large modeled shift toward the renminbi (Section III.F, pp. 683–685, Figure 11).
