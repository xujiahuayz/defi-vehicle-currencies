### Cong, Li, and Wang (2022), Token-based Platform Finance — Online Appendix

- Roles: Published-version theoretical supplement; institutional-motivation source; proof and comparative-statics audit; independent second read; JFE supplementary-material exemplar.
- Source: Lin William Cong, Ye Li, and Neng Wang, “Token-based Platform Finance: Online Appendix,” saved as 2022-CongLiWang2022TokenPlatformAppendix-supplement-supplementary-material-to-token-based-platform-finance.pdf, 25 PDF pages: unnumbered title page plus appendix pages A-1–D-5.
- Version: Online Appendix to the published article “Token-based platform finance,” Journal of Financial Economics 144(3), June 2022, pp. 972–991, DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.10.002; this artifact is supplementary material, not the paginated journal article or a working paper.
- Uses: Supports attribution that platform tokens can jointly provide a local payment medium, compensate contributors, finance development, and permit dynamic supply management (Appendix A opening, PDF p. 2/A-1; Sections A.1–A.3, PDF pp. 3–13/A-2–B-1); supplies the transaction-surplus microfoundation behind token convenience yield and user demand (Section B, PDF pp. 13–15/B-1–C-1, Eqs. B.1–B.4); permits verification of Propositions 1–5, Corollary 1, and Lemmas 1–3 (Section C, PDF pp. 16–21/C-2–D-1, Eqs. C.1–C.19); and documents additional competition and user-heterogeneity comparative statics (Sections D.1–D.2, PDF pp. 21–23/D-1–D-3, Figs. D.1–D.2).
- Scientific: The supplement asks whether the main model’s defining ingredients—transaction demand for tokens, discretionary issuance or buyback, platform investment, and blockchain-enabled commitment—have institutional and analytical foundations. Its contribution is theoretical support rather than new empirical evidence: Section B derives token holdings as a convenience yield from Poisson transaction opportunities and avoided transaction costs; Section C establishes the stated equilibrium and policy results; Section D extends numerical comparative statics. The model uses continuous time, infinitely lived risk-neutral agents, endogenous adoption, platform quality At, token price Pt, token supply Mt, investment Lt, heterogeneous transaction need ui, and normalized supply mt=Mt/A_t^δ. Identification is structural and assumption-driven, not causal or econometric. There are no estimated parameters, standard errors, confidence intervals, formal model comparison, or systematic dataset. The case evidence is illustrative and selected from platform documents, regulatory complaints, websites, and media. Central boundary conditions include zero-cost instantaneous dollar borrowing, concave transaction-cost savings, Poisson opportunities, functional-form restrictions in Eq. B.4, Pareto user heterogeneity, focus on a positive-token-price Markov equilibrium, and inherited main-text financing and productivity dynamics (PDF pp. 13–20/B-1–C-6). Thus the artifact demonstrates internally consistent mechanisms, not that discretionary token policy or blockchain commitment causes higher investment in observed platforms.
- Structure: The title page identifies the supplement (PDF p. 1). Appendix A supplies institutional motivation: overview and dual token roles (p. 2/A-1), Kik/Kin case (Section A.1, pp. 3–4/A-2–A-3), token embedding and required holdings (Section A.2(i), pp. 4–6/A-3–A-5), dynamic token supply and platform management (A.2(ii), pp. 6–8/A-5–A-7), entrepreneur payouts and buybacks (A.2(iii), pp. 8–9/A-7–A-8), and discretion plus blockchain commitment technologies and limitations (Section A.3, pp. 10–13/A-9–B-1). Appendix B derives platform transaction surplus and optimal labor/token demand (pp. 13–15/B-1–C-1, Eqs. B.1–B.4). Appendix C gives proofs for Proposition 1 and Corollary 1, Propositions 2–5, and Lemmas 1–3 (pp. 16–21/C-2–D-1). Appendix D reports competition and user-distribution comparative statics (pp. 21–23/D-1–D-3, Figs. D.1–D.2). References occupy pp. 24–25/D-4–D-5.
- Depth: Analytical depth is concentrated in the transaction-surplus derivation and equilibrium proofs, especially optimal holdings and adoption (Eqs. C.3–C.10, PDF pp. 16–18), homogeneity and state reduction (Eqs. C.11–C.19, pp. 18–20), and payout/buyback boundary conditions (p. 20/C-6). Institutional depth is greatest in Appendix A’s numerous platform examples and discussion of token allocation, burning, smart contracts, storage, and governance. Numerical depth is lighter: Figs. D.1–D.2 vary ω, κ, and ξ only narrowly and provide qualitative interpretation without sensitivity tables or reported magnitudes. Measurement validation, empirical identification, statistical uncertainty, data construction, and reproducibility materials are absent.
- Breadth: The appendix connects platform economics, monetary convenience yield, corporate financing, endogenous adoption, blockchain contracting, governance, auditing, and token-market practice. It considers centralized and blockchain platforms; payment, work, staking, and escrow uses; issuance, founder allocations, buybacks, and burning; and commitment through immutable records, smart contracts, distributed storage, and governance (PDF pp. 4–13/A-3–B-1). It acknowledges meaningful boundaries: tokens need not be blockchain-based (p. 8/A-7), discretionary governance remains common despite technical commitment possibilities (pp. 10–11/A-9–A-10), and blockchain commitment is not a panacea because contracting scope and oracle infrastructure are limited (p. 13/B-1). Breadth of examples does not substitute for representative evidence, and speculative demand, regulatory classification, protocol security, strategic manipulation, and empirical welfare measurement remain outside the analysis.
- Optics: The exact title clearly labels the artifact as an Online Appendix, but there is no abstract or independent roadmap beyond Appendix A’s opening paragraph (PDF p. 2/A-1). The communication strategy moves from a recognizable case to generalized institutional features, then microfoundation, proofs, and numerical extensions. Claims are often appropriately framed as mechanisms or possibilities, although language that three Kik/Kin observations “apply generally” (p. 4/A-3) exceeds what selected examples establish. The exhibit ladder is sparse but functional: equations carry the proof burden, while Figs. D.1–D.2 close with robustness intuition. Captions identify panels and parameter changes but do not report numerical effect sizes or full calibration details (pp. 22–23/D-2–D-3). Notation is dense and depends heavily on main-text equation and section references, so the supplement is not standalone. Copyediting is visibly weak: typographical and grammatical errors recur, and an informal drafting annotation appears above Fig. D.2 on PDF p. 23/D-3. Roughly eleven pages are devoted to institutional grounding, eight to microfoundation and proofs, three to comparative statics, and two to references, signaling that reviewer-facing credibility rests on institutional plausibility and analytical completeness rather than empirical validation.

- Implication: Cite this supplement as authoritative support for the published model’s institutional assumptions, derivations, proofs, and conditional comparative statics. Do not cite it as representative empirical evidence that token foundations generally exercise discretion, that buybacks improve platform outcomes, or that blockchain adoption causally mitigates underinvestment. Any project claim should be calibrated as a model result conditional on the stated transaction-demand, financing-friction, heterogeneity, and commitment assumptions. As a JFE exemplar it is useful for supplementary architecture and proof placement, but its selected case evidence, inherited notation, and copyediting defects make it a poor standalone prose or empirical-credibility template.
