### Graham, Grennan, Harvey, and Rajgopal (2022), “Corporate culture: Evidence from the field”

- Status: Complete full-text source-set read. The 42-page published article and 43-page supplementary PDF were read sequentially, including all tables, figures, notes, the image-embedded survey instrument, appendices, and relevant references.
- Roles: Independent second read; JFE venue exemplar; load-bearing source for descriptive claims about executives’ perceptions of corporate culture, the values-versus-norms measurement distinction, and survey-validation practice. Not suitable as stand-alone causal evidence that culture raises firm value or that changing leadership, compensation, or governance improves culture.

- Uses: Supports the attributed claim that surveyed executives perceive culture as economically important: 91% call it important or very important, 53.5% rank it among the top three value drivers, and 91.9% believe improvement would increase value (Section 3.4, Table 4, pp. 561–564). Supports descriptive claims that 53.6% would abandon a culturally misaligned acquisition, 40.6% select the NPV-inferior hypothetical project, 84.5% believe ineffective culture increases unethical conduct, and 69% say leadership should invest more time in culture (Tables 4–5 and 7, pp. 564–569). Supports the values-versus-day-to-day-norms construct and a detailed exemplar of survey measurement and external validation (Sections 2 and 5, pp. 556–579; Supplement C–F). Any use must say “executives report,” “believe,” “perceive,” or “is associated with”; the paper does not identify causal effects.
- Scientific: The paper asks what executives think corporate culture is, whether they perceive it to affect valuation and corporate actions, and what reinforces or obstructs an effective culture. Its central contribution is a large executive survey that operationalizes culture as seven aspirational/current values and nine behavioral norms, embeds these in a framework where leadership and formal institutions affect their alignment, and cross-checks survey measures against external data (Fig. 1, p. 555; Sections 2–5). Data comprise 18 mostly CEO/CFO interviews conducted October 2014–April 2015 and an online survey fielded September–October 2015; 1,898 responses become 1,348 executives at U.S. and Canadian for-profit public and private firms after exclusions, with 70% from private firms (pp. 556–558; Appendix A, pp. 580–588; Appendix Table C.2, supplement p. 2). Five coders classify open text, requiring three-of-five agreement; average inter-coder Spearman correlation is 0.54, ranging from 0.34 for detail-orientation to 0.72 for customer-orientation (pp. 557–558). Values and norms are descriptive constructs, not randomized treatments. Identification is cross-sectional and observational: the main evidence consists of proportions, conditional comparisons, and regressions with firm, executive, survey-noise, and industry controls. The authors expressly concede omitted variables, reverse causality, and the impossibility of causal statements (pp. 555, 572, 579). External validation is directionally useful but restricted to selected, identifiable public firms: Glassdoor n=171, website n≈188, Tobin’s Q n=158, and Total Q n≈146 (Figs. 2–3 and Tables 10–11, pp. 574–577). Culture effectiveness is positively associated with Glassdoor culture ratings (0.247, SE 0.068) and Tobin’s Q (0.402, SE 0.184); aggregate norms, but not aggregate values or website-advertised values, are associated with Tobin’s Q (Table 11, p. 577). Uncertainty remains substantial: reported significance generally assumes a single test despite many comparisons; selection tests cannot establish representativeness; controls, placebos, halo specifications, and cross-validation address measurement stability rather than causal identification. Supplement E reports positive norm and value–norm interaction coefficients but negative standalone aggregate-value coefficients for several self-reported outcomes (Table E.4, supplement p. 34). Most importantly, Q14 asks the magnitude, not direction, of culture’s effect; Appendix F shows it can test “no effect” but cannot formally test a positive effect, although only 7% of open-ended examples describe negative effects (supplement pp. 30, 41–42). Credible alternatives therefore include successful firms generating favorable culture perceptions, executive optimism, common-method bias, and culture-effectiveness measures partly reflecting outcomes rather than mechanisms. Boundary conditions are North American for-profit firms, senior-executive perceptions, and a 2015 cross-section; results do not automatically extend to employees, other countries, nonprofits, or realized effects of culture-change interventions.
- Structure: The abstract front-loads headline percentages and the values/norms distinction (p. 552). Section 1 defines three research blocks, previews results and survey objections, positions the paper against Guiso et al. (2015), and supplies the values–norms–formal-institutions framework (pp. 553–556). Section 2 covers interviews, sampling, coding, measurement, and advantages of surveys (pp. 556–558). Section 3 moves from summary statistics and cross-sectional heterogeneity to perceived value, M&A, risk, investment, ethics, reporting, and firm-type comparisons (pp. 558–567; Tables 1–6). Section 4 studies obstacles, leadership, investors, formal systems, and compensation (pp. 567–572; Tables 7–8). Section 5 addresses selection, self-serving answers, external validation, placebo tests, halo effects, and omitted drivers (pp. 572–579; Figs. 2–3, Tables 9–11). Section 6 summarizes findings and states ingredients for future theory (pp. 579–580). Appendix A supplies survey logistics and the complete instrument (pp. 580–588); Appendix B defines variables and external datasets (pp. 589–592); references occupy pp. 592–593. Supplement C provides benchmarking and external-validation robustness (internal pp. 0–16); D examines selection and response heterogeneity (pp. 17–27); E analyzes self-reported culture–outcome associations and prediction (pp. 28–40); F formalizes what Q14 can and cannot identify (pp. 41–42, Eqs. F.1–F.2).
- Depth: Measurement effort is substantial: expert vetting, 20 beta tests, randomized response ordering, open-ended coding, redundant and hypothetical questions, and detailed variable definitions appear in Section 2 and Appendices A–B. Descriptive and institutional depth is concentrated in Sections 3–4, with granular treatment of industry, ownership, turnover, risk, ethics, compensation, and leadership. Robustness and construct validation receive unusually heavy allocation—Section 5 plus all 43 supplement pages—including alternative samples, external datasets, placebos, halo controls, LASSO/ridge selection, and ten-fold cross-validation. Theory is lighter: Fig. 1 organizes mechanisms and the conclusion lists desirable model ingredients, but the paper neither develops nor tests a structural mechanism. Causal-identification depth is explicitly absent; robustness is aimed at authenticity and measurement validity, not exogenous variation.
- Breadth: The paper spans organizational economics, corporate finance, accounting, governance, incentives, relational contracting, social capital, ethics, innovation, risk-taking, M&A, short-termism, and intangible value (Introduction, pp. 553–556; references, pp. 592–593). It considers values, norms, leadership, investors, compensation, governance, finance, hiring and promotion, coordination, trust, and adaptability, while addressing selection, self-presentation, website aspiration, employee disagreement, halo effects, measurement error, multiple proxies, and omitted product-market success. This topical breadth exceeds the design’s evidentiary breadth: nearly all mechanisms and outcomes remain executive perceptions or conditional correlations. Declared boundaries include the imperfect empirical separation of values from norms (p. 560), public-firm-only external matches (pp. 573–578), unresolved unobservables (p. 555), and Q14’s inability to identify effect direction (Supplement F, pp. 41–42).
- Optics: The plain title and “Evidence from the field” subtitle immediately signal a broad empirical contribution. The abstract opens with 92% and 84%, poses “how can that be achieved?,” then lists definition, outcomes, obstacles, and external validation (p. 552). The introduction uses three numbered research blocks, previews the exhibit ladder, distinguishes values from norms early, positions the closest paper explicitly, and preempts survey criticism before formal methods (pp. 553–556). Exhibits proceed coherently from conceptual framework and measurement (Fig. 1; Tables 1–3), to valuation and actions (Tables 4–6), institutional obstacles (Tables 7–8), and validation (Tables 9–11; Figs. 2–3). Captions are unusually self-contained, repeating question wording, coding, samples, controls, and significance conventions. Notation is minimal in the article and isolated to Supplement F. Prose is dense but direct; approximately eight article pages and most of the supplement are devoted to validation and robustness, which builds reviewer-facing credibility. Claim calibration is generally careful—“perceived,” “believe,” “associated,” and an explicit no-causality disclaimer recur—but the numerical abstract and some concluding language can be read more strongly than the observational design permits. The most important interpretive qualification, that Q14 cannot establish a positive sign, is relegated to Supplement F.

- Implication: The durable scientific contribution is a rich, partially externally validated map of how senior executives describe culture and associate it with firm decisions, plus an influential empirical distinction between aspirational values and enacted norms. The evidence justifies claims about executive beliefs, construct measurement, correlational proxies, and research hypotheses. It does not verify that improving culture causes higher value, that stated M&A discounts predict completed transactions, or that compensation and leadership causally create or destroy culture. As a JFE communication exemplar, it demonstrates a strong pattern: numerical stakes in the abstract, a question-driven introduction, a simple conceptual framework, descriptive-to-mechanism-to-validation exhibit ordering, self-contained captions, explicit limitations, and extensive online robustness.
