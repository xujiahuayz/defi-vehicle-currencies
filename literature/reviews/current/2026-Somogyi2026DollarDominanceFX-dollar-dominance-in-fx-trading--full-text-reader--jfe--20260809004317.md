- Uses: Supports the proposition that strategic avoidance of price impact can induce indirect exchange through a vehicle currency and amplify trading-volume dominance: Figure 2 (pp. 5–6), equilibrium quantities and price impact in Equations (5)–(9) and Proposition 1 (pp. 10–12), and dominance conditions in Definition 1 and Proposition 2 (pp. 12–13). Supports a carefully qualified estimate that vehicle motives account for about 13%—$7.77 billion, rounded to $8 billion—of daily interdealer volume in sampled dollar pairs: holiday design in Equation (1) (pp. 5–7), Figure 3 and Table 1 (pp. 8–9). Supports evidence that fundamental-demand dominance is quantitatively more important than the two price-impact conditions: Figure 5, Equation (15), and Table 4 (pp. 18–20). Does not establish direct observation of routed trades, a unified theory of dollar dominance, applicability to crypto/DeFi markets, or causality of the reduced-form dominance regressions (pp. 2, 4, 13, 19).
- Scientific: The question is why USD involvement in FX transactions exceeds the U.S. share of global economic activity (pp. 1–4). The contribution combines a strategic demand-submission model with an empirical decomposition of interdealer volume into fundamental and vehicle-currency components. In the model, traders may deviate from fundamental currency-pair demands to reduce endogenous price impact; volume rises with average and variance of fundamental demand and, subject to qualifications, falls with return variance (Equations (2)–(9), Proposition 1, pp. 9–12). Proposition 2 gives three dominance conditions: larger fundamental demand, more volatile fundamental demand, and less volatile returns in dollar pairs (pp. 12–13). Data comprise hourly CLS volume and customer order flow plus Olsen indicative quotes for 11 major currencies and 25 pairs from September 1, 2012 through September 30, 2022; CLS lacks transaction prices, identities, initiators, and trade paths (pp. 3–4; p. 21 n. 5). Interdealer volume is aggregate volume minus customer buys and sells; net absolute customer flow proxies fundamental dealer demand; intraday flow variance and realized return variance proxy model primitives; Amihud-style realized power variation divided by volume measures price impact (pp. 4, 13–15). Identification uses volume changes in dollar pairs on third-country nonoverlapping holidays, conditional on trade, securities-flow, offshore dollar-debt, interest-differential, CIP-basis, and spread controls and holiday interactions (Equation (1), pp. 5–8). This is quasiexperimental evidence only under the exclusion restriction that such holidays affect the tested dollar pair through lost vehicle demand after controls. Regional holidays may also suppress unrelated fundamental, speculative, dealer, or global-liquidity activity; several controls are monthly; and the data cannot identify actual two-leg routes. The author explicitly acknowledges upward and downward biases (pp. 7, 9). Table 1 reports a volume-weighted mean vehicle share of 12.60%, with a 4.34%–24.83% range across minimum and maximum triplet estimates; USDEUR, USDJPY, and USDCHF reach approximately 25%–38%, while Scandinavian dollar pairs are near zero (pp. 8–9). Twelve of 15 triplets are dollar-dominated; EUR dominates the three Scandinavian triplets (pp. 16–18). Relative-importance results attribute 5.3% of daily DD variation to C1 and 3.1% jointly to C2–C3 (Figures 4–5, pp. 16, 19–20). Standardized panel estimates are C1 0.39, C2 0.18, and C3 0.04 for volume-based dominance in Table 4 column 6, with within R² 6.43% and overall R² 20.26% (p. 20). Inference uses Newey–West/Hodrick corrections and two-way clustering, but only 15 triplet clusters are available and no small-cluster correction is reported (Tables 3–4, pp. 17, 20). The reduced-form regressions are expressly noncausal because dominance and conditions are equilibrium outcomes (p. 19).
- Structure: The abstract and Introduction frame the excess-USD-volume puzzle, state the price-impact channel and 13% result, delimit the contribution, preview the model and evidence, and position three literatures (pp. 1–3). Section 2 describes CLS and Olsen data, coverage, construction, and limitations (pp. 3–4). Section 3 builds motivating evidence from macro-volume comparisons, direct-versus-indirect trading costs, and the holiday-based vehicle-volume decomposition (Figures 1–3, Equation (1), Table 1, pp. 4–9). Section 4 supplies the theoretical setup, equilibrium, comparative statics, definition of dominance, sufficient conditions, and testable implications (Equations (2)–(9), Propositions 1–2, pp. 9–13). Section 5 maps model objects to empirical proxies, reports descriptive statistics, classifies triplets, conducts hypothesis tests and relative-importance regressions, and briefly summarizes robustness (Table 2, Equations (10)–(15), Figures 4–5, Tables 3–4, pp. 13–20). Section 6 concludes on p. 20; acknowledgments and endnotes occupy pp. 20–21; references occupy pp. 21–23.
- Depth: Effort is concentrated in the economic mapping between endogenous price impact and vehicle routing, the construction of model-consistent empirical variables, and the cross-sectional triplet classification (pp. 9–20). Institutional discussion of CLS aggregation, internalization, indicative quotes, and dealer/customer distinctions is unusually useful (pp. 3–4, 13–15). The holiday design receives intuition, controls, and directional-bias discussion in the article (pp. 5–9), but its event inventory, pooled specification, full coefficients, and decisive diagnostics are placed in unread Appendix D. The analytical comparative statics hold a demand coefficient fixed, while the claimed resolution through simulations is in unread Appendix B (pp. 11–12). Main-text robustness is light—seasonality and detrending are summarized rather than exhibited (p. 19). Mechanism discrimination is strongest against bid-ask-spread savings through Figure 2, but remains incomplete against broader liquidity, network, dealer-inventory, or regional-activity explanations.
- Breadth: The paper integrates FX volume/order-flow, vehicle-currency, market-microstructure, and international-dollar literatures, explicitly relating its account to trade-invoicing, safe-asset, and dollar-debt views (pp. 2–3). It considers direct versus indirect spreads and price impact, fundamental versus vehicle demand, customer internalization, proprietary trading, funding liquidity, triangular-arbitrage deviations, seasonality, and euro regional dominance (pp. 5, 9–10, 13–19). Declared boundaries are important: it does not explain why fundamental USD demand is initially large or offer a unified theory of dollar dominance (p. 2); it abstracts from fixed costs and spreads in the model (p. 9); uncontingent market clearing is defended mainly at seconds-to-minutes horizons despite hourly/daily empirical implementation (p. 10); and the sample covers major CLS currencies, excluding important currencies such as the renminbi (p. 21 nn. 5–6).
- Optics: The declarative title is broad, while the abstract efficiently supplies the empirical puzzle, proposed mechanism, identification claim, headline estimate, theoretical conditions, and empirical test (p. 1). The opening uses a sharp 85%-versus-economic-share contrast and explains the two-leg vehicle trade before introducing notation (pp. 1–2). Claim calibration is mixed: the paper clearly disclaims a unified theory and labels the panel exercise noncausal (pp. 2, 19), yet repeatedly calls the channel “pivotal” and the holiday approach an “identification strategy” despite unobserved routes and a demanding exclusion restriction. The exhibit ladder is effective: macro fact in Figure 1, cost comparison in Figure 2, holiday estimate in Figure 3/Table 1, model, empirical mapping, classification, and panel estimates. Captions generally state construction, sample, units, and inference. Notation is dense but consistently tied to empirical counterparts. Reviewer-facing credibility benefits from explicit data limitations and two-sided bias discussion (pp. 4, 7, 9, 13–15). A substantive prose error appears in Section 4.4.2: p. 13 says C2 and C3 imply price impact in dollar pairs “will be larger,” contradicting the model and surrounding discussion that it should be lower. The nuanced qualification that each condition is individually sufficient only when the other two are equal appears after the more sweeping proposition language (p. 12). The main credibility burden remains in the unavailable online appendix.

- Implication: The published article is admissible support for a calibrated claim that vehicle-currency routing can reinforce incumbent currency dominance when indirect legs have lower expected price impact, and that such activity may constitute a meaningful minority of major-pair interdealer volume. The 13% figure must be described as a holiday-based inferred average, not observed routing or a model-free causal fact. The paper is not evidence that the same magnitudes or mechanism carry into decentralized or crypto markets without additional institutional validation. Any load-bearing use of its proofs, simulations, holiday exclusion restriction, pooled estimates, or robustness must remain provisional until the online appendix is saved and read.
