### Lehar and Parlour (2025), “Decentralized Exchange: The Uniswap Automated Market Maker”

- Uses: Supports the mechanism that constant-product AMM pool size adjusts until fee revenue balances arbitrage-related picking-off losses (Section II.B, pp. 330–334, Proposition 2 and equation (16); Section IV.A, pp. 339–341, Proposition 3 and Table II); documents Uniswap V1/V2 pool and transaction composition (Section III, pp. 334–338, Table I and Figures 6–7); provides descriptive evidence that WETH is the network’s central counterasset—99,863 of 105,098 pools, or 95.02%, trade directly against ETH/WETH (Appendix D, pp. 368–369, Figure D1); compares an Amihud-style minute-level price-impact measure across 43 Uniswap–Binance pairs (Section V.B, pp. 347–353, Figures 11–13 and Tables IV–V); and quantifies a narrow incremental blockchain-cost comparison between one DEX swap and two token transfers (Appendix F, pp. 371–373, Figure F1). It does not establish endogenous vehicle-currency choice, causal network dominance, or universal AMM superiority.
- Scientific: The question is why passive, pro-rata AMM liquidity is viable and when it can be cheaper or more stable than strategic limit-order liquidity (pp. 322–325). The model separates liquidity provision from price discovery: two limit-order suppliers can invest to obtain monopoly rents, whereas AMM suppliers commit paired assets, earn fees, and are adversely rebalanced by arbitrageurs after value innovations (Section II, pp. 328–334; Propositions 1–2). Pool size, rather than a supplier-chosen quote, equilibrates fee income and picking-off risk; predicted pool size is linear in liquidity-trade size and decreasing in innovation size and probability (Proposition 3, pp. 339–340). The data reconstruct ordered log-level interactions for 105,098 V1/V2 pools from November 2, 2018 through December 21, 2022: 94,232,292 Ethereum transactions, including 2,097,032 liquidity additions, 959,312 withdrawals, and 91,175,948 token trades (p. 335), with a regression sample of 59,606,977 observations in 1,525 pools (p. 338). Table II’s pool-and-day-fixed-effect specifications associate a one-unit increase in annualized block-level FX volatility with a USD 0.152–0.163 million smaller pool, while volume, immediate reversals, and trade count enter positively; two-way clustered standard errors are reported (pp. 340–341). Liquidity is descriptively stable: only 33,256 same-wallet deposit/withdrawal reversals occur within 50 blocks, withdrawals are 1.02% of interactions, and approximately 17% of selected stablecoin-pool liquidity leaves around the May 2021 crash episode (pp. 341–343, Figure 9). Table III associates high-return days with USD 1.705 higher withdrawal gas costs and USD 17.73 higher pool fee revenue, using pool fixed effects and pool-clustered errors (p. 344). For 43 cross-listed pairs, relative price impact is defined as Binance impact divided by Uniswap impact minus one, using the median daily absolute one-minute price change per unit volume (equations (18)–(19), pp. 348–351); Table IV reports positive linear and negative squared trade-size coefficients and higher relative Binance impact when reversal volume is greater, while the March 2022 PancakeSwap indicator is negative in pool-fixed-effect specifications (p. 352). Table V similarly reports greater relative volatility of Binance price impact (pp. 351–353). These are prediction-consistent associations, not causal identification: volatility, volume, reversals, pool size, and venue choice are jointly determined; the reversal proxy may contain informed arbitrage as well as noise-trade correction; the price-impact measure mixes information-driven price changes with mechanical impact; and the PancakeSwap event dummy cannot isolate integration from contemporaneous time effects. The comparison also conditions on different custodial/use-value attributes, excludes each venue’s first 90 trading days and observations with pricing errors above 10%, lacks Binance quote-book data, and acknowledges venue sorting and different order-splitting incentives (pp. 346–348). The abstract’s “absence of long-lived arbitrage opportunities” is supported mainly by small mature-pool pricing discrepancies—0.19% mean absolute error for USDC/ETH after scale-up—and price plots, not a formal duration test (pp. 348–349, Figures 11–12). Theory boundaries include a single period, binary innovations, risk-neutral deep-pocketed suppliers, fixed liquidity-trade quantity, zero normalized capital opportunity cost, sufficiently small fees, deterministic constant-product pricing, and an approximation that separates fees from the pool with error stated to be of order τ² (pp. 328–334). Proposition 5 therefore establishes conditional model regions, especially low informed-trading probability, rather than general empirical dominance (pp. 345–346).
- Structure: The abstract and introduction establish scale, novelty, three institutional contrasts, contributions, findings, and literature position (pp. 321–325). Section I explains V2 liquidity tokens, swaps, fees, bonding curves, and pool-dependent depth (pp. 325–328, Figures 1–2, equations (1)–(5)). Section II builds the limit-order and AMM models, derives investment, pool-size, and arbitrage equilibria, and states Propositions 1–2 (pp. 328–334, Figures 3–5). Section III explains Ethereum execution, constructs the full V1/V2 data, and reports activity and volume facts (pp. 334–338, Table I, Figures 6–7). Section IV tests pool-size comparative statics and studies ordinary and crisis-period liquidity stability (pp. 339–344, Figure 8, Tables II–III, Figure 9). Section V derives venue-choice predictions and compares custody, access costs, pricing errors, price impact, impact volatility, and volume across Uniswap and Binance (pp. 344–354, Propositions 4–5, Figures 10–14, Tables IV–V). Section VI interprets AMMs as a redistribution of liquidity provision and price discovery (p. 354). Appendix A supplies proofs (pp. 355–363); Appendix B documents log reconstruction (pp. 363–365); Appendix C treats volume measurement, flash swaps, attacks, and fraudulent tokens (pp. 365–368); Appendix D maps the token-pool network (pp. 368–369); Appendix E gives numerical trading and arbitrage examples (pp. 369–371); Appendix F estimates gas costs (pp. 371–373); references and the supporting-information notice close the artifact (pp. 373–374).
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in the equilibrium model and full proofs (pp. 328–334 and 355–363), while institutional and measurement depth is strongest in the protocol mechanics, ordered blockchain-log reconstruction, attack-contaminated volume treatment, and gas calculations (pp. 325–328, 334–338, and Appendices B–F). Empirical specifications use large samples, fixed effects, clustered uncertainty, alternative noise proxies, a stated 20-day volatility check, and pre/post subsamples (pp. 340–353), but identification remains comparatively light: there is no instrument, discontinuity, matched causal design, order-book reconstruction, formal arbitrage-duration model, or demonstrated exogeneity of PancakeSwap integration. Replicability cannot be audited without the unread code companion.
- Breadth: The paper connects limit-order-book competition, precedence and pro-rata allocation, HFT investment, AMM theory, arbitrage, custody, blockchain execution, gas costs, flash swaps, oracle attacks, fraudulent tokens, and token-network structure (pp. 324–325 and Appendices B–F). It considers pool equilibrium, intraday and crisis liquidity, cross-venue pricing, price-impact levels and volatility, and limited access-cost comparability. Declared or evident boundaries are Ethereum Uniswap V1/V2 and Binance through December 2022; mature and sufficiently active pools; constant-product rather than concentrated-liquidity AMMs; 43 matched pairs for cross-venue tests; and observed viable pools satisfying the model’s parameter restrictions. Uniswap V3, endogenous fee tiers, concentrated liquidity, MEV and transaction ordering, multiple competing arbitrageurs, endogenous gas in the core model, liquidity-provider heterogeneity, dynamic capital allocation, and endogenous currency or routing-network choice are outside scope.
- Optics: The direct title and compact abstract combine institutional scale, equilibrium contribution, unusually large data, arbitrage evidence, and a conditional dominance claim (p. 321). The opening uses the success of a market without supplier-set prices as its motivating puzzle, then organizes the paper around three memorable AMM–limit-order differences before stating four empirical observations (pp. 322–324). The exhibit ladder is effective: Figures 1–5 teach mechanics and equilibrium; Table I and Figures 6–7 establish scale; Figure 8 and Table II test pool-size predictions; Figure 9 and Table III address stability; and Figure 10 through Tables IV–V deliver the venue comparison. Table captions generally define variables, units, samples, fixed effects, and clustering. Claim calibration is mixed: the empirical text often says “associated with” and acknowledges comparability problems (pp. 346–348), while the abstract and conclusion use stronger language about arbitrage absence and liquidity efficiency (pp. 321 and 354). Audit clarity is weakened by several internal presentation inconsistencies: Figure 9’s caption dates the crash May 19, 2020 while the text identifies May 19, 2021 (pp. 342–343); Appendix A labels the proof of Proposition 3 as “Proof of Corollary 3” (p. 359); Section V says the relative-impact function should be U-shaped although the stated mechanism and positive-linear/negative-quadratic estimates imply an inverted U (pp. 350–352); the prose interpretation of reversal volume in Table V does not cleanly match its positive coefficients (pp. 351 and 353); and Figure 13’s caption assigns green to both empirical and analytical series although the body identifies orange as empirical Uniswap impact (pp. 349–350). The displayed definition of ω also appears inconsistent between the main proposition/proof restatements and the derivation in equations (A10)–(A12), warranting consultation of the typeset equation or code before reusing the formula (pp. 333 and 358–361).

- Implication: For a DeFi vehicle-currency project, the defensible takeaway is a mechanism: lower pair volatility and greater liquidity-oriented flow can support a larger constant-product pool, which mechanically lowers predictable price impact, while WETH’s observed position as counterasset for 95.02% of pools supplies strong descriptive evidence of network centrality. The paper cannot by itself show that AMM design caused WETH to become a vehicle currency, that centrality is an equilibrium currency-selection outcome, that arbitrage opportunities are categorically absent, that PancakeSwap caused Binance’s measured improvement, or that its V1/V2 results generalize to V3 and other AMM designs. Any use of the exact equilibrium formula should first resolve the apparent ω inconsistency and inspect the replication code.
