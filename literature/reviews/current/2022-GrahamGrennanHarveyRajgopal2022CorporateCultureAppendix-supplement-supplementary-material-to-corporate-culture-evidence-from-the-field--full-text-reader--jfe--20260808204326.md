### Graham, Grennan, Harvey, and Rajgopal (2022), Corporate Culture: Evidence from the Field — Internet Appendices C–F

- Status: Supplied supplementary artifact read sequentially in full; paper-level source set remains incomplete because the published article and Internet Appendices A–B were not supplied or read.
- Roles: Independent second full-text reader; companion-source audit; scientific claim verification; JFE research-communication exemplar.
- Source: John R. Graham, Jillian Grennan, Campbell R. Harvey, and Shivaram Rajgopal, Corporate Culture: Evidence from the Field, 2022; saved file 2022-GrahamGrennanHarveyRajgopal2022CorporateCultureAppendix-supplement-supplementary-material-to-corporate-culture-evidence-from-the-field.pdf; 43 PDF pages, internally numbered 0–42.
- Version: Published supplementary material, Internet Appendices C–F, accompanying the Journal of Financial Economics article, volume 146 (2022), pp. 552–593; this artifact is not the standalone published article.
- Companions: Incomplete: Internet Appendices C–F are present and read; the main published article and repeatedly cited survey instrument and variable definitions in Internet Appendices A–B are missing and unread. The artifact provides no source-discovery record establishing whether corrections or corrigenda exist.
- Uses: Supports assessment of survey representativeness through Compustat benchmarking and response-bias analyses (Tables C.1–C.7, pp. 1–7; Appendix D, pp. 17–27); supports qualified external-validation claims using Glassdoor, company websites, Tobin’s Q, financial reporting, and other external outcomes (Tables C.8–C.16, pp. 8–16); supports claims about associations between values, norms, perceived cultural effectiveness, and self-reported outcomes (Appendix E, pp. 28–40); establishes the authors’ explicit limit that Q14 can test perceived nonzero effects but not positive effects or outcome levels (Appendix F, Eqs. F.1–F.2 and direct tests, pp. 41–42); supplies a JFE exemplar of a robustness-heavy internet appendix.
- Scientific: The supplement asks whether executive survey measures of culture are credible, whether participation or respondent composition explains them, and how values and day-to-day norms relate to perceived and externally measured outcomes. Data center on 1,348 executives at public and private North American firms, with matched external samples generally around 70–191 firms and within-survey regressions around 937–1,310 observations (Tables C.8–C.16, E.3–E.10). Measures include hand-coded cultural values from Q1/Q14, norms from Q6, cultural effectiveness from Q4b, and ordinal perceived outcomes from Q14; their full construction is deferred to missing Appendix B. The survey public-firm sample is substantially larger and more profitable than Compustat firms—employee-category mean 5.29 versus 3.68 and revenue-category mean 5.00 versus 3.94—while credit rating, leverage, ROE, and CEO tenure are closer (Table C.1, p. 1). External validation is selective: Glassdoor text predicts adaptability, collaboration, and results orientation in the baseline but mainly adaptability and collaboration with fuller controls (Table C.8, p. 8); survey–website value mismatch predicts worse reported alignment (Table C.9, p. 9); and halo-controlled Q4b predicts Tobin’s Q by 0.389–0.440 and Total Q by 0.361–0.504 across horizons (Table C.16, pp. 16). Within-survey OLS shows aggregate norms positively associated with all, innovation, and productivity/value outcomes, while aggregate values enter negatively; the values-by-norms interaction is 0.28 for the aggregate outcome (Table E.4, p. 34). Perceived cultural effectiveness predicts aggregate, ethics, and productivity/value outcomes but not innovation (Table E.5 Panel A, p. 35); values, norms, and leadership predict effectiveness (Table E.5 Panel B), and ten-fold cross-validation reports mean absolute percentage errors of 12% for all outcomes and 17% for single-channel effectiveness models (Table E.7, p. 37). These are descriptive, predictive, and mechanism-consistent associations, not causal identification: there is no exogenous variation, temporal design, or instrument, and cross-validation tests prediction rather than mechanism. Common-method covariance, executive optimism, selection, reverse causality, and correlated omitted management quality remain credible readings. Halo specifications, placebos, source restrictions, and response-bias splits reduce but do not eliminate those alternatives (Tables C.13–C.16, pp. 13–16; Appendix D). Reported significance generally assumes single tests despite many outcomes and specifications. Appendix F’s 0.76–0.99 direct-test means show that executives usually report some effect, but the authors expressly concede that the effect’s sign is unobserved and a positive-effect hypothesis is not testable (pp. 41–42).
- Structure: Appendix C opens directly with benchmarking, demographics, correlations, ownership and size splits, then moves through external validation, placebo tests, and halo controls (pp. 0–16). Appendix D states the participation-bias agenda, presents timing and job-title figures, and compares respondents across invitation universe, title, firm size, email source, cultural influence, and email-identification status (pp. 17–27). Appendix E first motivates the limitations of within-survey analysis, then progresses from univariate outcome splits to specific multivariate associations, aggregate interactions, a two-step effectiveness channel, variable selection, cross-validation, restricted samples, and alternative outcomes (pp. 28–40). Appendix F closes with a formal interpretation of Q14, two linear equations, direct tests of perceived nonzero effects, and a proof-based explanation of why sign claims are unavailable (pp. 41–42).
- Depth: Effort is concentrated in measurement validation and robustness: nine external-validation/placebo/halo tables (C.8–C.16, pp. 8–16), two figures and eight selection tables (D.1–D.8, pp. 18–27), and ten business-outcome tables (E.1–E.10, pp. 31–40). Institutional and mechanism depth is moderate: Appendix E connects norms to intrinsic motivation and expectation alignment and separates culture’s determinants from perceived effectiveness and outcomes (pp. 29–30; Tables E.5–E.6, pp. 35–36). Identification depth is light because all designs remain observational, and measurement auditability is impaired by the absent survey questions and definitions in Appendices A–B. Statistical depth includes clustered or heteroskedasticity-robust errors, industry fixed effects, ridge/LASSO, and cross-validation, but no systematic multiple-testing correction or causal sensitivity analysis.
- Breadth: The artifact covers public versus private ownership, family ownership, firm size, title, email source, response timing, cultural influence, and willingness to identify the firm (Tables C.4–C.7, pp. 4–7; Appendix D, pp. 17–27). It spans ethics, innovation, reporting, risk, leverage, growth, productivity, profitability, and value, and draws external measures from Glassdoor, websites, Compustat, Audit Analytics, patents, KLD, Sustainalytics, brand rankings, Best Places to Work, and RepRisk (Tables C.8–C.16, pp. 8–16). The theoretical discussion invokes intrinsic motivation, expectation alignment, organizational coordination, and values–norms complementarity (pp. 28–30). Declared boundaries are unusually explicit: within-survey inference is conceptually and statistically difficult (p. 28), Q14 may encode a perceived slope rather than an outcome level (p. 41), and the sign of the effect cannot be tested (p. 42). Generalizability beyond surveyed North American executives and the 2015 survey setting is not established here.
- Optics: The artifact has no title page, abstract, or conventional opening; it starts with “Supplementary Material: Internet Appendices C–F” on p. 0. Its exhibit ladder is reviewer-facing and cumulative: sample comparability, external validity, selection checks, self-reported mechanisms, then formal interpretive limits. Captions are generally self-contained about samples, controls, standard errors, and question numbers, though nearly every table depends on missing Appendix B definitions. Claim calibration is strongest where Appendix E calls results associations and Appendix F denies sign identification, but phrases such as “support the theoretical argument” and the direct rejection that culture has “no effect” can read more strongly than respondent perceptions warrant (pp. 29, 41–42). Prose is dense but functional, with 17 pages allocated to Appendix C, 11 to D, 13 to E, and 2 to F. Credibility is weakened by copyediting and method-description defects: Table E.6 says ridge regression pushes coefficients exactly to zero, a property associated with LASSO rather than ridge, and its selection labels are consequently difficult to audit (p. 36); several captions contain question-number inconsistencies, malformed spacing, or “single test” significance language despite a large specification family. Mathematical notation is confined to Eqs. F.1–F.2 and is legible conceptually despite typesetting defects (pp. 41–42).

F.1–F.2 and the direct-test exhibit, pp. 41–42.
- Implication: Use this artifact as evidence that the authors conducted extensive representativeness, external-validation, placebo, halo, prediction, and internal-consistency analyses, and as evidence for associations in which norms appear more informative than stated values. Do not use it to claim that culture causally raises firm value or business performance, that Q14 identifies a positive effect, or that external validation is uniformly successful. Any paper-level or venue-exemplar closure must await the published article and Internet Appendices A–B.
