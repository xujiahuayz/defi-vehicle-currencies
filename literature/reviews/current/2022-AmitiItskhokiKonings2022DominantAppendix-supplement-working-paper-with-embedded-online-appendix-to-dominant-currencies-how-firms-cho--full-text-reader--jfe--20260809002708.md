- Uses: Supports the descriptive claim that euro and dollar invoicing coexist as dominant currencies in Belgian extra-EU trade (Section 3.2, Table 1, Figure 1, PDF pp. 16–18); supports the conditional firm-level claim that non-euro export invoicing rises with foreign-input exposure, firm size, and competitors’ foreign-currency use (Section 4, Table 2, pp. 20–23; Table 4, pp. 25–27); supports the mechanism-consistent claim that invoicing currency predicts persistent differential ERPT after controlling for import intensity and size (Section 5.1, Tables 5–6, pp. 27–31; Section 5.2, Figure 3, pp. 31–34); and supports delayed allocative quantity responses, not an immediate quantity effect (Equation 18 and Figure 4, pp. 35–36; Table B7, p. 55).
- Scientific: The paper asks how firms select invoicing currency and whether that choice changes the response of export prices and quantities to exchange rates. Its theoretical contribution combines heterogeneous firms, variable markups, imported inputs, strategic complementarities, staggered price setting, and endogenous currency choice: firms choose the currency minimizing variance of the desired price in that currency (Lemmas 1–2 and Equations 1–5, PDF pp. 7–9); desired ERPT depends on imported-input exposure and competitor-price sensitivity (Lemma 3 and Equations 6–9, pp. 9–11); and a Calvo model maps long-difference coefficients to non-adjustment probability through Equations 10–11 (p. 12; derivation in Online Appendix A, pp. 43–45). Data merge Belgian customs invoicing records for extra-EU firm-product-country-month transactions from March 2017–December 2020 with trade, accounts, VAT, employment, FDI, and R&D records; annual ERPT analysis spans 2012–2020 (Section 3.1, pp. 14–15; Online Appendix B, pp. 46–47). Descriptively, dollars account for 51.4% and euros 34.9% of export value; corresponding import shares are 51.9% and 42.5% (Table 1, p. 16). In the preferred currency-choice IV specification, import intensity is 0.308 (SE 0.112), log employment 0.089 (0.015), and competitors’ foreign-currency share 0.536 (0.150); the first-stage Cragg–Donald statistic is 1,865.4 and Hansen p-value 0.567 (Table 2, column 5, p. 21). Annual ERPT estimates imply approximately complete euro pass-through for small, non-importing euro-pricers; foreign invoicing changes euro ERPT by −0.383 (0.037), dollar invoicing changes dollar ERPT by 0.411 (0.041), and import-intensity interactions are −0.257 (0.080) and 0.328 (0.075) (Table 5, column 1, p. 29). Dynamic estimates are consistent with monthly Calvo non-adjustment near 0.90, an average ten-month price duration, 28% of prices unadjusted after twelve months, and 8% after twenty-four months (Figure 3 discussion, pp. 32–34). Quantity effects are insignificant at short horizons and become negative later; at 24 months Table B7 reports elasticities of −1.104 (0.331) using all instruments and −1.337 (0.357) using sticky-price instruments only (p. 55). The evidence strongly establishes description and conditional prediction and is consistent with the proposed sticky-price mechanism, but mechanism discrimination and causal identification are incomplete: actual invoicing currency is not instrumented in the ERPT regressions, and Table 6’s comparison of actual choices with predicted choices or proxies does not itself create exogenous currency assignment (pp. 30–31). Identification instead assumes that residual cost and markup shocks are orthogonal to currency choice after controls (p. 28). Credible alternatives include unobserved firm-market traits jointly affecting invoicing and exchange-rate sensitivity, unit-value composition changes, and direct quantity effects of the exchange-rate interactions used as price instruments. Further boundaries are that invoicing is observed only for extra-EU trade, currency choices from 2017–2020 are extrapolated backward for the 2012–2016 ERPT sample, prices are unit values rather than observed posted-price spells, observations with price ratios outside one-third to three are removed, and competitor measures omit non-Belgian firms (pp. 14–15, 20, 22, 27).
- Structure: The cover and abstract state the question, findings, and publication claim (PDF p. 1); Section 1 motivates two identification problems, previews four result families, locates the paper in invoicing and ERPT literatures, and supplies a roadmap (pp. 2–6); Section 2 builds the static currency-choice model, desired and realized pass-through equations, dynamic Calvo interpretation, and fixed-cost, global-value-chain, and financing extensions (pp. 6–13); Section 3 describes data and variables and establishes aggregate, bilateral, and variance-decomposition facts (pp. 14–19); Section 4 estimates export currency choice, strategic complementarities, additional determinants, robustness, and vehicle-versus-local currency choice (pp. 19–27); Section 5 estimates annual ERPT, probes selection versus direct invoicing effects, traces dynamic price adjustment, calibrates price duration, and estimates quantity responses (pp. 27–36); Section 6 draws macroeconomic and dominant-currency counterfactual implications (pp. 36–37); the article appendix supplies additional figures and descriptive tables (pp. 38–39), followed by references (pp. 40–42); the embedded Online Appendix supplies derivations, data definitions, import-invoicing analysis, summary statistics, robustness, IV first stages, and quantity-regression stages (pp. 43–55).
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in the mapping from currency choice to desired and realized ERPT (Section 2.1–2.3, PDF pp. 7–12) and its full Calvo derivation (Online Appendix A, pp. 43–45). Identification effort is concentrated in competitor-currency IV designs (Section 4, pp. 22–23; Table B4, p. 52), actual-versus-predicted invoicing tests (Table 6, pp. 30–31), within-industry-destination-time comparisons (pp. 29, 32, 35), and the two-stage quantity design (Equation 18, pp. 35–36; Table B7, p. 55). Robustness is extensive across product types, markets, fixed effects, instruments, controls, and samples (pp. 25, 29–31, 33–34; Tables B3–B7, pp. 51–55). Measurement receives useful variable definitions and coverage information (Section 3.1 and Online Appendix B, pp. 14–15, 46–47) but lighter validation: unit values are acknowledged as noisy, price spells are not observed, customs suppression and incomplete currency coverage receive limited diagnostics, and the backward extrapolation of invoicing is defended mainly by persistence. Institutional detail about contracting, bargaining, hedging, and who selects invoice currency remains comparatively thin.
- Breadth: The paper spans producer-, local-, and dominant-currency pricing; currency choice, ERPT, price rigidity, strategic complementarities, imported-input hedging, variable markups, global value chains, financing, and expenditure switching. It connects open-economy policy, endogenous invoicing, micro-price, trade elasticity, and dominant-currency literatures in the introduction and references (PDF pp. 5–6, 40–42). Alternative mechanisms are explicitly introduced through currency-specific fixed costs, uniform pricing across destinations, GVC participation, and financing constraints (Section 2.4, pp. 13; Table 3 and discussion, pp. 23–25; Table B5, p. 53), while import invoicing is treated separately in Online Appendix C (pp. 48–49). Addressed objections include macro confounding, selection, simultaneity in competitor choices, omitted flexible-price determinants, destination exchange-rate regimes, product differentiation, and short-horizon timing error. Declared or evident boundaries include Belgian manufacturing and extra-EU currency records, weak within-firm currency switching, incomplete foreign-competitor measures, no financing-currency data, no direct price-adjustment observations, and no estimated general-equilibrium counterfactual (pp. 14–15, 18–20, 24–25, 27, 33–34, 37).
- Optics: The title uses a broad phenomenon followed by a direct “how” and “why it matters” subtitle; the abstract immediately joins choice determinants to price and quantity consequences (PDF p. 1). The opening poses a policy-relevant question, states confounding and selection obstacles, introduces the unusual Belgian data, and previews four results before the literature review (pp. 2–5). The exhibit ladder is effective: aggregate currency shares and bilateral patterns (Table 1, Figure 1), firm-size gradients and variance decomposition (Figure 2, Table A1), currency-choice regressions (Tables 2–4), annual causal-interpretation checks (Tables 5–6), dynamic coefficients (Figure 3), and allocative quantities (Figure 4), with robustness deferred to the online appendix. Captions are unusually self-contained about samples, variables, fixed effects, clustering, and interpretation. Notation is internally coherent but dense, especially where several desired-pass-through components and currency indicators enter Equations 8–11 and 16–17. Prose is compact and signposted, with substantial page allocation to theory and identification rather than institutional description. Reviewer-facing credibility is strengthened by reporting first-stage strength, overidentification tests, alternative instruments, multiple fixed-effect structures, and deviations from the calibrated Calvo model. Claim calibration is weakest where the abstract and introduction call invoicing’s effect “direct causal” and say quantity results “establish” allocative effects (pp. 1, 3–4): the later empirical argument is careful, but the observational assignment of actual invoicing currency does not fully warrant those formulations.

- Implication: Use this paper as strong evidence that invoicing currency is a persistent, heterogeneous firm-level correlate of exchange-rate transmission and that its predicted price effects decay gradually while quantity effects emerge slowly. For load-bearing causal prose, qualify the result as a structurally interpreted, selection-controlled comparison within common exchange-rate environments, not exogenous or randomized assignment of invoice currency. The clearest defensible contribution is the joint theory-and-data decomposition of sticky- and flexible-price ERPT determinants, together with delayed quantity adjustment.
