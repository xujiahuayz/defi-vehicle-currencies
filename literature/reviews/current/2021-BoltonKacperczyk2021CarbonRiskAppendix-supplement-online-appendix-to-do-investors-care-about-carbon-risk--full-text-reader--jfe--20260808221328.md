### Bolton and Kacperczyk (2020), Do Investors Care about Carbon Risk?

- Companions: Incomplete: the declared Online Appendix is embedded and fully read at PDF pp. 68–88 (manuscript pp. 65–85, Tables A.1–A.15); the separate 2021 published article was not supplied or read, and no correction or corrigendum can be ruled out from this artifact alone. An additional imputed-emission-growth analysis is described as “available upon request” rather than reported (PDF p. 32, manuscript p. 29 n. 27).
- Uses: Supports the descriptive claim that, from 2005–2017, higher firm-level emission totals and annual changes predict higher monthly U.S. stock returns after extensive controls and fixed effects (Section 3.2, equation 1, PDF pp. 22–24; Table 8, PDF pp. 55–57), while emission intensity generally does not; supports the narrower claim that aggregate institutional ownership is negatively associated with scope-1 emission intensity, concentrated in salient industries and especially investment advisers, insurance companies, and pensions (Sections 3.4–3.5, equation 3, PDF pp. 26–29; Tables 11 and 13, PDF pp. 62 and 64); supports a nonpublished-version communication exemplar through the hypothesis-led introduction, measurement section, and exhibit ladder. It does not support a causal statement that investors demand compensation for carbon risk or a closed claim about the final JFE publication.
- Scientific: The question is whether corporate carbon emissions enter the cross-section of stock returns and institutional holdings. The contribution is to separate total emissions, year-over-year changes, and sales-scaled intensity across scopes 1, 2, and upstream 3, then connect those measures to returns, factor exposures, ownership, industry salience, and investor awareness. The proposed interpretations are a carbon-risk premium, underpricing or “carbon alpha,” and divestment-induced limited risk sharing (Introduction, PDF pp. 6–10). Data combine Trucost EDX emissions with FactSet returns, fundamentals, and ownership for 3,421 matched U.S.-listed firms over 2005–2017 (Section 2, PDF p. 13); scope 3 is input-output-model estimated, downstream scope-3 emissions are excluded, and missing scope-1/2 values may also be estimated (Section 2.1, PDF pp. 13–15). Main pooled regressions include firm predictors, year-month effects, and sometimes industry effects, with standard errors clustered by firm and year (equation 1 and Table 8); reported one-standard-deviation monthly return effects for emission levels and changes are approximately 15/26 basis points for scope 1, 24/18 for scope 2, and 33/31 for scope 3 (PDF p. 9, manuscript p. 6). Listed asset-pricing factors do not absorb the average coefficients (equation 2 and Table 10, PDF pp. 25–26 and 59–61), but this rejects only those factor explanations, not omitted risk, characteristics, preferences, or mispricing. Identification is observational and predictive rather than causal: emissions correlate with size, tangible capital, leverage, profitability, and industry (Table 7, PDF p. 54); the level premium disappears under reduced controls and reappears only with richer controls or industry adjustment (Table A.2, PDF pp. 68–70); many results are stronger among firms whose emissions Trucost estimates, while disclosed-firm scope-1 and scope-2 growth coefficients are insignificant (Table A.11, PDF p. 83). The timing exercise shows coefficients attenuating around 6–12 months but cannot establish actual information-availability dates (Table A.3, PDF pp. 70–74). Paris-period evidence is mixed: only the scope-1-level treatment interaction is robust in Table 15, matched groups remain imbalanced on size, book-to-market, ROE, and PPE in Table A.7, and the post-2015 premium disappears among legacy firms in Table A.6 (PDF pp. 66, 79–80). The 1990s comparison depends on back-casting emissions from later intensities and contemporaneous sales (Section 3.6 and Table 16, PDF pp. 31–32 and 67). Credible alternative readings therefore include emissions proxying for omitted expected-return characteristics, sample-provider coverage, estimated-emissions structure, or industry composition; the evidence does not discriminate cleanly among risk compensation, tastes, attention, and residual misspecification.
- Structure: PDF pp. 1–4 provide ECGI covers and repeated abstracts; Section 1 frames policy salience, develops three hypotheses, previews results, and locates the paper in climate-finance research (PDF pp. 5–12); Section 2 defines the matched sample, emission construction, return factors, and ownership variables (PDF pp. 13–20); Section 3.1 models emission determinants (PDF pp. 20–21); Section 3.2 presents the core return regressions and timing and earnings-announcement checks (PDF pp. 21–25); Section 3.3 tests standard factors (PDF pp. 25–26); Sections 3.4–3.5 examine divestment and coarse industry categorization (PDF pp. 26–29); Section 3.6 studies Paris and imputed 1990s comparisons (PDF pp. 29–32); Section 3.7 summarizes robustness (PDF pp. 33–34); Section 4 concludes and is followed by references (PDF pp. 35–36); Figures 1–7 occupy PDF pp. 37–46, Tables 1–16 PDF pp. 47–67, and Online Appendix Tables A.1–A.15 PDF pp. 68–88.
- Depth: Effort is concentrated in variable construction and empirical specification proliferation: three emissions scopes crossed with levels, changes, and intensity; returns, factor-premium, and ownership outcomes; industry and state effects; salient-industry exclusions; Paris splits and matching; disclosed-versus-estimated samples; crisis exclusion; alternative industries; and legacy firms (Tables 7–16 and A.1–A.15). Institutional and measurement discussion is substantial for the Greenhouse Gas Protocol and Trucost scope-3 model (Section 2.1), but validation against alternative emissions vendors is cited rather than performed. Theory is light and supplies interpretations rather than testable separating restrictions. Identification depth is limited: there is no exogenous firm-level emissions shock, instrument, tradable carbon factor, disclosure-date design, or direct measure of investor beliefs; inference also relies in several core specifications on only 13 year clusters.
- Breadth: The paper spans cross-sectional asset pricing, climate finance, environmental disclosure, sin stocks, institutional ownership, bounded attention, and sparse categorization (Introduction and literature review, PDF pp. 5–12). It considers regulatory, carbon-price, fossil-energy, renewable-technology, underpricing, ethical-screening, coarse-industry, and awareness mechanisms. Breadth is narrowed by U.S.-listed Trucost-covered firms, 2005–2017, upstream-only scope 3, annual emissions mapped to monthly returns, and provider-estimated observations; it does not study international pricing, bonds, realized regulatory losses, downstream product-use emissions, investor flows, voting or engagement, or welfare. The claimed systematic-risk interpretation is explicitly left unresolved (PDF p. 6), and the “available upon request” growth-imputation result is not auditable from the artifact.
- Optics: The interrogative title and compact abstract foreground one empirical question, three main results, and a calibrated “consistent with” interpretation (PDF pp. 1–4). The opening moves from Paris and institutional engagement to three named hypotheses before presenting magnitudes and the literature, a strong reader-orientation strategy (PDF pp. 5–12). The exhibit ladder is intelligible: descriptive emissions and coverage figures, cumulative premium plots, summary and correlation tables, core returns, factor tests, ownership, industry exclusions, awareness, and robustness. Captions are generally self-contained, and equations 1–4 anchor the designs, but all exhibits are back-loaded after the references, making verification cumbersome. The conclusion’s statement that investors “are pricing in carbon risk” is stronger than the observational design warrants (PDF p. 35). Reviewer-facing credibility is weakened by internal presentation inconsistencies: equation 1/Table 8 are described as pooled OLS, whereas Section 3.3 calls their period-by-period coefficients Fama–MacBeth estimates (PDF pp. 22 and 25); the introduction names mutual funds among divestors while Table 11 instead identifies advisers and shows an insignificant investment-company coefficient (PDF pp. 9–10 and 62); salient motor-industry codes vary across Section 3.5, Tables 12–13, and Figure 7 (PDF pp. 28, 46, 63–64); Figure 4–6 captions point to Table 7 controls although Table 8 is the return specification; Table A.4 contains panel-description errors; Table A.13 is titled “Salient Industries” but says the sample excludes them; and Table 16’s caption calls the whole sample 1990–1999 although Panel A covers 2005–2017.

- Implication: The artifact is strong evidence for a robust conditional association between carbon-emission totals or changes and subsequent/contemporaneous stock returns in the covered 2005–2017 sample, plus coarse scope-1-intensity screening in institutional portfolios. It should be cited as descriptive or predictive evidence, not as causal identification of a carbon-risk price or proof that investors consciously demand compensation. Any venue-exemplar, final-estimate, or published-versus-working-paper use must await the 2021 JFE version and any associated correction record; manuscript drafting should retain the hypothesis-led architecture and exhibit sequence but tighten claim calibration and repair the numerous caption, coding-label, and method-description inconsistencies.
