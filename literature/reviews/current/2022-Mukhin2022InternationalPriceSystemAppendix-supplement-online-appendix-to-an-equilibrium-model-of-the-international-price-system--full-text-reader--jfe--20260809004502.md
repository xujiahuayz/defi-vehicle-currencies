- Scientific: The scientific question is how firms’ invoicing-currency choices and equilibrium price pass-through can generate dominance and persistence of a vehicle currency. The core mechanism is that, under a second-order profit approximation, a firm chooses the currency minimizing variation in its desired price expressed in that currency (Lemma 1 and equation A11, pp. 3–4); desired prices depend on marginal costs, destination prices, input-output linkages, and exchange rates, while invoicing choices feed back into aggregate price indices, creating strategic complementarity. Equations A9–A13 and Proposition proofs on pp. 3–5 connect equilibrium pass-through to producer-, local-, and dollar-currency pricing; Corollary 1, pp. 5–7, derives a transition between vehicle currencies; Proposition A1, pp. 7–8, establishes symmetric-equilibrium existence and instability of mixed equilibria under restrictive parameter conditions; and Proposition A2, pp. 8–10, distinguishes exporter dollar pricing from domestic dollarization. The quantitative model combines 63 economies and 32 industries using ICIO input-output tables for 1995, 2005, and 2015, IMF IFS monthly exchange rates principally over 1980–2015, IMF invoicing shares, and Swiss partner-product invoicing data (Sections B.2.1–B.2.4, pp. 15–18). Identification is structural calibration and simulation, not reduced-form or causal identification; model-data concordance in Figures A7–A11, pp. 34–37, is descriptive discipline and does not discriminate causally against alternative invoicing mechanisms. Model-derived mean pass-through statistics are reported in Tables A3–A4, pp. 24–27, and simulated country and sector currency shares in Tables A5–A6, pp. 28–30, without standard errors, confidence intervals, posterior uncertainty, or formal fit statistics. Important boundaries include second-order/local approximations (pp. 3–4, 11), flexible- or fully sticky-price limits and symmetry restrictions in several analytical results (pp. 4–10), complete markets and random-walk exchange rates in the quantitative system (p. 10), selected admissible vehicle currencies and domestic local-currency pricing in the algorithm (p. 14), sparse invoicing coverage and settlement-versus-invoicing measurement (pp. 17–18), and counterfactual assumptions of unchanged trade costs and current-account imbalances plus common within-country sectoral growth (p. 20).
- Structure: Section A.1, pp. 1–3, states the baseline equilibrium, steady state, price indices, and pass-through; Section A.2, pp. 3–7, proves Lemmas 1, 2, and 4, Propositions 1–3, Corollary 1, and an existence/uniqueness lemma; Section A.3.1, pp. 7–8, treats multiple equilibria and stability; Section A.3.2, pp. 8–10, extends currency choice to domestic firms. Section B.1, pp. 10–14, constructs the full multicountry-multisector model, derives the dynamic currency-choice statistic and NKPC system, and gives the numerical solution algorithm. Section B.2, pp. 15–18, documents trade flows, exchange rates, remaining parameters, and invoicing data. Section B.3, pp. 18–20, develops the gravity/hat-algebra counterfactual and calibration. Section B.4, pp. 21–37, supplies six tables and nine additional figures.
