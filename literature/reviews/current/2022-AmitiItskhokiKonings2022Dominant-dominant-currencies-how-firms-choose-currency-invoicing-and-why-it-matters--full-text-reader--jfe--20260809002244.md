- Uses: Supports the descriptive claim that the dollar and euro jointly dominate Belgian extra-EU invoicing, with the dollar carrying 51.4% of export value and 51.9% of import value and the euro 34.9% and 42.5%, respectively (Section III.B, Table I, pp. 1456–1458; Figure I, p. 1459); supports the predictive claim that foreign-input exposure, firm size, and competitors’ invoicing choices predict firms’ departure from euro invoicing (Section IV, equation (15), Table II, pp. 1462–1467), and that dollar-invoiced imports predict dollar export invoicing whereas larger firms favor destination currency over the dollar among non-euro choices (Section IV, Table IV, pp. 1471–1473); supports, subject to the paper’s conditional-identification assumptions, the claim that invoicing currency predicts persistent differential ERPT beyond flexible-price determinants (Section V.A, equations (16)–(17), Tables V–VI, pp. 1473–1482); supports the claim that the associated quantity response is delayed and becomes economically meaningful after roughly 12–18 months (Section V.B.2, equation (18), Figure IV, pp. 1484–1486). It does not directly support claims about digital currencies, stablecoins, DeFi adoption, monetary-network displacement, or causal currency switching after a regime change.
- Scientific: The paper asks how exporters choose invoicing currency and whether that choice changes the response of export prices and quantities to exchange rates. Its contribution is to connect a unified endogenous-currency-choice model to matched Belgian firm, trade, invoicing, cost, employment, and balance-sheet data. Equations (1)–(12), pp. 1443–1453, derive currency choice as minimizing desired-price variance in the invoicing currency and decompose realized ERPT into sticky-price effects of invoicing and flexible-price effects of imported-input exposure and strategic complementarities. Currency data cover firm–CN8-product–country–month transactions outside the EU from March 2017 through December 2020; annual and monthly price/quantity analyses cover 2012–2020, with prices measured as destination-currency unit values and large annual price changes outside one-third to three dropped (Section III.A, equations (13)–(14), pp. 1453–1456). Table II reports that ex-eurozone import intensity predicts non-euro export invoicing (0.286–0.411 in the main specifications), log employment predicts it strongly (about 0.085–0.089), and instrumented competitor foreign-currency use has a coefficient of 0.536, falling to 0.364 with a positive large-firm interaction; instruments use other Belgian exporters’ import intensity/employment and non-Belgian import shares from the United States, China, and dollar pegs (pp. 1463–1467). Table IV shows dollar import intensity predicting dollar rather than destination-currency invoicing (roughly 0.526–0.705), while log employment predicts destination-currency use (roughly −0.080 to −0.095; pp. 1471–1473). In annual ERPT regressions, the baseline euro ERPT is 1.007; import intensity lowers euro ERPT by 0.257 and raises dollar ERPT by 0.328; foreign-currency invoicing lowers euro ERPT by 0.383 and dollar invoicing raises dollar ERPT by 0.411 (Table V, column 1, pp. 1475–1477). Dynamic estimates show euro invoicers near complete euro ERPT at all horizons, non-euro invoicers rising from about 0.40 at four months to 0.65 at 24 months, and dollar invoicers’ dollar ERPT declining from nearly 0.60 to just above 0.30 (Figure III, pp. 1481–1483). A Calvo calibration of λ≈0.90 implies an average ten-month price duration, 28% unadjusted after 12 months, and 8% after 24 months; this is structural interpretation rather than direct observation of price spells (pp. 1482–1484). Quantity 2SLS estimates are negative but small and insignificant below one year, exceed unity after about 18 months for all goods, approach 1.5 in the long run, and exceed 2 by 24 months for differentiated goods (Figure IV, pp. 1484–1486). Uncertainty is reported through clustered standard errors in tables and 95% confidence bands in Figures III–IV. The currency-choice results are predictive except where competitor choice is instrumented; the ERPT treatment interpretation relies on conditional orthogonality of invoicing to unobserved firm-specific shocks after controls, fixed effects, and common exchange-rate environments, not randomized or instrumented invoicing. Table VI’s proxy-versus-realized-choice comparisons are supportive but do not eliminate unobserved selection. Further limitations are unit values rather than transaction prices, Belgian rather than total destination-market shares, extra-EU coverage only, currency choices observed only from 2017 and extrapolated backward for 2012–2016, rare observed currency switching, possible direct effects of exchange-rate interactions on quantities that would challenge the second-stage exclusion restriction, and a structural Calvo interpretation that fits less well at short horizons. Credible alternative readings are therefore that observed invoicing partly proxies for persistent contracting, customer, product, hedging, or supply-chain traits, and that delayed quantities reflect relationships, delivery contracts, or measurement aggregation rather than a uniquely identified quantity-adjustment friction.
- Structure: Section I (pp. 1435–1442) frames two identification challenges, previews four results, establishes macroeconomic relevance, positions the paper in invoicing/ERPT and dominant-currency literatures, and gives the roadmap; Section II.A–II.D (pp. 1442–1453) moves from desired and preset prices to endogenous currency choice, flexible versus realized pass-through, Calvo dynamics, and additional fixed-cost/financing mechanisms; Section III.A (pp. 1453–1456) defines the matched data, sample, firm characteristics, unit-value outcome, and peg classification; Section III.B (pp. 1456–1460) establishes descriptive currency shares, bilateral patterns, and variance decomposition; Section IV (pp. 1460–1473) progresses from size-bin evidence to baseline firm-level currency-choice regressions, IV evidence on strategic complementarities, additional determinants, robustness, and vehicle-versus-local currency choice; Section V.A (pp. 1473–1480) estimates annual ERPT, separates sticky- and flexible-price terms, and examines causal interpretation versus selection; Section V.B (pp. 1480–1486) estimates nonparametric ERPT dynamics, maps them to a Calvo model, and instruments prices to estimate quantity responses; Section VI (pp. 1487–1488) interprets the findings through dollar persistence and possible multicurrency transitions; the printed appendix (pp. 1488–1489) supplies two supplementary figures and three descriptive tables; supplementary-material/data statements and references occupy pp. 1490–1493.
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in Section II’s mapping from desired-price covariances to currency choice and from observed pass-through coefficients to sticky- versus flexible-price mechanisms, especially equations (4), (8)–(11). Institutional and measurement depth is strongest in Section III.A’s linkage of customs, VAT, social-security, annual-account, and trade records, but direct price spells, contractual bargaining, hedging currency, customer identity, and non-Belgian competitor characteristics are unavailable. Identification effort is concentrated in the competitor-choice IV design (Section IV, Table II), common-industry–destination–time comparisons (Table V, column 4; equation (18)), and predicted-versus-realized invoicing tests (Table VI); identification of the invoicing treatment itself remains assumption-based. Robustness is extensive by the article’s references to alternative products, firm sizes, fixed effects, samples, exchange-rate regimes, macro controls, and first-stage tests, but most supporting detail resides in the unread Online Appendix. Quantity identification is comparatively lighter than price identification and depends on the exclusion of exchange-rate-by-firm-characteristic instruments from quantities except through measured unit values.
- Breadth: The paper spans endogenous invoicing, dominant-currency pricing, strategic complementarities, imported-input cost exposure, variable markups, sticky prices, global value chains, financial constraints, exchange-rate pass-through, and trade elasticities. It explicitly compares PCP, LCP, and DCP; considers fixed costs, uniform pricing across destinations, FDI, R&D, productivity, balance-sheet channels, dollar pegs, differentiated versus homogeneous products, and imported versus exported currency use (Sections II.D, III.B, IV). It addresses selection, simultaneity/reflection, omitted dollar exchange rates, common macro environments, price-duration heterogeneity, measurement error, and sluggish quantity adjustment. Declared boundaries include incomplete currency data for intra-EU trade, no direct price-duration observations, no currency-of-financing data, Belgian competitors as proxies for the full competitive set, and no general-equilibrium counterfactual; the conclusion explicitly leaves such counterfactual analysis to a model combining these estimates with general equilibrium (pp. 1487–1488).
- Optics: The title poses both the decision and consequence clearly; the abstract compresses data novelty, firm-level determinants, a causal claim, dynamic persistence, and quantity implications into one result-dense paragraph (p. 1435). The opening immediately states the causal and selection obstacles, then answers them through a unified data-and-theory pitch before presenting four numbered contributions (pp. 1435–1440). Claim calibration is generally careful for descriptive and predictive evidence but strongest causal wording exceeds what the non-random invoicing design alone establishes. The exhibit ladder is disciplined: aggregate shares (Table I), bilateral visualization (Figure I), size bins (Figure II), currency-choice regressions (Tables II–IV), annual ERPT and selection probes (Tables V–VI), dynamic ERPT (Figure III), then allocative quantities (Figure IV). Captions are unusually self-contained and define samples, variables, fixed effects, clustering, and graphical encodings. Notation is dense but deliberately reused from theory in empirical equations; prose is formal, compressed, and mechanism-led. Roughly 11 pages go to theory, 7 to data/stylized facts, 13 to currency choice, and 14 to ERPT/quantities, signaling that identification and dynamic consequences carry the paper. Reviewer-facing credibility comes from explicit threats, structural coefficient mappings, instrument diagnostics, fixed-effect saturation, sample splits, and acknowledged deviations from Calvo dynamics, although reliance on the separate Online Appendix makes the published article alone non-self-contained for audit.

- Implication: The durable scientific takeaway is that dominant-currency use can be a persistent equilibrium outcome generated jointly by input-cost exposure and strategic complementarity, while invoicing units of account materially organize short- and medium-run price adjustment and later expenditure switching. This makes the paper strong evidence for firm-level coordination and propagation mechanisms behind incumbent vehicle currencies, but only indirect background for new currency technologies or prospective dominant-currency transitions. Any project should attribute the firm-choice and ERPT evidence specifically to Belgian extra-EU trade, label the causal interpretation as conditional on observables and orthogonality assumptions, and avoid converting the conclusion’s dollar/renminbi scenarios into estimated counterfactual results.
