### Chen and Duffie (2021), “Market Fragmentation”

- Uses: Supports the possibility claim that visible fragmentation across disconnected lit exchanges can improve allocation even while worsening each exchange’s depth, through order splitting and isolation of cross-exchange price impacts (abstract and introduction, pp. 2247–2249; Theorem 1(vii), p. 2257; mechanism, pp. 2258–2262). Supports the narrower predictions that individual-exchange prices become less informative while all prices jointly become more informative (Proposition 3, p. 2262), and that a specially chosen number of exchanges implements efficient trade in the constructed dynamic equilibrium (Theorem 3, pp. 2268–2269). Limit policy or real-market attribution using the explicit caveats at pp. 2248 and 2271–2272.
- Scientific: The question is whether fragmenting trade in one asset across multiple exchanges necessarily harms welfare through reduced depth. The contribution is a theoretical channel in which traders can split simultaneous, locally price-contingent demand schedules across exchanges, shielding an order on one venue from price impacts on other venues; imperfect cross-exchange price correlation then makes effective aggregate price-impact cost fall despite higher venue-level price impact (equations (3)–(4), pp. 2255–2256; Proposition 1 and discussion, pp. 2258–2260). The baseline has at least three strategic traders with private i.i.d. Gaussian inventories, quadratic holding costs, quasilinear cash utility, and independent venue-specific liquidity orders (Section II, pp. 2253–2255). Measurement is model-internal: price impact is inverse depth Λ_E (equation (3), p. 2255), order aggressiveness is Eα_E (equation (5), p. 2256), allocative efficiency is equal sharing/minimization of aggregate quadratic holding costs (pp. 2256–2257), and informativeness is the conditional variance of average strategic inventory given prices (p. 2262). The unique symmetric affine baseline equilibrium implies that Eα_E rises with fragmentation, crosses the efficient value one, and converges to N/(N−1); any E greater than one dominates centralization in that baseline, although excessive fragmentation is worse than the interior optimum (Theorem 1(vii), p. 2257; pp. 2260–2261). The continuous optimum is E*=2+2/(N−2)+[(N−1)/(N−2)]Nσ_X²/σ_Q², with the integer optimum at its floor or ceiling (equation (9) and Proposition 2, p. 2261). An observable-aggregate-endowment variant isolates the order-splitting channel and approaches efficiency as E diverges (Theorem 2, pp. 2263–2264). The dynamic analysis establishes existence of an efficient perfect Bayesian equilibrium only at E*, not for arbitrary E, while incorporating within-period and cross-period cross-exchange price impact (equations (19)–(20) and Theorem 3, p. 2268). There are no empirical estimates, statistical identification, standard errors, or confidence intervals: all reported effects are equilibrium implications and comparative statics. Credible alternative readings are explicitly supported by the extensions: payoff-relevant private information can make fragmentation harmful (Section VII.B, p. 2270), and correlated trade motives can reverse the baseline welfare comparison (Section VII.C, p. 2270). Proof validation remains pending because the online Appendix is unread.
- Structure: The introduction states the apparent depth-versus-efficiency paradox, mechanism, principal static and dynamic results, and limitations (pp. 2247–2249). Section I situates visible fragmentation, mixed empirical evidence, prior theory, disconnected demand schedules, and order-splitting practice (pp. 2249–2253). Section II defines the baseline market and welfare criterion (pp. 2253–2255). Section III constructs the symmetric affine equilibrium and identifies aggressiveness and efficient sharing (pp. 2255–2257). Section IV develops the main comparative statics in sequence—price impact, allocative efficiency, and price informativeness—with Propositions 1–3 and Figures 1–2 (pp. 2258–2262). Section V removes hidden aggregate inventory to isolate order splitting (pp. 2262–2264). Section VI builds the dynamic special-case equilibrium and belief state (pp. 2264–2269). Section VII summarizes extensions relegated to the online Appendix (pp. 2269–2270). Section VIII calibrates the contribution and exclusions (pp. 2271–2272), followed by references (pp. 2272–2274).
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in equilibrium construction, the interaction between cross-exchange inference and order splitting, and closed-form comparative statics (Sections III–IV, pp. 2255–2262). The dynamic belief problem receives substantial setup and intuition but only a special-E equilibrium, with the authors expressly declining to solve arbitrary E because of the infinite regress of higher-order beliefs (pp. 2266 and 2269). Institutional motivation is substantive but secondary (Section I, pp. 2249–2253). Empirical measurement, external calibration, quantitative fit, and policy analysis are absent. Robustness breadth is announced in Section VII, but the actual derivations reside in the unread online Appendix, so their depth cannot be audited from this artifact.
- Breadth: The paper covers visible fragmentation among lit exchanges, demand-function competition, welfare, depth, order aggressiveness, information aggregation, static versus dynamic trading, endogenous local liquidity demand, private payoff information, and correlated endowments (Sections I and VII, pp. 2249–2253 and 2269–2270). It engages both mixed empirical findings and theories of limited participation, adverse selection, exchange competition, and connected versus disconnected exchanges. Declared boundaries exclude lit-versus-dark fragmentation, endogenous exchange entry, fees and subsidies, tick sizes, bid-offer spreads, queue priority, sniping and latency arbitrage, trade-through effects material to small orders, and general policy evaluation (pp. 2248, 2249–2250, and 2271–2272). The central result relies critically on orders at one exchange being unable to condition on contemporaneous prices elsewhere and on prices not being perfectly correlated (pp. 2253–2254 and 2272).
- Optics: The terse title and five-sentence abstract foreground a counterintuitive result, then immediately distinguish venue-level depth from aggregate allocation and information (p. 2247). The opening moves from a familiar empirical fact to the conventional concern and then to the paper’s reversal, followed by a compact model-and-results roadmap (pp. 2247–2249). Claims are strongly stated where proven but repeatedly calibrated as a distinct possible channel rather than a policy conclusion (p. 2248; pp. 2271–2272). The exhibit ladder is disciplined: equilibrium theorem, price-impact proposition and Figure 1, welfare proposition and Figure 2, information proposition, observable-information theorem, then dynamic theorem (pp. 2256–2268). Figure captions state parameters and plotted objects clearly (pp. 2260 and 2263). Notation is dense but stable; intuition surrounds the formal results, especially at pp. 2258–2262. Reviewer-facing credibility comes from exposing restrictive assumptions and reversal cases, although relegating every proof and extension derivation to an unavailable online Appendix makes this saved artifact alone unauditable at the proof level.

- Implication: The admissible inference is a possibility-and-mechanism result: under disconnected exchanges, imperfectly correlated venue prices, strategic order splitting, and the paper’s preference and information structure, fragmentation can reduce the aggregate distortion from price-impact avoidance even while measured venue-level depth deteriorates. It does not establish that observed real-world fragmentation causally raises welfare, that more venues are generally better, or that the closed-form E* is a policy target. Any project claim should preserve the distinction between venue-level price impact, aggregate allocative efficiency, and collective price informativeness, and should cite the reversal cases at p. 2270 and omissions at pp. 2271–2272.
