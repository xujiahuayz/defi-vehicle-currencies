- Uses: Supports the theoretical claim that unverifiable failure can optimally produce an initial unconditional-financing stage followed by a disclosure stage, with later and declining failure rewards, stronger performance sensitivity, and backloaded incentives (abstract, pp. 807–808; Proposition 3 and interpretation, pp. 816–817). Supports the extension that monitoring can substitute for termination and generate repeated financing/disclosure stages (Proposition 4, p. 823). Serves as a published JFE theory exemplar for mechanism-first exposition, proposition-led results, economic applications, and proof-heavy appendices (pp. 807–846). It does not provide original empirical or causal evidence for VC contracting patterns; those discussions interpret findings from cited studies (Sections 4.1–4.4, pp. 817–822).
- Scientific: The question is how a financier should dynamically contract when an agent supplies hidden effort, privately observes some failures, and can either conceal or fake failure (Sections 1–2, pp. 807–812). The contribution is a continuous-time principal-agent solution in which rewarding disclosed failure improves truth telling but encourages premature fake-failure reports; preserving project-development incentives then requires higher success rewards and agency rents (Eqs. 7–14, pp. 813–814). The optimal response trades those rents against wasteful continuation after hidden failure: unconditional financing occurs first, followed by truthful disclosure and a hard deadline (Proposition 3, pp. 816–817). Data, measurement, econometric identification, statistical estimates, and sampling uncertainty are absent; Figures 2–5 are numerical model illustrations rather than empirical estimates (pp. 816, 821–824). Analytical results include the full-disclosure value and deadline in Eqs. 15–16 (p. 814), the staged-contract characterization (Proposition 3, p. 816), monitoring (Proposition 4, p. 823), and restricted comparative statics for sufficiently small π (Corollary 1, p. 820; proof, p. 839). Boundary conditions include risk neutrality, baseline zero discounting, limited liability, full principal commitment and bargaining power, binary effort and outcomes, Poisson completion, exogenous parameters, observable success, deterministic deadlines/inspections, and focus on full effort under stated parameter restrictions (pp. 810–812; Appendix C.1, p. 839). Robustness covers discounting, alternative effort effects on completion, unobservable success, and convex effort costs (Appendices C.2–C.5, pp. 839–846), but not empirical validation, renegotiation, noisy/endogenous monitoring, or richer organizational learning. A credible alternative reading is that the VC discussion supplies mechanism-consistent rationalizations and hypotheses, not discrimination from competing mechanisms such as learning, selection, bargaining, or financing constraints.
- Structure: The abstract and Introduction state the tension, two-stage solution, applications, and literature position (pp. 807–810). Section 2 defines technology, information, effort, payments, and principal/agent objectives (pp. 810–812; Fig. 1 and Eqs. 1–3). Section 3 builds from observable failure to full disclosure and then the optimal staged contract (pp. 812–817; Propositions 1–3, Fig. 2). Section 4 translates the model into VC, R&D, and executive-compensation applications, derives project-characteristic comparative statics, and distinguishes over- from under-financing (pp. 817–822; Figs. 3–4, Corollary 1). Section 5 adds costly inspections and repeated stages (pp. 822–824; Proposition 4). Section 6 separates moral hazard from adverse selection and summarizes robustness (pp. 824–825; Fig. 5). Section 7 concludes (p. 825). Appendix A gives closed forms (pp. 825–826), Appendix B supplies incentive-compatibility and proposition proofs plus the monitoring solution (pp. 826–839), Appendix C develops additional variants and financing-horizon calculations (pp. 839–846), and references occupy pp. 847–848.
- Depth: Analytical depth is concentrated in contract characterization and proof: truth-telling and effort constraints on pp. 813–814, the staged solution on pp. 815–817, and approximately 22 pages of closed forms, verification, deterministic-termination arguments, monitoring derivations, and model variants on pp. 825–846. The monitoring extension is substantial rather than cosmetic (Section 5, pp. 822–824; Appendix B.5, pp. 834–839). Institutional depth is moderate in the VC application (pp. 817–821) but relies on mappings to prior empirical papers rather than contract data assembled here. Measurement, empirical identification, uncertainty quantification, and formal comparison of observationally equivalent explanations are light or absent.
- Breadth: The paper connects continuous-time dynamic contracting, moral hazard, adverse selection, disclosure, innovation finance, venture capital, monitoring, R&D incentives, and executive compensation (literature discussion, pp. 809–810; applications, pp. 817–820). It treats concealment and fabrication of bad outcomes, grace periods, declining failure rewards, deadlines, staged finance, over- and under-investment, and inspections. Alternatives explored mathematically include discounting, effort-controlled completion, hidden success, and continuous effort with convex costs (pp. 839–846). Scope remains deliberately theoretical: the principal has strong commitment and information arrives through stylized Poisson technology; applications beyond VC are interpretive, and no mechanism-discriminating empirical exercise is attempted.
- Optics: The title is direct and mechanism-oriented; the abstract gives setup, tension, contract shape, and applications in one compact paragraph (p. 807). The introduction opens with Theranos, converts the anecdote immediately into three financing frictions, previews the complete result, then devotes substantial space to empirical mappings and literature differentiation (pp. 807–810). Claims are generally calibrated as model implications, but “explains several empirical patterns” in the abstract and similar application language exceed what the paper itself tests. The exhibit ladder is disciplined: timing tree (Fig. 1, p. 811), contract/value illustration (Fig. 2, p. 816), comparative statics (Fig. 3, p. 821), investment inefficiency (Fig. 4, p. 822), and moral-hazard/adverse-selection comparison (Fig. 5, p. 824). Captions identify parameters but provide little standalone interpretation. Notation and prose are dense, with many inline qualifications and long interpretive passages; credibility is built through propositions in the text and extensive proofs/variants in pp. 825–846. A visible wording inconsistency occurs on p. 818: the text says the unconditional stage is “relatively short” when φ is large, immediately before stating that its relative length increases with φ; Section 4.3 and Fig. 3 instead support “relatively long” (pp. 820–821).

- Implication: Cite the paper for a formal mechanism linking unverifiable failure to grace-period financing, later declining failure rewards, staged performance sensitivity, and monitoring/termination substitution, anchored to Proposition 3 (pp. 816–817) or Proposition 4 (p. 823). Treat the VC, R&D, compensation, and industry-allocation statements on pp. 817–822 as theoretical predictions or interpretations; use the underlying cited empirical studies—not Mayer’s model—as evidence that those patterns exist.
