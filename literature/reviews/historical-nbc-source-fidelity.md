# Krugman1980VehicleCurrencies
Krugman (1980), "Vehicle Currencies and the Structure of International Exchange," Journal of Money, Credit and Banking

**Historical dual-read reconciliation:** both agents reported reading the full text directly (Read 1: 14 pp. incl. appendix; Read 2: 15 pp. incl. appendix + literature cited — the small page-count mismatch is likely just a counting convention difference, not an access issue). Neither reported being unable to access the paper in that pass.

## 1. Where they agree (trustworthy, cross-checked)

- **Bibliographic**: sole-authored Krugman, *JMCB* 1980, vol. 12 no. 3, pp. 513–526; no co-authorship issue.
- **Core question**: in a 3-country/3-currency world, when does a payment between two countries route through the third currency rather than exchange directly.
- **"Structure of payments" vs. "structure of exchange"**: without transaction costs, bilateral imbalances are pinned down by BOP identities ("conservation of imbalance," a feature Krugman flags as special to the 3-country case), but which currency-pairs actually get traded is indeterminate.
- **Transaction costs resolve the indeterminacy**: the currency with the two lower-cost markets (α, if t_αβ, t_γα < t_βγ) becomes the vehicle; whether the outcome is "partial" or "total" indirect exchange turns on comparing (1−t_αβ)(1−t_γα) against (1−t_βγ). Both cite the same "clockwisdom" wedge D = E_αβE_βγE_γα.
- **Footnote 3**: clockwisdom explicitly does not generalize beyond three currencies — this is a 3-country model only, not an n-currency theory.
- **Section 4 endogenization**: transaction cost as a decreasing function of volume, F(V), F′<0, motivated only by a quoted claim from Kubarych (1978) — not by data Krugman himself gathered. This creates a self-reinforcing volume→lower-cost→more-volume loop.
- **Multiple equilibria**: symmetric payments structure (Fig. 4) → multiple self-confirming vehicle candidates; one country dominant in payments (Fig. 5) → unique self-confirming vehicle even when the analysis is forced to try alternatives.
- **Hysteresis/"catastrophic" switching is explicitly NOT a proven result**: both flag Krugman's own disclaimer that a full dynamic analysis is "beyond this article's scope," so the sterling-persistence and discontinuous-switch material is narrative/conjectural, grafted onto the static multiple-equilibrium result.
- **Scope restriction to medium-of-exchange only**: both note Krugman explicitly carves out vehicle-currency-as-medium-of-exchange from the reserve/store-of-value and invoicing/unit-of-account roles of money.
- **No reconciliation with asset-market exchange-rate theory**: both quote Krugman's own flag that his flow-demand framework is "preliminary" relative to the asset/stock-equilibrium view.
- **Pure theory, zero data/econometrics** throughout.

## 2. Disagreements / things one caught that the other missed — flagged, not averaged

- **Boundary condition on partial vs. total indirect exchange under dominance**: Read 2 reports a specific formal result — if [1−F(∞)]² < 1−F(0), partial indirect exchange is *always* the outcome regardless of how dominant one country becomes (total indirect exchange is not guaranteed by dominance alone). Read 1 does not mention this; instead it frames the partial/total ambiguity via inequalities (9)/(10) on R, S, T, I being "not mutually exclusive." These may describe compatible parts of the same appendix argument, but Read 1 omits a specific formal boundary condition that Read 2 states plainly. **Unconfirmed by cross-check — treat as Read 2's claim only until verified.**
- **"Small transaction costs" / "approximate equilibrium" restriction**: Read 2 states that Krugman's Section 3 equilibrium concept is explicitly restricted to "small" transaction costs and ignores feedback from the exchange structure back onto the payments structure itself — an "approximate equilibrium." Read 1 never mentions this restriction at all. This is a non-trivial scope limitation (it affects how much weight the transaction-cost mechanism can bear for large-cost regimes) that only one read caught. **Flag as missing from Read 1, not resolved.**
- **Minor**: page-count discrepancy (14 vs. 15 pp.) — almost certainly a counting-convention artifact (whether "Literature Cited" is counted), not a substantive disagreement, but noted for completeness.

## 3. Historical candidate citation boundary (current verification still required)

Krugman (1980) is a sole-authored, purely theoretical three-country/three-currency model — no data, no econometrics — showing that (i) absent transaction costs, the pattern of bilateral payments imbalances among three countries is mechanically determined by balance-of-payments identities, but the pattern of which currency-pairs are actually traded is indeterminate; (ii) once transaction costs differ across the three currency-pair markets, cost-minimizing routing uniquely selects the currency with the two lowest-cost markets as the "vehicle" for indirect (triangular) exchange, and determines whether the equilibrium is "partial" or "total" indirect exchange; and (iii) when those costs are endogenized as decreasing in trading volume (a stylized assumption resting on a single quoted practitioner source, not on data Krugman collected), the model yields a self-reinforcing volume→lower-cost→more-volume loop that can produce multiple self-confirming vehicle-currency equilibria under symmetric payments structures, collapsing to a unique vehicle equilibrium when one country's payments dominate. It may be cited for this specific three-country formal mechanism, for its explicit restriction to the medium-of-exchange function of money only (not reserve/store-of-value or invoicing/unit-of-account roles), and as the origin of the "clockwisdom" and partial/total indirect-exchange constructs. It must **not** be cited as proving — rather than informally conjecturing — that vehicle-currency status persists after the underlying economic dominance fades, or that transitions between vehicle currencies are discontinuous/"catastrophic" (Krugman explicitly disclaims having done the dynamic analysis this would require); must not be extended beyond three currencies (footnote 3 blocks generalization); must not be treated as empirical; and must not be read as reconciled with asset-market/stock-equilibrium theories of exchange rates (Krugman flags his flow-demand framework as preliminary on exactly this point). Two finer mechanical claims — a boundary condition on the endogenous cost function said to guarantee partial indirect exchange regardless of dominance, and an explicit "small transaction costs / approximate equilibrium" restriction on the Section 3 result — were reported by only one of the two independent reads each; any framing claim resting on that level of granularity should be independently verified before being stated as settled.

# DowdGreenaway1993CurrencyCompetition
Dowd & Greenaway (1993), "Currency Competition, Network Externalities and Switching Costs," Economic Journal

## Historical dual-read reconciliation
**Historical dual-read reconciliation:** both readers reported reading the full ten-page text rather than an abstract; Read 2 additionally cited the specific local PDF path used. Neither flagged an access failure in that pass. Current reuse still follows source admission and the literature audit.

## 1. Where the two reads agree (trustworthy, cross-checked core)

- **Target and mechanism**: paper combines network externalities (utility rises in log of other users, `n = ln N`) with a fixed switching cost `s`, explicitly building on Farrell & Saloner (1986)'s installed-base/compatibility apparatus (both quote the same "builds upon" line) — applied to currency choice, not originated by Dowd & Greenaway.
- **Formal structure**: utility from incumbent currency `(a+bn)/r`, from new currency `(c+dn)/r`, `c≥a`, `d≥b`; both reads independently derive the same three regimes — universal switching dominant iff `(c−a)/r > (s+bn)/r` (eq. 5), no-switching dominant iff `(c−a)/r+dn/r < s` (eq. 7), and an indeterminate zone of multiple equilibria driven purely by self-fulfilling expectations, labeled "excess inertia" / "excess momentum" (both terms explicitly borrowed from Farrell-Saloner).
- **Fiat-currency corollary**: for `c=a=0` (pure fiat, no intrinsic non-network value), condition (5) can never hold — this is both reads' explanation for why fiat currencies are "sticky"/resistant to competitive displacement.
- **Comparative static**: as network size N grows, the threshold quality gap needed to trigger switching rises, so currency competition's disciplining power over monetary misbehavior weakens as networks/economies grow — both flag this as a genuine derived result, not a throwaway line.
- **Monetary-union extension (eqs. 12–16)**: comparing options for imposing a common currency, both reads agree the model shows switching costs are minimized by having the smaller currency area adopt the larger one's currency rather than introducing a brand-new common currency — this is the basis of the paper's specific "D-Mark over ecu" conclusion, and both flag it as the part of the model most original to Dowd & Greenaway (beyond the F&S base case).
- **Nature of the paper**: purely theoretical — no data, no estimation, no empirical test; any "stylised facts" (e.g., persistence of demand for hyperinflating currencies) are asserted informally, not demonstrated.
- **What's NOT licensed** (both agree): claiming this as empirical evidence; treating it as the origin of monetary-network-externality ideas generally (that credit goes to Farrell & Saloner, and the authors say so); treating the welfare-rectangle-vs-triangle inflation-cost idea and the language-choice analogy as worked results rather than paper's own flagged "preliminary"/speculative extensions.
- **Co-authorship**: no textual division of labor between Dowd and Greenaway; both are Nottingham-affiliated, joint throughout, no internal attribution signal in the paper itself.

## 2. Disagreements / things one caught that the other missed — flagged, not averaged

- **OCA-literature framing (real tension)**: Read 1 frames the paper as arguing that "standard optimum-currency-area (OCA) theory... [is] incomplete," implying engagement with/extension of Mundell-McKinnon OCA criteria. Read 2 explicitly quotes footnote 1 — the authors state their "starting point is... quite different from the well-known optimum currency area literature which builds on the work of Mundell (1961) and McKinnon (1963)" — and draws the conclusion that the paper should **not** be cited as evidence for or against traditional OCA criteria, only as an "alternative lens." This is a meaningful disagreement in emphasis: Read 2's textual evidence (a direct footnote quote) is more specific and cautions against a framing Read 1's summary could invite. **Flag: defer to Read 2's more conservative, textually-anchored reading on this point.**
- **Legal restrictions — derived vs. bolted-on (real tension)**: Read 1 says the authors "use the same apparatus" (i.e., the formal model) to argue legal restrictions on switching more likely entrench excess inertia than correct excess momentum. Read 2 explicitly quotes the paper — "Legal restrictions were ignored earlier to focus on the underlying 'pure' economics" (Section II) — and concludes this discussion is qualitative commentary bolted on afterward, **not derived from the equilibrium conditions** of the formal model. This is a substantive disagreement about how much formal warrant the legal-restrictions claim carries. **Flag: Read 2's direct textual quote is stronger evidence; treat the legal-restrictions/inertia-bias claim as informal qualitative argument, not a modeled result.**
- **Multi-currency holding excluded (Read 2 caught, Read 1 missed entirely)**: Read 2 notes footnote 4 explicitly restricts the model to binary either/or currency choice, bracketing dollarization/currency-substitution-portfolio cases as an unworked extension. Read 1 never mentions this. **Flag: this is a real gap in Read 1's coverage** — the "does not license" list for citation purposes should include no support for multi-currency-holding claims.
- **D-Mark/ecu ceteris-paribus scope (Read 2 caught, Read 1 understates)**: Read 1 calls the D-Mark result "a genuinely specific, derivable comparative-statics result... not a vague intuition" without noting its scope conditions. Read 2 additionally notes the result holds "explicitly under a ceteris paribus assumption that a₁=a₂, b₁=b₂ across currencies" and quotes the authors' own hedge that political considerations could dictate a different route without altering "the economics of the case considered here." **Flag: the D-Mark conclusion is conditional on equal-quality-parameter assumptions; Read 1's framing risks overstating its unconditional force.**
- **Author-specific context (Read 2 adds, explicitly labeled speculative)**: Read 2 notes — as its own inference, not from the text — that this paper is notable because Kevin Dowd is otherwise a free-banking/currency-competition advocate, and this paper is unusual in *qualifying* rather than supporting that view; also speculates Greenaway's trade-economics background maps to the OCA/policy framing portion. Read 1 explicitly declines to speculate about the co-authors' outside work, saying only that it has no basis to assess division of labor. This isn't a contradiction (Read 2 flags it as external inference), but it's additional context Read 1 omits — useful color, not a citable fact of the paper.
- **DeFi/stablecoin scoping (present in Read 1, absent in Read 2)**: Read 1 explicitly rules out citing this paper for cryptocurrency/DeFi/stablecoin vehicle-currency competition without qualification. Read 2 doesn't address this domain at all. Not a contradiction — nothing in Read 2 conflicts with Read 1's caution — but it means only Read 1 has explicitly stress-tested this particular (likely relevant, given the file path referencing a defi-vehicle-currencies project) extension.

## 3. Historical candidate citation boundary

Dowd & Greenaway (1993) is a purely theoretical, data-free model — explicitly built on Farrell & Saloner's (1986) installed-base/compatibility framework — that combines network externalities (`(a+bn)/r`-type utility, network benefit diminishing in log of network size) with a fixed switching cost `s` to show that currency-switching decisions can have multiple self-fulfilling equilibria, including "excess inertia" (failure to switch to a superior currency) and "excess momentum" (switching to an inferior one), that this logic implies pure fiat currencies (zero non-network intrinsic value) can never satisfy the condition for guaranteed universal switching, that currency competition's disciplining power over monetary misbehavior weakens as the incumbent network grows, and that — in the paper's own most original extension (eqs. 12–16) — imposing a common currency minimizes aggregate switching costs by having smaller currency areas adopt an existing large-circulation currency rather than by introducing a brand-new one (their D-Mark-over-ecu argument, valid under an explicit equal-quality-parameter ceteris-paribus assumption). It should NOT be cited as an empirical result, as an engagement with or extension of Mundell/McKinnon-style OCA criteria (the authors explicitly distance themselves from that literature), as a modeled treatment of legal restrictions on currency use (that discussion is qualitative commentary the authors themselves say was kept outside the formal model), as covering multi-currency holding/dollarization (explicitly excluded by assumption), or as licensing any claim about cryptocurrency, stablecoin, or DeFi vehicle-currency competition (the paper concerns fiat national currencies and the ecu only) — any such application is the citer's extension, not the paper's claim. Two specific characterization points are contested between independent reads and should be treated as open rather than settled: (i) whether the paper's legal-restrictions discussion carries any of the formal model's derivational warrant (one read says yes via "the same apparatus," the other quotes the text explicitly excluding legal restrictions from the model and treats it as bolted-on commentary — defer to the more conservative, textually-quoted reading), and (ii) how strongly the paper engages the standard OCA literature (one read implies continuity/critique, the other cites a footnote explicitly disclaiming that framing).

# GopinathStein2021Making
Gopinath & Stein (2021), "Banking, Trade, and the Making of a Dominant Currency," QJE

**Historical dual-read reconciliation:** both agents reported having read the full published QJE (2021) text (all sections, propositions, numerical example, empirical subsection), not just the abstract, so that pass recorded no access caveat.

## (1) Where they agree (trustworthy, cross-checked core)

Both reads converge, often near-verbatim, on every load-bearing point:

- **Central thesis**: trade-invoicing dominance and safe-asset/banking dominance are two sides of one equilibrium, mutually reinforcing via strategic complementarity — not separate phenomena, not explained by country size/fiscal capacity alone.
- **Mechanism chain**: sticky dollar invoicing → importer hedging demand for dollar deposits → EM banks must supply dollar-safe claims once cheap sources (Treasury, naturally dollar-earning firms) are exhausted → currency-mismatched local banks/firms are marginal, inefficient suppliers → they demand compensation → that compensation **is** the UIP violation / "exorbitant privilege" (dollar assets earn a *lower* expected return) → cheaper dollar funding then gives EM exporters a financial (not just pricing-to-market) incentive to invoice in dollars → feeds back into other countries' import-invoicing shares → complementarity loop.
- **Formal structure**: same nested models (Section II exogenous M/Proposition 2 threshold M̄; Section III endogenous η, bang-bang FOC, Proposition 3's four M-regions; Section IV continuum of EMs with parameters a and b, Proposition 4 multiplicity for b above a threshold; Section V symmetric US/euro setup, Proposition 5, with a proven **stability** argument — symmetric no-mismatch equilibria are unstable, asymmetric single-dominant-currency equilibria are stable).
- **History/selection is informal, not modeled**: the model is symmetric between US and Europe by construction, so it cannot pin down *which* currency wins on fundamentals; the paper's history/incumbency argument is a verbal/heuristic selection device, not a proven result.
- **Empirical section is explicitly non-causal**: small cross-sectional correlation(s) (~8–10 countries, R²≈0.72–0.82), self-described by the authors as "preliminary evidence," not identification.
- **What it doesn't cover**: medium-of-exchange/vehicle-currency role explicitly set aside (footnote 5, asserted-not-derived "only strengthens" claim); central-bank reserve-holdings result belongs to the **companion paper**, Gopinath and Stein (2018b, AER P&P), only summarized here; underlying invoicing-share stylized facts (e.g., country invoicing shares, Turkey example) belong to **Gopinath (2015)** alone; the paper is explicit its explanation is "distinct/complementary" to Hassan, Gourinchas-Rey, He-Krishnamurthy-Milbradt, Farhi-Maggiori rather than a refutation of them; Libra discussion is explicitly speculative, not modeled.
- **Co-authorship**: fully joint (Gopinath and Stein), no internal division of labor within this specific article; both flag the same two "don't misattribute" traps (2018b for reserves, Gopinath 2015 for invoicing facts).

This overlapping core — mechanism, propositions, what's licensed/not licensed, companion-paper boundary, co-authorship — is the trustworthy, cross-checked characterization safe for a framing agent to lean on.

## (2) Disagreements / one catching something the other missed

Flagging explicitly, not averaging:

- **Read 1 alone** surfaces a specific, easily-misquoted result from **Section V.C**: increasing the *exogenous supply* of a currency's safe assets (e.g., "issuing a euro safe bond"), holding demand fixed, makes that currency's dominance **less** likely in the model — because more supply lowers the return banks must offer, reducing the incentive for EM banks/exporters to tilt toward it. Read 2 does not mention this result at all. This is a substantive, non-trivial finding (directly counter to a naive "more official euro debt → more euro internationalization" intuition) that only Read 1 caught. Treat this as **unverified by cross-check** — worth flagging to a framing agent as a claim to confirm against the text directly (specifically Section V.C) before citing, rather than treating it as fully corroborated.
- **Read 1 alone** notes an NBER working-paper predecessor, Gopinath and Stein (2018a), containing a smoother/non-bang-bang invoicing-function variant, and warns that a robustness check using that functional form belongs to 2018a, not the 2021 QJE piece. Read 2 mentions 2018a only in passing (as part of the joint research agenda) without this attribution warning. Minor, but Read 1's version is more precise for citation-attribution purposes.
- **Read 2 alone** adds a modeling-detail caveat not in Read 1: footnote 9's point that the Section II collateral-constraint result is driven by the *worst-case* exchange rate (not the full distribution), which the authors themselves flag as a modeling artifact generalizable to a tail-risk/VaR interpretation. Read 1 omits this nuance.
- **Read 2 alone** explicitly disclaims the **forward premium puzzle** (footnote 11) — the paper says nothing about higher-frequency, interest-rate-change-linked UIP violations, only average cross-country rate differentials. Read 1 does not mention this scope limit at all. This is a meaningful "does NOT license" boundary that only one read caught — flag it as worth independently verifying, but it is the kind of narrow footnote-level claim consistent with the rest of both reads' characterization of the paper's scope, so moderately trustworthy even singly-sourced.
- **Read 2 alone** attributes the pooling/tranching bank micro-foundation (why aggregating N projects yields a safe worst-case payout) to Gorton and Pennacchi (1990) via Stein (2012)'s formulation specifically. Read 1 doesn't address this attribution. Minor provenance detail, singly sourced.

No point where the two reads actively contradict each other on a substantive claim — the divergences are all one-caught-it-the-other-didn't additions, concentrated in footnote-level or single-section details, not in the paper's central claims or main propositions.

## (3) Historical candidate citation boundary

Gopinath and Stein (2021, QJE) is a **theoretical** general-equilibrium model — not a causal/empirical identification paper — showing that a currency's trade-invoicing dominance and its dominance as a global safe-asset/banking currency are mutually reinforcing through a specific collateral-based mechanism (currency-mismatched EM banks as marginal suppliers of dollar safe claims, compensated via a UIP violation/"exorbitant privilege," which in turn cheapens dollar borrowing and gives EM exporters a *financial* incentive — distinct from standard pricing-to-market channels — to invoice in dollars), and that this strategic complementarity can formally generate multiple equilibria, including one where a single currency dominates despite symmetric US/Europe fundamentals (with a proven stability argument favoring the asymmetric-dominance equilibrium); it should be cited only for this mechanism and equilibrium-multiplicity/stability result, not for *which* currency wins on fundamentals (that is left indeterminate by the model and resolved only by an informal history/incumbency heuristic), not for any causal empirical estimate (the paper's own cross-sectional correlations, n≈8–15, are explicitly labeled "preliminary" and non-causal by the authors), not for the medium-of-exchange/vehicle-currency role (explicitly set aside), and not for the central-bank reserve-holdings channel (that belongs to the companion paper, Gopinath and Stein 2018b) or the underlying invoicing-share stylized facts (that belong to Gopinath 2015) — both of which are referenced but not original to this article. One claim surfaced by only one of the two independent reads and not yet cross-verified — that Section V.C shows increasing a currency's exogenous safe-asset supply alone, absent a demand increase, makes that currency *less* likely to achieve dominance in the model — is plausible given the rest of the mechanism but should be confirmed directly against Section V.C before a framing agent relies on it.

# LeharParlour2024Uniswap
Lehar & Parlour (2024/2025), "Decentralized Exchange: The Uniswap Automated Market Maker," Journal of Finance

# Comparison of Two Independent Reads — Lehár & Parlour (2024/2025), "Decentralized Exchange: The Uniswap Automated Market Maker," *Journal of Finance*

**Historical dual-read reconciliation:** both agents reported reading the full 54-page PDF directly (not abstract-only) — Read 1 from the local file path, Read 2 via PyMuPDF extraction of the same file — covering introduction, full theory (Sections I–II), empirical sections (III–V), conclusion, and appendix proofs. Neither reported an access failure in that pass. This records two independent reads rather than one read validating itself.

## 1. Where they agree (trustworthy, cross-checked core)

- **Mechanism**: AMMs replace price-adjustment (LOB) with pool-size-adjustment as the margin that balances fee revenue against arbitrageur "picking-off" risk. Both describe this identically, including the structural separation of liquidity provision (LPs earn fees) from price discovery (arbitrageurs capture the correction profit).
- **Model primitives**: identical description of the one-period setup (p0, jump ±σ with probability α, noise trader q, arbitrageur). Identical statement of Lemma 1 (monopolist LP earns q(1−α)σ), Lemma 2 (Bertrand zero-profit competition), and Proposition 1's closed form γ* = (1−α)σq / [2a+σq(1−α)].
- **AMM equilibrium**: both give the same account of Lemma 3 (fee revenue), Lemma 4 (picking-off loss via arbitrageur FOC on the bonding curve), and Proposition 2 (equilibrium T0 solves fee revenue = expected picking-off loss).
- **Proposition 3 comparative statics**: equilibrium pool size linear in q, decreasing in σ, decreasing in α — both agree this is the empirically tested claim.
- **Proposition 4–5 / venue comparison**: AMM is not dominated by the LOB; dominance is conditional (threshold α*, τ/η < σ/p0), and AMM price impact is deterministic/less volatile than LOB price impact, which is stochastic depending on monopolist-vs-competitor realization.
- **Empirical scale and design**: 95.8M interactions, 105,098 pools, Nov 2018–Dec 2022; regression tests of H1–H3 against volatility and noise-trading proxies; the 43-cross-listed-pair Uniswap-vs-Binance comparison; the May 19, 2021 ETH crash as evidence of liquidity stickiness; the March 2022 Binance–PancakeSwap integration as a descriptive before/after shift.
- **Scope limits agreed by both**: this is V1/V2 constant-product only, not V3 concentrated liquidity, not other AMM designs (Curve/Balancer); the empirical work is model-consistent regression evidence, not a causal-identification (IV/RD) design; no blanket "AMMs beat LOBs" claim — Proposition 5 is explicitly conditional and confined to the specific 43-pair Binance comparison.
- **Co-authorship**: two authors only, no division-of-labor language in the paper, and both independently flag the same disambiguation risk — do not conflate this paper (equilibrium pool size / AMM-vs-LOB cost) with the separate Lehár & Parlour (2023) paper on liquidations/leverage cited within it.

This overlapping core is unusually dense and specific (matching formulas, proposition numbers, and hedging language almost verbatim) — it is the trustworthy characterization a framing paragraph can lean on.

## 2. Disagreements and things one caught that the other missed (flagged, not averaged)

- **Bibliographic detail conflict**: Read 1 gives the page range as pp. 321–355; Read 2 gives pp. 321–359. This is a factual discrepancy in citation metadata that needs independent verification before it goes into any reference list — do not silently pick one.
- **Causal-framing tension over the Binance–PancakeSwap event**: Read 1's Section 2 calls it a "difference-in-differences-flavored natural shock," language that leans toward quasi-causal identification, even though Read 1's own Section 3 immediately downgrades it to "descriptive... not identification." Read 2 is uniformly more skeptical throughout, explicitly stating it is "a dummy variable, not an IV... suggestive, not identified in a strict sense." **Read 2 caught a framing risk that Read 1 half-introduced and then had to walk back** — a framing-drafting agent should adopt Read 2's stricter language here to avoid overclaiming causal identification for this event.
- **Arbitrage-convergence / mature-pool pricing-error finding**: Read 1 reports a specific empirical result — average absolute pricing error vs. Binance of ~0.19% once a pool the size of USDC/ETH is mature, versus errors exceeding 40% for thin pools (<100 ETH) — and uses this to qualify the abstract's "absence of long-lived arbitrage opportunities" claim as being about mature pools only. **Read 2 does not mention this finding at all.** This is either a substantive detail Read 2 missed, or a de-prioritization; either way, treat the 0.19%/40% figures as single-sourced until corroborated, and do not treat "no long-lived arbitrage opportunities" as an unqualified claim without checking Read 1's mature-pool caveat.
- **Optimal AMM design / fixed fee tier**: Read 2 explicitly flags that the paper does **not** license claims about optimal AMM design, because the 30bp fee τ is treated as fixed/exogenous, not optimized within the model. **Read 1's exclusion list does not mention this at all.** This is a caveat Read 2 caught that Read 1 missed — worth keeping if the citing paper discusses fee-tier optimization or design-space questions.
- **Impermanent loss / LP profitability and MEV/sandwich-attack exclusions**: Read 1 explicitly lists these as things the paper does NOT license (no claims about whether being an LP is profitable in absolute terms; no modeling of MEV/front-running/gas auctions). Read 2 does not explicitly state either exclusion. Likely an omission rather than a disagreement, but a framing agent should treat these as unlicensed by default regardless, since Read 1's reasoning (LPs' outside option is normalized to zero; the arbitrageur is a single frictionless informed trader with no ordering game) is sound and uncontested by Read 2.
- **Gas-cost "negligibility" is average-case only**: Read 1 gives specific figures ($0.94 average incremental fee vs. CEX withdrawal; spikes to $11.57 in May 2021) and stresses this is a typical-case, not universal, finding. Read 2 references "gas-cost estimates" only generically. Not a contradiction, but Read 1's caveat (don't generalize the parity claim across all periods/traders) should be preserved since Read 2 provides no basis to relax it.

## 3. Historical candidate citation boundary

This paper (Lehár & Parlour, "Decentralized Exchange: The Uniswap Automated Market Maker," *Journal of Finance* 80(1), Feb 2025, pp. 321–355/359 [page range unresolved — verify before citing]) may be cited for the narrow, cross-checked claim that in a constant-product AMM (Uniswap V1/V2 specifically, not V3 or other bonding-curve designs), liquidity providers cannot set prices and instead reach equilibrium through pool size, which theoretically and empirically shrinks with asset volatility/informed-trading probability and grows with uninformed (noise) trading volume, because a smaller pool raises price impact and caps the loss arbitrageurs can extract via "picking-off" trades — and that, under specific and bounded conditions (low volatility, high noise trading, medium trade size, and confined in the paper's own tests to a 43-pair Uniswap-vs-Binance comparison), such an AMM can deliver lower and less volatile price impact than a comparable limit order book, without this constituting a general claim that AMMs dominate limit order books, that any AMM design is optimal, that LPs are profitable in absolute terms, that MEV/front-running dynamics are addressed, or that the paper's few natural-experiment-flavored observations (the Binance–PancakeSwap integration, the May 2021 crash) establish causal identification rather than model-consistent descriptive correlation.

# Somogyi2026DollarDominanceFX
Somogyi (2026), "Dollar Dominance in FX Trading"

## Historical dual-read reconciliation
Both agents explicitly report reading the full text directly from the PDF (Read 1: "all 23 pages plus references... not just the abstract"; Read 2: "full PDF directly (24 pages, extracted via PyMuPDF)... full text, model, tables, and references all present"). ## 1. Where they agree (trustworthy, cross-checked characterization)

- **Paper identity**: Fabricius Somogyi, "Dollar Dominance in FX Trading," *Management Science*, Articles in Advance, 2026, single-authored, Northeastern University.

# Yuan2005BenchmarkSecurities
Yuan (2005, solo), "The Liquidity Service of Benchmark Securities," Journal of the European Economic Association 3(5), 1156-1180

# Comparison of the Two Reads of Yuan (2005), "The Liquidity Service of Benchmark Securities"

**Historical dual-read reconciliation:** both agents independently located and reported reading the full working-paper PDF (LSE-hosted pre-print, same URL: `https://personal.lse.ac.uk/yuan/papers/benchmark.pdf`), not just an abstract, and both identified it as the paper published as Yuan, K. ## 1) Where they agree (trustworthy, cross-checked)

The two reads converge on every substantive point:

- **Mechanism**: Heterogeneously informed traders (systematic-factor-informed vs. single-security-idiosyncratic-informed) face adverse-selection leakage when forced to trade/hedge via ordinary securities or synthetic baskets. A pure-systematic-risk benchmark security lets each type align its trade exactly with its signal, raising expected profits and thus informed-trader entry (via a zero-expected-profit / fixed-cost-*c* entry condition).
- **Model class**: Kyle (1989)-style noisy REE model extending Subrahmanyam (1991) and Caballe-Krishnan (1994); factor structure ṽ = v̄ + βγ̃ + μ̃; the critical extra assumption is that market makers observe order flow across *all* securities (not just their own), unlike Subrahmanyam (1991).
- **Core result**: Proposition 2 / Corollary 2 — introducing the benchmark raises equilibrium entry of both trader types and raises liquidity/price informativeness for *every* individual security, not just benchmark-heavy ones; the effect scales with systematic-risk variance, a security's β, benchmark liquidity, and the security's idiosyncratic risk. The proof is by contradiction (Appendix C, partitioned-matrix-inverse algebra), not empirical estimation.
- **Positioning vs. prior literature**: This is an alternative (not a refutation) to Subrahmanyam (1991)/Gorton-Pennacchi (1993), which explain benchmark popularity via *uninformed* traders' low adverse-selection exposure. Yuan's channel runs through informed traders instead.
- **Pure theory, no empirical test**: The only numbers in the paper are an illustrative calibrated two-security example, not an estimation on data. Both explicitly warn against citing this paper as empirical evidence.
- **Scope exclusions agreed on**: no model of benchmark-security *supply*/issuance/scarcity (deferred to DeMarzo-Duffie 1999, Rahi 1996, and Yuan's own 2001 sovereign-bonds paper); no welfare claims.
- **Authorship**: Solo-authored by Kathy Yuan; "we" is academic convention, not evidence of co-authorship.
- **Critical disambiguation, agreed verbatim in substance**: This paper must not be conflated with the co-authored, *empirical* "Dittmar, Robert F., and Kathy Yuan (2005), 'The price impact of sovereign bonds'" — cited within this paper's introduction as motivating context but a wholly separate work. Anyone citing "Yuan (2005)" for an empirical sovereign/corporate-bond liquidity-spillover result is very likely citing the wrong paper.

## 2) Disagreements / one catching something the other missed
