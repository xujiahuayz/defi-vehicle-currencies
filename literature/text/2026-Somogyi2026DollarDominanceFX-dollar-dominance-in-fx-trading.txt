Abstract. Over 85% of all foreign exchange (FX) transactions involve the U.S. dollar, whereas 
the United States accounts for a much smaller fraction of global economic activity. My paper 
attributes the dominance of the dollar in FX trading to strategic avoidance of price impact. 
Using a novel identification strategy, I show that, on average, 13% of the volume in dollar 
pairs arises from using the dollar as a vehicle currency to indirectly exchange two nondollar 
currencies. To rationalise this result, I derive three model-based conditions for dollar domi -
nance. I empirically test these conditions and provide evidence consistent with the theoretical 
framework.

1. Introduction
The U.S. dollar (USD) plays a central role in the global 
economy. This is particularly the case for the foreign 
exchange (FX) market, which is the largest financial 
market in the world. Over 85% of all FX transactions 
involve the U.S. dollar, despite the fact that the United 
States accounts for just around 40% of global cross- 
border economic activities. Against this backdrop, the 
goal of this paper is to explain why the U.S. dollar is 
used significantly more in global FX transactions than 
is warranted by U.S. economic activities.
I show that the use of the U.S. dollar as an interme -
diate vehicle currency is a pivotal determinant of its 
dominance in global FX spot trading volumes. Specifi -
cally, market participants that are strategic about their 
trading costs avoid directly transacting in nondollar cur -
rency pairs whenever the expected trading cost is suffi -
ciently large. Instead, market participants exchange 
nondollar pairs indirectly by using the U.S. dollar as an 
intermediate vehicle currency. That is, market partici -
pants first exchange a nondollar currency into U.S. 
dollars and then trade those dollars for their target 
currency.
Using a globally representative FX trade data set, I 
quantify the empirical relevance of vehicle currency 
trading along two dimensions. First, I use a novel 
identification strategy to establish a stylised fact 
about vehicle currency trading: on average 13% of the 
daily trading volume in dollar currency pairs (around 
$8 billion per day) corresponds to vehicle currency 
trading activity. Second, I show that my model-based 
conditions for dollar dominance (DD) in FX volumes 
are accurate predictors of the actual dollar dominance 
observed in the data.
To guide the empirical analysis, I build on Rostek 
and Yoon ( 2021 )’s model to show that the price impact 
of the indirect vehicle trade involving two dollar cur -
rency pairs is less than the direct exchange if two con -
ditions are satisfied: the two dollar currency pairs 
exhibit (i) at least twice as volatile fundamental trading 
demands, but (ii) half as volatile exchange rate returns 
as the nondollar pair. The lower expected price impact 
in dollar currency pairs generates additional trading 
volume beyond what the U.S. share in global economic 
activity would suggest. Taken together, large funda -
mental trading demands coupled with significant 
vehicle currency trading because of strategic avoid -
ance of price impact engender that dollar pairs domi -
nate FX volumes. The empirical analysis supports 
these theoretical predictions.
Understanding the determinants of dollar dominance 
in FX trading is relevant for both global investors and 
policymakers. This is because the concentration of FX 
trading volume in U.S. dollar currency pairs entails 
both costs and benefits for the world economy.
1
Poten -

which, in turn, facilitates international trade and invest -
ment. But interconnectedness can also be a source of 
systemic risk and international spill-over effects if it 
amplifies shocks from the U.S. to other economies 
around the world.

The main contribution of this paper is to show 
empirically that vehicle currency trading because of 
strategic avoidance of price impact is a pivotal deter -
minant of dollar dominance in FX trading. Hence, 
the paper does not aim to provide a unified theory of 
dollar dominance in general. In particular, the paper 
does not strive to answer why fundamental trading 
demands for U.S. dollars are large to begin with. 
Instead, the paper focuses on how strategic price 
impact avoidance generates vehicle currency trading 
demands for U.S. dollars such that trading volume in 
dollar pairs exceeds what would be warranted by fun -
damental trading demands.
To illustrate the relevance of this economic channel, 
I establish a novel stylised fact about vehicle currency 
trading. Specifically, I disentangle trading volume in 
dollar currency pairs because of fundamental trading 
motives from vehicle currency demands. For identifica -
tion, I exploit the quasiexogenous variation in vehicle 
currency trading demands associated with nonoverlap -
ping holidays. The intuition is as follows. Consider, for 
instance, the case where Australia is on holiday but nei -
ther Japan nor the United States are (e.g., Anzac Day 
on April 25). On such a day, hardly any of the inter -
dealer volume in USDJPY is driven by vehicle currency 
trading motives stemming from indirectly exchanging 
Australian dollars to Japanese yen via the U.S. dollar. 
This is because the number of counterparties that wish 
to exchange Australian dollars for another foreign cur -
rency, such as the Japanese yen, is lower because of 
the public holiday. Eventually, my measure of vehicle 
currency trading activity is the difference between 
interdealer volume and my implied measure of funda -
mental trading demand based on nonoverlapping holi -
days. Using an event study regression design that 
controls for various sources of fundamental trading 
demands, I find that vehicle currency trading demands 
for the largest and most liquid dollar pairs (e.g., 
USDEUR or USDJPY) account for up to 25%–38% of 
aggregate daily interdealer volume in dollar pairs.
The remainder of the paper proceeds in two parts. 
The first part presents a stylised model that identifies 
the strategic avoidance of price impact as a novel eco -
nomic channel through which a currency can domi -
nate FX volumes relative to its use in other areas of 
the global financial system (e.g., official FX reserves, 
trade invoicing, cross-border loans, and safe asset sup -
ply). Specifically, the model provides a set of sufficient 
conditions for dollar dominance. These conditions 
accurately predict which nondollar currency pairs are 
more likely to be exchanged indirectly via the dollar. 
Perhaps surprisingly, the conditions imply that even a 
symmetrical market with identical fundamental trad -
ing demands across currency pairs is prone to dollar 
dominance if dollar pairs feature lower expected price 
impacts.
To be specific, the model incorporates vehicle cur -
rency trading into a demand submission game that 
draws on Rostek and Yoon ( 2021 ). Traders in the 
model represent large institutional investor or dealer 
banks that consider their price impact when exchang -
ing one currency against another. Ideally, traders pre -
fer not to deviate from their fundamental trading 
demand in a given currency pair. However, they are 
willing to deviate from their fundamental trade inter -
est and use a vehicle currency if they are sufficiently 
compensated by trading cost reductions. In general, 
my modelling approach reflects the key features of 
the FX market, which is a decentralised market that is 
organised via a network of limit order books.
In equilibrium, the optimal traded quantity increases 
both in traders’ fundamental and vehicle currency 
trading demand. The latter is determined by the distri -
bution of price impacts across currency pairs. The 
larger the expected price impact in a currency pair, the 
lower the amount of vehicle currency trading. Price 
impact in this model is endogenous and determined by 
the distribution of fundamental trading demands and 
expected currency returns. I show that the equilibrium 
price impact decreases in the variance of fundamental 
trading demands but increases in the variance of 
expected currency returns.
Equipped with the intuition from the comparative 
statics, I derive a set of conditions for dollar domi -
nance, as follows. Conditional on observing larger 
trading volume in dollar pairs relative to their funda -
mental trading demands, a triplet of currency pairs is 
dominated by the U.S. dollar if trading volume in each 
of the two dollar currency pairs exceeds trading volume 
in the direct nondollar cross pair. Based on this defini -
tion, the sufficient condition for dollar dominance is 
that the following three conditions are satisfied: U.S. 
dollar currency pairs exhibit (i) larger average funda -
mental trading demands, (ii) more volatile fundamental 
trading demands, and also (iii) less volatile currency 
returns than nondollar pairs. Therefore, dollar domi -
nance in this model is not a co-ordination result but a 
rationale equilibrium outcome.
The second part of the paper tests the predictions of 
the model using actual FX trade and quote data from 
two sources. First, I use spot FX trading volume and 
order flow data from CLS Group (CLS), which oper -
ates the world’s largest multicurrency cash settlement 
system. CLS is the only source of globally representa -
tive FX trade data that are not specific to a particular 
market segment or trading platform. Second, I pair 
the hourly FX trading volume and order flow data 
with intraday spot rates from Olsen Data.
The empirical analysis employs panel regressions to 
show that the model-based conditions are both eco -
nomically and statistically significant determinants of 
the time and cross-sectional variation in my measure

dollar for vehicle currency trading because of a lower 
expected price impact in dollar currency pairs relative 
to nondollar pairs.
This paper contributes to three strands of literature. 
First, I expand the literature on FX volume by explain -
ing the concentration of FX trading activity in U.S. 
dollar currency pairs both empirically and theoreti -
cally. In contrast to the FX order flow literature (e.g., 
Evans 2002 ; Evans and Lyons 2002 , 2005 ), the litera -
ture on trading volume is relatively scarce because of 
the lack of comprehensive data sets. Earlier research 
has focused largely on the interdealer segment, which 
is dominated by two platforms: Reuters (e.g., Evans 
2002 , Payne 2003 ) and EBS (e.g., Chaboud et al. 2008 , 
Mancini et al. 2013 ). Alternative sources of FX trading 
volume are proprietary data from either specific 
bank holding companies (e.g., Bjønnes and Rime 2005 , 
Froot and Ramadorai 2005 ) or central banks (e.g., 
Czech et al. 2021 ). Relatively recent public access to 
CLS data has enabled researchers to study global FX 
volumes at higher frequencies (e.g., hourly). Fischer 
and Ranaldo ( 2011 ) are the first to study FX volume 
from CLS around central bank decisions using confi -
dential settlement rather than actual FX trade data. Has -
brouck and Levich ( 2017 ) and Ranaldo and Santucci 
de Magistris ( 2022 ) use CLS data to study commonal -
ity in FX volatility and trading volume. Cespa et al. 
( 2021 ) introduce a momentum-based FX trading strat -
egy that conditions on CLS volume.
Second, I add to the monetary economics literature 
on vehicle currency trading. My contribution is two -
fold: (i) provide empirical evidence of vehicle cur -
rency trading, and (ii) derive explicit conditions for 
dollar dominance in FX trading volume. Methodolog -
ically, my model incorporates the market-size (e.g., 
Chrystal 1977 , Krugman 1980 , Rey 2001 , Devereux 
and Shi 2013 ), risk-aversion (e.g., Black 1991 , Hart -
mann 2004 ), and (asymmetric) information-driven 
(Lyons and Moore 2009 ) approaches to international 
currencies. Thus, FX trading volume in my model is a 
function of both fundamental and vehicle currency 
trading demands for a currency.
2
In particular, tra -
ders may trade both directly (nondollar pairs) and via 
the dollar, even in the absence of fundamental trading 
demands for dollar pairs. As a result, vehicle currency 
trading is not an “either/or outcome” in this model, 
which is consistent with the empirical evidence that 
trading activity in nondollar currency pairs accounts 
for a nontrivial share of global FX volume according 
to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) trien -
nial surveys. Furthermore, the empirical evidence on 
vehicle currency trading is largely descriptive and 
lacks comprehensive results because of the scarcity of 
data. I fill this gap by employing a variety of different 
empirical tools to test the predictions of my model 
using a granular FX trade data set.
Third, I contribute to the growing literature on the 
international role of the U.S. dollar
3
and its omnipres -
ence in the global financial system (Farhi and Mag -
giori 2017 , Gourinchas et al. 2019 ). My contribution is 
to show that vehicle currency trading because of stra -
tegic avoidance of price impact is a key driver of dol -
lar dominance in FX trading volumes. Thus, I provide 
complementary insights into general theories of dollar 
dominance that can be classified into three categories. 
The first category is the “trade view,” which argues 
that the reason for dollar dominance is trade invoicing 
in dollars (e.g., Portes and Rey 1998 , Goldberg and 
Tille 2008 , Bahaj and Reis 2020 , Gopinath and Stein 
2020 , Gopinath et al. 2020 , Tille et al. 2021 ). The sec -
ond category is the “safe asset view,” according to 
which dollar dominance arises both from its safe 
haven properties (e.g., Hassan 2013 , Farhi and Mag -
giori 2017 , Maggiori 2017 , He et al. 2019 , Jiang et al. 
2021 ) and from the growing demand for safe assets 
(e.g., Caballero et al. 2008 , 2017 , 2021 ). The third cate -
gory is the “debt view” of dollar dominance (Eren 
and Malamud 2022 ), which emphasises the role of 
firms’ debt currency denomination. Maggiori et al. 
( 2020 ) support this view by showing that global bond 
portfolios are mostly denominated in dollars.
2. Data
The empirical analysis employs trade and quote data 
from two publicly accessible sources. The data set on 
spot FX volume and order flow data comes directly 
from CLS Group (CLS). CLS operates the world’s larg -
est multicurrency cash settlement system and handles 
over 40% of global spot FX transaction volume accord -
ing to Hasbrouck and Levich ( 2017 ) and Cespa et al. 
( 2021 ).

At settlement, CLS alleviates principal and 
operational risk by simultaneously settling both sides 
of the trade. These data have been used previously,

The CLS system is owned by its 72 settlement mem -

banks. To protect member anonymity, CLS has been 
reluctant to disclose any transaction-level information 
about settlement activity. Therefore, the CLS data set 
only contains hourly aggregates of the trading activity 
in each currency pair and provides no information 
about counterparty identities or agreed transaction 
prices. Furthermore, CLS provides no information on 
trade initiators because it solely observes the executed 
trade price used for settlement rather than the market 
behaviour of the bids and offers preceding execution. 
Despite these limitations, CLS data are particularly 
well suited to my analysis because they enable study -
ing the properties of dollar dominance in FX volume 
on a global scale rather than just for a specific market 
segment or trading platform. However, CLS is, of 
course, not able to distinguish between trades that 
occur for fundamental reasons (e.g., portfolio rebalan -
cing) rather than vehicle currency trading motives. I 
tackle this issue in the next section empirically by 
devising a suitable instrument based on nonoverlap -
ping holidays.
From the volume and order flow data sets, I can con -
struct two key variables: interdealer volume and 
dealer-to-customer volume. To do this, I leverage the 
fact that the CLS volume and order flow data are inter -
related. Specifically, volume data include the sum of 
all dealer-to-customer and interdealer trades, whereas 
order flow data contain separate entries for buying 
and selling activity but only for dealer-to-customer 
trades. The buy and sell volume in a given hour and 
currency pair refers to how much of the base currency 
was bought and sold by customers from dealer banks. 
Customers can be categorised into four customer 
groups: corporates, funds, nonbank financial firms, 
and nondealer banks. The difference between aggre -
gate volume and the sum of buying and selling activity 
by customers captures the amount of interdealer vol -
ume. Online Appendix Section C provides details on 
how CLS categorises market participants into custo -
mers, dealers, and nondealer banks.
Next, I pair the hourly FX volume and order flow 
data with intraday spot bid and ask quotes from Olsen 
Data, a market-leading provider of high-frequency 
data and time series management systems. Thus, FX 
trading volume, order flow, and exchange rate returns 
are measured hourly. By compiling historical tick-by- 
tick data from various trading platforms (e.g., IDC, 
Morningstar, and Reuters), these quote data are also 
representative of the global spot FX market rather than 
merely of a specific segment (e.g., interdealer or cus -
tomer-dealer). The full sample period spans Septem -
ber 1, 2012, to September 30, 2022, and includes data 
for 11 major currencies and 25 currency pairs.

3. Motivating Evidence
The purpose of this section is to present descriptive 
evidence supporting the idea that vehicle currency 
trading is a pivotal determinant of dollar dominance 
in FX trading. To do this, the ongoing section pro -
ceeds in two steps. First, it provides prima facie evi -
dence of dollar dominance in spot FX trading volume. 
Second, it carefully establishes a novel stylised fact 
concerning the amount of vehicle currency trading 
activity in the FX market using a novel identification 
strategy based on nonoverlapping holidays.
3.1. Dollar Dominance
Daily trading volume in the spot FX market has 
increased from $400 to $2,100 billion over the past two 
decades, according to the BIS. This rapid rise in volume 
has often been associated with electronification and 
algorithmic trading. According to Rahmouni-Rousseau 
and Churm ( 2018 ), over 8 0% of total trading is executed 
electronically, whereas roughly 7 0% of total spot FX 
volumes on EBS are initiated by algorithms. As a result, 
search costs in today’s market are negligible compared 
with 15 or 20 years ago when FX trading was mostly 
done via telephone. Moreover, at least 8 5% of all spot FX 
trades passing through the CLS settlement system have 
a U.S. dollar leg. This is consistent with what the BIS 
reports in its triennial central bank surveys over the past 
30 years. It is instructive to contrast this amount with the 
U.S. share of global real and financial economic activity, 
which is just around 35%–5 0%. I use this share of global 
GDP, trade, stocks, and debt markets as a benchmark for 
the dominance of the U.S. dollar in FX trading volume.
Figure 1 illustrates the pervasive dominance of the 
U.S. dollar relative to the euro by comparing FX trad -
ing volume to both real and financial economic activ -
ity.
6
It is important to note that the U.S. share of world 
trade accounts for trades that are either invoiced in 
U.S. dollars (Gopinath and Stein 2020 ) or originated in 
countries where the dollar is the official currency (e.g., 
Ecuador). Moreover, the stark discrepancy between 
the dollar share in FX trading volume and the U.S. 
share in real economic activity persists even after 
accounting for any currencies that are pegged to the 
dollar (i.e., Hong Kong and Singapore dollar).

by the forward discount or premium, which I compute

and the spot midquote from Bloomberg. Fifth, follow -

as the difference between the direct dollar interest rate 
in the base currency from the cash market and the syn -
thetic interest rate obtained by swapping the quote 
currency into the base currency. Lastly, I compute the 
relative bid-ask spread as the ratio of the absolute bid- 
ask spread and the midquote price (average of bid and 
ask rates).
In sum, the above approach bears two implications in 
terms of interpreting the estimates of vehicle currency

to appropriately control for variation in fundamental

only capture vehicle currency trading demands from a

3.4. Summary
Taken together, the preliminary empirical evidence in 
this section underscores the idea that the share of trad -
ing volume in dollar currency pairs exceeds the U.S. 
share of global economic activity because of the strate -

this intuition by outlining a simple theoretical frame -

4. Theoretical Framework
This section has two goals. First, I incorporate vehicle 
currency trading within currency pair triplets into 
Rostek and Yoon ( 2021 )’s model. Second, I use com -
parative statics analysis to derive a set of conditions 
for dollar dominance in FX trading volume. This 
model is symmetric in the sense that it does not 
assume that the U.S. dollar is “special” in some way. 
Hence, the same conditions also apply to any other 
currency. The equilibrium is the same as in Rostek 
and Yoon ( 2021 ), and hence, I relegate the detailed 
solution of it to the Online Appendix. Note that the 
model has no closed form solution unless I make sim -
plifying assumptions about the properties of covari -
ance matrices, rendering the equilibrium symmetric. 
To justify these simplifying assumptions (which I 
explain in more detail in the proofs), I simulate the 
model numerically and show that the comparative 
statics based on simulations for nonsymmetric equi -
libria are fully consistent with the ones pertaining to 
the symmetric equilibrium.
The stylised model is motivated by the decentra -
lised nature of the currency market. Hence, traders 
buy and sell currencies from each other in a decentra -
lised market setting. Some of these trades take place 
directly between two traders, whereas others occur 
between traders and dealers. The model does not 
explicitly distinguish trader types or dealers. Traders 
aim to satisfy their fundamental trade interest for cur -
rency pairs. Consider, for instance, an asset manager 
who seeks to invest in Japanese equities but receives 
fund inflows in British pounds. Therefore, the fund 
manager would have a fundamental demand for trad -
ing GBPJPY. When traders accommodate their funda -
mental trading demand, they consider their price 
impact (e.g., Glosten and Harris 1988 ; Stoll 1989 ; 
Foster and Viswanathan 1990 ; Hasbrouck 1991a , b ) 
but abstract away from any additional fixed trading 
costs such as order handling fees, broker fees, and 
bid-ask spreads. This assumption is motivated by, for 
instance, Frazzini et al. ( 2018 ) showing that for institu -
tional investors, the main cost of trading is price 
impact.
10
In the model, traders are willing to deviate 
from their fundamental trading demand and use a 
vehicle currency if they are sufficiently compensated 
by trading cost reductions. Following this intuition, I 
show that such behaviour results in a lower expected 
price impact and, hence, in greater volume in dollar 
pairs.

the existence of limit order books does not imply that 
market clearing exchange rates are determined jointly 
for all currency pairs unless exchange rates are observ -
able without any delay and trades are executed imme -
diately across all trading venues.

4.4.2. Summary. The key economic insight provided 
by the model is threefold. First, traders trade more in 
currency pairs where they face a lower expected price 
impact. Second, this effect is driven by the relative 
riskiness of a currency pair on the one hand and by 
the distribution of fundamental trading demands on 
the other. Hence, if each trader plays equilibrium, 
then strategic avoidance of price impact creates a 
more liquid market for currencies that are either less 
risky or that exhibit more uncertain trading demands. 
Lastly, the comparative statics of the model imply 
three conditions for dollar dominance. The first condi -
tion states that the fundamental demand for dollar 
pairs has to be larger than in nondollar pairs. Condi -
tions two and three operate via the vehicle currency 
trading channel. They imply that the price impact in 
dollar pairs will be larger than in nondollar pairs if 
dollar pairs exhibit more volatile fundamental trading 
demands but less volatile currency returns than non -
dollar pairs.
The model’s testable implications fall into two parts. 
First, it enables me to evaluate the three dominance con -
ditions for the cross-section of currency pair triplets. 
The aim is to understand if the empirical counterparts 
of these conditions are consistent with the actual dollar 
dominance in the data. This is useful not only to predict 
which triplets are dominated by the dollar but also to 
identify which ones are on the verge of switching to 
another currency. Put differently, such an empirical 
exercise can speak to two questions: (i) which condi -
tions are close or far from being “necessary” for dollar 
dominance in trading volume, and (ii) how realistic are 
these conditions for dollar dominance empirically.
Second, the model allows disentangling the relative 
importance of the three conditions for explaining the 
time variation of dollar dominance. To establish this, I 
derive a time-varying empirical measure of dollar 
dominance using actual FX trade data. Following the 
model, dollar dominance increases in the first and sec -
ond conditions but decreases in the third one.
5. Empirical Analysis
This section presents empirical evidence that is consis -
tent with the model in two parts. First, I focus on 
the cross-section of currency pair triplets to evaluate 
which of the three conditions in Proposition 2 are 
empirically supported. Second, I use panel regressions 
to test the model in reduced form and evaluate the rel -
ative importance of each dominance condition.
5.1. Determinants of Dollar Dominance in 
FX Trading
According to the theoretical framework in Section 4 , FX 
trading volume is driven by both fundamental and vehi -
cle currency trading demands. The latter are inversely 
related to price impact: the larger the expected price 
impact in a currency pair, the lower the amount of vehi -
cle currency trading. In the model, this reciprocity is 
governed by two primitives: (i) the variance of funda -
mental trading demands, and (ii) the variance of 
exchange rate returns. The goal of this section is to 
derive an empirical counterpart for fundamental trading 
demands and the two theoretical determinants of price 
impact.
The main challenge for testing the model’s predic -
tions is to identify an empirical proxy for fundamental 
trading demands. The reason is that such a proxy has 
to be available for both dollar and nondollar currency 
pairs and at a sufficiently high frequency to compute 
measures of realised variance. Following the literature 
on dollar dominance, U.S. imports and exports, for -
eign purchases and sales of long-term U.S. securities, 
and dollar-denominated debt instruments that are 
issued outside of the U.S. are obvious low-frequency 
measures of fundamental trading demands that origi -
nate from real trading motives, cross-border financial 
flows, and foreign demand for dollar debt, respec -
tively. Unfortunately, these variables are neither avail -
able at sufficiently high frequencies (e.g., weekly) nor 
for “nondollar currency pairs” (e.g., AUDJPY). For 
instance, to my best knowledge, there is no compre -
hensive source documenting the amount of Australian 
dollar–denominated debt instruments that are issued 
in Japan and vice versa. I overcome this challenge by 
employing dealer banks’ customer order flows as a 
proxy for dealers’ fundamental trading demand.
This measure has two potential limitations. First, it 
implicitly assumes that bank trading is driven mainly 
by customer flows rather than by dealer banks’ opera -
tional (driven by operating expenses) or proprietary 
trading demands. This assumption is reasonable 
given that my sample covers the post–financial crisis 
period where the ability of banks to take proprietary 
positions in OTC markets has been curtailed after the 
introduction of regulatory reforms.

the dealer bank has done any internal netting. Inter -

5.2. Evidence of Dollar Dominance in FX Trading
What follows provides empirical evidence of dollar 
dominance that is consistent with the economic intui -
tion of my model. Theoretically, a triplet of currency 
pairs (e.g., GBPJPY, USDGBP, and USDJPY) will be 
dominated by the U.S. dollar if, conditional on observ -
ing larger trading volume in dollar currency pairs rela -
tive to their fundamental trading demands, at least one 
of the following three conditions is satisfied, whereas the 
other two remain equal: dollar currency pairs exhibit (i) 
larger average fundamental trading demands, (ii) more 
volatile fundamental trading demands, or (iii) less vola -
tile currency returns than nondollar currency pairs. The 
intuition for these conditions stems directly from the 
comparative statics of trading volume in Proposition 1 . 
To avoid clutter, I refer to these conditions as C1, C2, and 
C3 throughout the remainder of the empirical analysis.

5.2.2. Classification. Based on the empirical estimates 
of dollar dominance DD and on the three dominance 
conditions, I classify the 15 triplets in Figure 4 into 
three regions: (i) dollar dominance (title in upper 
case), (ii) multiplicity (title in italics), and (iii) nondol -
lar dominance (title in lower case). First, a triplet of 
currency pairs lies in the region of dollar dominance if 
the dollar is the dominant currency (positive DD) and, 
in addition, all three conditions are jointly satisfied. 
Second, the region of multiplicity characterises triplets 
for which only one or two conditions are satisfied while 
the dollar is still the dominant currency. This supports 
the idea that the status quo of dollar dominance can 
potentially be scrutinised in triplets currently within 
the region of multiplicity. Lastly, currency pair triplets 
lie in the region of nondollar dominance (i.e., euro 
dominance) if the U.S. dollar is not the dominant cur -
rency (negative DD) and if at least two conditions (see 
Proposition 2 ) are violated in the data.
Following this classification, 12 out of 15 triplets of 
currency pairs lie either in the region of multiplicity 
or in that of dollar dominance. Six currency pair tri -
plets lie in the region of multiplicity because the third 
condition (i.e., C3) for realised volatility is not satis -
fied. Nevertheless, these triplets are still dominated by 
the dollar (i.e., positive DD). As a result (and not by 
definition), the currency pair triplets that exhibit dollar 
dominance also satisfy at least one of the three condi -
tions. Furthermore, the first two conditions (i.e., C1 
and C2) with respect to the mean and variance of cus -
tomer trading demands are empirically “necessary” 
for dollar dominance. This insight stems from the 
observation that there is no evidence of dollar domi -
nance (positive DD) unless these two conditions jointly 
hold.
In my sample, the USD-EUR-DKK, USD-EUR-NOK, 
and USD-EUR-SEK triplets are not dominated by the 
dollar in terms of FX trading volume. This is because 
trading volume in at least one of the dollar pairs is less 
than trading volume in the direct nondollar cross 
despite the fact that interdealer volume in dollar pairs 
exceeds customer flows. Moreover, based on the evi -
dence in Table 3 and Figure 4 , the euro seems to enjoy 
regional dominance as a vehicle currency for exchang -
ing Scandinavian currencies against the U.S. dollar. In 
line with this observation, the conditions for euro dom -
inance are largely satisfied for all three aforementioned 
currency triplets (see Table D.5 in the Online Appendix 
for details). In sum, the evidence here underscores the 
general nature of the model in Section 4 in the sense 
that it does not assume that the U.S. dollar is ex ante 
“special” in some way. Put differently, given that the 
model is symmetric, the same set of conditions applies 
to any other currency, and hence, it is straightforward 
to adapt Definition 1 and Proposition 2 to reflect the 
perspective of, for instance, euro dominance.

6. Conclusion
This paper studies how vehicle currency trading con -
tributes to dollar dominance in FX trading volume 
and contributes both theoretically and empirically to 
the prior literature. On the theory side, I use a stylised 
model of FX trading to show how strategic avoidance 
of price impact is a pivotal determinant of dollar dom -
inance in FX trading. The key insight of the model is 
three conditions for dollar dominance that can predict 
which nondollar currency pairs are more likely to 
trade indirectly via the U.S. dollar.
On the empirical side, I apply the model to actual 
FX trade data and document two novel empirical facts 
that corroborate the theoretical framework. First, I use 
nonoverlapping holidays as a novel identification tool 
to disentangle trading volume because of fundamen -
tal trading motives from vehicle currency demands. 
Second, I estimate the model in reduced form and 
present empirical evidence that the three model-based 
conditions for dollar dominance correctly predict the 
actual dollar as well as euro dominance observed in 
the data.
