Liquidity Provision Contracts and Market
Quality: Evidence from the New Y ork Stock
Exchange
Hendrik Bessembinder
Arizona State University
Jia Hao
Babson College and Chinese University of Hong Kong
Kuncheng Zheng
Northeastern University
We exploit a discontinuity in the New Y ork Stock Exchange Designated Market Maker
(DMM) contract to identify causal effects of DMM participation on equilibrium market
outcomes. We document that contractual features that enhance DMM participation are
associated with increased depth, narrower bid-ask spreads, and higher rates of price
improvement, with most of the improvements attributable to increases in liquidity provision
on markets other than the NYSE. These results cannot be attributed to the mechanical effects
of the contractual changes and support the interpretation that market making is characterized
by strategic complementarity. ( JEL G10, G12, G19, G29)

strategic complementarity, as deﬁned by Bulow, Geanakoplos, and Klemperer

aggressive quotes on the NYSE. Documenting strategic complementarity in
liquidity supply is potentially important, because such complementarity implies
that exogenous shocks to liquidity supply will be ampliﬁed in equilibrium.

Clark-Joseph, Y e, and Zi (2016) report a widening of bid-ask spreads for
NYSE-listed ﬁrms traded on non-NYSE markets when the NYSE was forced
by a technological malfunction to stop trading for a portion of the July 8, 2015,
trading day. They attribute the spread increase to the removal of the NYSE
DMM during the trading halt, and support this interpretation by showing that
spreads on average widened more for those securities where DMMs had a
larger pre-closure share of overall trading. However, their evidence is indirect
and does not deﬁnitively rule out that the widening of spreads was attributable
to attributes of the NYSE other than the DMM per se. For example, the
NYSE is the listing exchange for the affected securities, and the majority of
price discovery has traditionally taken place at the NYSE (Hasbrouck 1995).
Further, the NYSE operates the lone remaining physical trading ﬂoor for U.S.
equities, implying that ﬂoor brokers who may be entrusted with large but
conﬁdential orders are also removed from the market when the NYSE ceases
trading. In addition, the NYSE trading glitch was accompanied by reports of
computer systems problems at other companies, including United Airlines
and the Wall Street Journal, which may have fueled fears of a coordinated
attack on information systems (Hope and V aishampayan 2015). The regression
discontinuity design we employ directly assesses the effects on liquidity of
exogenous (to the decisions of the DMM or the exchange) changes in the
obligations and compensation of the NYSE market makers, without potential
obfuscating effects attributable to the shutdown of the entire NYSE trading
system or the need to rely on data from a single trading day.
Our results show that liquidity is improved when the DMM contract
is enhanced. In particular, quoted and effective bid-ask spreads are both
signiﬁcantly smaller, and rates of price improvement (i.e., trade executions
at prices better than the best quotes) are higher when the DMM contract is
enhanced. We rule out that the empirical results obtained here are attributable to
some inherent attribute of the prior month’s volume or other bias in the research
design by conducting placebo tests that focus on counterfactual average daily
volume breakpoints of 0.5 and 1.5 million shares, rather than the actual
breakpoint of 1.0 million shares, and that assess outcomes for Nasdaq-listed
securities and for NYSE securities during a period prior to the implementation
of the current DMM contract. On balance, our results provide strong evidence
that exogenous increases in the scope of DMM obligations and compensation
are associated with improved liquidity. Therefore, they support the Clark-
Joseph, Y e, and Zi (2016) interpretation of their empirical results, and more
broadly show that DMM contracts can improve outcomes for typical securities,
not just those where ﬁrms self-select to employ DMMs.

We also use the discontinuity in the NYSE DMM contract to investigate the
nature of competition in the business of liquidity provision. The enhanced DMM
contract incents, both by obligation and compensation, the NYSE DMM to be
more aggressive in supplying liquidity on the NYSE. NYSE-listed securities are
also quoted and traded off the NYSE. Strikingly, we document lower effective
bid-ask spreads, higher rates of price improvement relative to the NBBO,
and greater quoted depth for non-NYSE markets that quote and trade NYSE-
listed securities, whereas each of these measures, except effective spreads, are
essentially unchanged on the NYSE.
These results support the reasoning that more frequent and aggressive
quotations from the NYSE DMM reduce competitors’ marginal cost of
providing liquidity on the other markets that also trade NYSE securities,
potentially because liquidity providers there understand that they can more
readily unwind unwanted inventory positions against the NYSE DMM’s
quotations. More broadly, the results imply that market making is characterized
by “strategic complementarity” as the term is deﬁned by Bulow, Geanakoplos,
and Klemperer (1985), whereby more aggressive play by one participant
improves the marginal proﬁtability of competitors, leading to more aggressive
play on their part as well.
The effects of DMM obligations on market quality comprise a question of
ﬁrst order importance for those who design and regulate ﬁnancial markets.
While any market participant can choose to provide liquidity on an electronic
limit order market, in practice most liquidity is provided by high-frequency
trading ﬁrms.
9 In the absence of DMM contracts, high-frequency ﬁrms can

1.2 Strategic complementarity
Here, we show that the enhanced NYSE DMM contract improves market
liquidity. This evidence, obtained for a DMM contract that applies to all listed
securities, complements the ﬁndings of improved liquidity in the wake of
the selective adoption of DMM contracts for some securities on European
Exchanges, as documented by V enkataraman and Waisburd (2007), Anand,
Tanggaard, and Weaver (2009), Skjeltorp and Ødegaard (2015), and Menkveld
and Wang (2013).
Potentially more important, our evidence sheds light on the economics of
liquidity supply. The shifts in NYSE DMM obligations and compensation at
the 1-million-shares threshold are modest, yet we document substantial effects
on equilibrium market liquidity. In a similar vein, the evidence of improved
liquidity after the adoption of DMM contracts on the Stockholm Exchange
presented by Anand, Tanggaard, and Weaver (2009) occurs even though the
magnitude of the direct compensation to DMM ﬁrms, which is presumably
sufﬁcient to offset any costs of providing additional liquidity, averages only
about $3,000 per month.
We propose that the reason that relatively small changes in incentives
attributable to DMM contracts can have substantive effects on market outcomes
is that liquidity supply is characterized by “strategic complementarity.” Bulow,
Geanakoplos, and Klemperer (1985) introduced this term to refer to situations
where an increase in an activity by one player increases the marginal beneﬁt of
the activity to other players. Strategic complementarity is important in ﬁnancial
markets, because it implies the existence of “ampliﬁcation” effects, where
the impact of a given exogenous shock is magniﬁed by strategic interactions
between players.
Ampliﬁcation resulting from strategic interactions has been identiﬁed in a
number of studies. Chen, Goldstein, and Jiang (2010) note that mutual fund
outﬂows impose costs that are borne by investors who remain in the fund,
implying that the marginal beneﬁt of immediate exit increases when other
investors exit. They show that this strategic complementarity manifests itself
in stronger outﬂows in reaction to negative performance news at equity mutual
funds that hold less liquid assets. Related, Goldstein, Jiang, and Ng (2017)
document an asymmetry whereby corporate bond mutual fund ﬂows are more
sensitive to negative than to positive performance news. The resultant “ﬁrst
seller” advantage implies that bond fund ﬂows may amplify the effects of
negative bond performance.
Barlevy and V eronesi (2000) present a model where asset prices depend
both on information regarding fundamentals and on random liquidity shocks.

2. Regression Discontinuity Research Design
To identify the impact of the NYSE DMM contract on market quality, we use a
regression discontinuity research design focused on the discrete shift in DMM
obligations and compensation at 1 million shares for the prior’s month average
daily trading volume. The regression discontinuity design (RDD) is a quasi-
experimental approach with the deﬁning characteristic that the probability of
receiving treatment changes discontinuously as a function of one or more
underlying variables (Hahn, Todd, and van der Klaauw 2001; Cameron and
Trivedi 2005). RDD methods have been widely adopted in Economics and
Finance research in recent years.
14

calendar month corresponds to the treatment of the enhanced DMM contract, in
that the DMM is required to maintain quotes at the NBBO for at least 15% of the
time and receives a higher liquidity-supply rebate. Firm-months with trading
volume of equal to or just more than 1 million shares provide the control, as
the DMM is required to maintain quotes at NBBO only 10% of the day and
receives a smaller rebate.

The intuition behind the RDD is that observations below and above the
cutoff can be compared directly to draw inferences regarding the effect of the
treatment. The RDD design is valid if the average daily trading volume in
a month can be considered as essentially randomly assigned below or above
the threshold. The key assumption of the RDD is that individuals are unable
to precisely manipulate the assignment variable, implying that treatment is
randomized. This assumption is empirically testable, as described by McCrary
(2008). In particular, the marginal density of the assignment variable should be
continuous in the vicinity of the threshold. In contrast, a jump in the density of
the assignment variable at the threshold would provide evidence of endogenous
selection and would invalidate the appropriateness of the RD design. In terms
of our study, if DMMs or ﬁrms could manipulate the assignment variable

Implementing the RDD design requires the selection of a “bandwidth,” that
is, the range of volume around the 1-million-shares threshold to be used to
estimate model parameters. The smaller the bandwidth the more accurate is the
implicit assumption that ﬁrms are randomly assigned to either side of the 1-
million-shares threshold. However, statistical power is reduced with a narrower
bandwidth due to the smaller sample size. Imbens and Kalyanaraman (2012,
hereafter IK) develop an optimal bandwidth based on minimization of the mean
square error (MSE). Their method trades off the bias introduced by use of a
larger bandwidth against the reduction in the standard error attributable to a
larger sample size. Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014, hereafter CCT)
introduce a nonparametric method that reﬁnes the IK method to both identify
the optimal bandwidth and to remove the estimated bias. Calonico et al. (2017,
hereafter CCFT) provide a Stata package that identiﬁes the optimal bandwidth
with an upgraded version of the bandwidth selector.
In our setting, the DMM obligation varies for a given security across months
based on the aggregated average daily trading volume in the previous month.
Liquidity is known to vary as a function of endogenous volume and other factors,
so we include appropriate control variables, as well as time and ﬁrm ﬁxed
effects. Unfortunately, the CCFT bias correction appears to not be well equipped
to handle the computational burden created by the inclusion of ﬁxed effects
for a large number of securities.
15 Therefore, we proceed by using the local
linear method proposed by IK, with optimal bandwidths from CCFT. 16 While
our point estimates remain biased in ﬁnite samples, we assess the effects of
potential bias by estimating each regression using bandwidths both greater and
less than the optimal. The results generally indicate larger point estimates with
smaller bandwidths (though accompanied by higher standard errors resulting
from the smaller sample). That is, the results indicate that the estimates obtained
at the optimal bandwidths are understated, that is, biased toward, rather than
away from, zero.
Speciﬁcation (2) controls for the direct effects of trading activity by
the inclusion of trading activity as an explanatory variable. The market
microstructure literature has documented (e.g., Stoll 1978; Benston and
Hagerman 1974; O’Hara 1995; Demsetz 1968) that additional variables,
including share price and return volatility, have pervasive explanatory power
for market liquidity. We therefore include inverse price and realized return
volatility as additional explanatory variables when we estimate expression (2).

The notion of strategic complementarity, introduced by Bulow, Geanakoplos,
and Klemperer (1985), is plausible in the case of DMM commitments, because
other ﬁrms or individuals who also supply liquidity in the same securities can
be more certain that they will be able to ofﬂoad unwanted inventory positions
against the DMM’s quotations. Strategic complementarity in liquidity provision
may be of particular importance, because its existence would imply also the
presence of spillover effects, where a shock to liquidity supplied by some
participants for reasons other than shifts in DMM incentives or obligations
also would be associated with same-direction changes in liquidity supply by
other market participants.

These results imply that the enhanced NYSE DMM contract affects the
strategies employed by other suppliers of liquidity as well. The ﬁnding that
depth on the NYSE is not improved while depth off the NYSE is improved
when the NYSE DMM contract is enhanced may arise from substitution effects
motivated in part by the fact that regulations do not enforce time priority (by
which the ﬁrst order to be posted at a given price receives execution priority)
across the various markets that trade NYSE securities, even while time priority
is enforced across orders at a given exchange. A liquidity supplier intending to
submit an order at the BBO who observes an existing NYSE order at the same
price might choose to submit the order to an alternative market instead. More
frequent NYSE DMM orders at the BBO may therefore lead other liquidity
suppliers to substitute their order submissions priced at the BBO to markets
other than the NYSE.
However, such a substitution effect in order submission does not explain
the ﬁnding of improved rates of price improvement off the NYSE. The “trade
through” rule (part of Securities and Exchange Commission Regulation NMS,
adopted in 2006) speciﬁes that trades cannot be executed at prices inferior to
the best quotation available on any electronically accessible exchange, but does
not require price improvement relative to the best quotes. Further, NYSE Rule
104 itself does not require the DMM to quote at a price that improves on the
existing NBBO quote or to execute trades at better prices. The higher rate of
price improvement off the NYSE associated with the enhanced DMM contract
therefore reﬂect endogenous changes in outcomes in the market for liquidity
supply.

Geanakoplos, and Klemperer (1985). To further assess the channels by which
strategic complementarity arises will likely require data identifying the class
of trader who submit each order.
This result implies positive spillover effects in liquidity supply and is
potentially important to those who develop theoretical models of equilibrium
in securities markets. The result also helps to explain why a DMM contract
that applies to only a single market participant and that would appear to have
only minor direct effects on the DMM’s quotation behavior can have a more
substantive effect on equilibrium liquidity supply. Finally, the result helps to
explain illiquidity spirals, as a shock (e.g., because of deterioration in funding
conditions or the value of collateral) to some participants’ willingness or ability
to supply liquidity can reduce liquidity supply by their competitors as well.
6. Conclusions
A key question facing those who design or regulate securities markets is whether
the electronic limit order book can attract sufﬁcient liquidity. A number of
stock markets have voluntarily adopted DMMs for at least some securities,
and recent MiFID II regulations require DMM contracts for algorithmic ﬁrms
on European markets, while leaving the parameters of the contracts to the
discretion of individual Exchanges. However, our collective understanding of
economics of DMM contracts remains quite incomplete.
We contribute to the understanding of these issues by studying DMMs
on the New Y ork Stock Exchange. The NYSE designates a DMM for every
listed security, so selection bias in the securities with DMMs is not a concern
with our research design. We exploit the fact that NYSE Rule 104 speciﬁes a
discontinuity in both DMM obligations and compensation. In particular, DMMs
are (1) required to maintain orders at the NBBO more frequently and (2) receive
larger rebates for supplying liquidity for securities where the prior month’s
average daily volume was less than the threshold level of 1 million shares.
We use a regression discontinuity research design to estimate the causal
effect of the enhanced DMM contract on market liquidity and trading volume.
The RDD design can provide unbiased estimates of the causal effect of a
change in DMM obligations and incentives. At the same time, the Regression

Our results indicate the enhanced DMM contract in effect when the prior

liquidity, in the form of lower average quoted and effective bid-ask spreads,
as well as greater market depth, higher rates of price improvement and higher
trading volume. These results therefore support Clark-Joseph, Y e, and Zi (2016)
in their interpretation that the widening of quoted spreads observed when the
NYSE abruptly ceased trading on July 8, 2015, was indeed attributable at least in
part to the fact that the NYSE DMMs were removed from the market. However,
our evidence is more speciﬁc, in that we can link liquidity directly to changes in
the DMM obligation, without the potentially confounding effects of eliminating
the entire NYSE infrastructure.
While the results reported here are informative, they are also in some ways
puzzling. The NYSE DMM contract calls only for the DMM to maintain orders
that match the best bid and offer for a relatively small percentage of each trading
day. The contract does not require that the DMM narrow bid-ask spreads by
entering orders at prices superior to the NBBO quotes. Further, the enhanced
rebates for supplying liquidity in low volume securities amounts to only about
0.13 bps relative to share prices. As a consequence, the observed magnitudes
of the improvements in bid ask spreads and rates of price improvement, and the
increased trading activity associated with the more stringent DMM obligations
are unlikely to simply arise mechanically. One possibility, as suggested by
Corwin and Coughenour (2008) is that DMMs have limited attention, and that
the more binding DMM constraint motivates them to focus their attention on
securities with the more binding obligation. However, this perspective cannot
provide a complete explanation, as we ﬁnd that rates of price improvement
are increased, market depth is increased, and average effective spreads are
decreased when the NYSE DMM contract is enhancedeven for trades executed
off the NYSE.
These results provide strong, if indirect, evidence that the knowledge that
NYSE DMM contract is enhanced affects other traders’ order submission
strategies and equilibrium in the market for liquidity. In particular, the results
support the reasoning that market-making equilibrium is characterized by
strategic complementarity in the sense of Bulow, Geanakoplos, and Klemperer
(1985), where more aggressive play by one supplier (the DMM) results in
decreased marginal costs for competitors. This result would arise if competitors
are more certain that they can unwind unwanted inventory positions when the
DMM obligation is more binding. The existence of strategic complementarity
implies positive spillover effects in the business of liquidity supply. Such
positive spillovers are important for theoreticians seeking to develop models
