In a dynamic principal-agent model, the principal, ﬁnancing the project, cannot observe
project failure and the agent, developing the project, can hide failure. As there is a tension
between incentives for disclosure of failure and project development, the optimal contract
does not reward failure and incentivize disclosure of failure during an initial uncondi-
tional ﬁnancing stage. During the subsequent disclosure stage, time-decreasing rewards for
failure provide incentives for disclosure of failure. The continuation of ﬁnancing becomes
more performance-sensitive across stages, and the agent’s incentives are backloaded. The
model explains several empirical patterns in venture capital ﬁnancing and the ﬁnancing of
innovation.

The example of Theranos highlights key diﬃculties in-
herent to ﬁnancing innovative projects. Innovative projects
typically i) exhibit substantial failure risk, ii) require a high
level of expertise from insiders developing the project, and
iii) require capital from investors with limited expertise.
To resolve agency conﬂicts between the insiders, devel-
oping the project, and investors, ﬁnancing the project,
the provision of ﬁnancing must be contingent on project
outcomes. However, when it is hard for investors to track
project progress, insiders can hide bad outcomes, such as
project failure. This paper studies dynamic contracting in
light of this tension.
In the model, a principal ﬁnances a project developed
by an agent with limited liability. Project development
requires funds from the principal and, absent frictions, it
is eﬃcient to ﬁnance the project until completion. The

affected by the agent. Completion results in either success
or failure, whereby the agent’s hidden effort during project
development determines the likelihood of success. Moral
hazard arises because the agent derives private beneﬁts
from shirking. Incentives for effort are provided by paying
the agent more for success than for failure. If both po-
tential project outcomes, success and failure, are publicly
observable and contractible, the principal pays the agent
only for success and ﬁnances the project until completion.
This paper studies incentive provision when it is hard
for the principal to observe and verify project failure
(whereas success is perfectly observable and contractible).
Project failure is observed by the agent but possibly (i.e.,
with some probability) not observed by the principal. Be-
cause it is eﬃcient to terminate ﬁnancing once the project
fails, the principal would like the agent to disclose failure.
However, the agent can hide failure to prevent the termi-
nation of ﬁnancing, which allows the agent to continue the
project after failure and yields private beneﬁts from doing
so. In addition, as failure and reports thereof are not ver-
iﬁable by the principal, the agent can misreport (i.e., fake)
failure before it occurs, which precipitates the termination
of ﬁnancing and ends project development prematurely.
To incentivize the agent to disclose failure at the time
it occurs, the contract stipulates rewards (pay) for failure.
These rewards for failure must decrease over time, because
otherwise the agent would delay disclosure of failure.
However, as the agent is rewarded for disclosing failure, he
ﬁnds it tempting to misreport (fake) failure before it oc-
curs and to seize these rewards for failure. To prevent this
outcome and hence to incentivize the agent to continue
project development, it becomes necessary to increase the
agent’s stake in the project by raising rewards for success,
leading to excessive rewards for success and agency rents.
That is, a tension arises between providing incentives for
disclosure of failure and project development.
As a result, the principal faces the following trade-off
when designing the contract. On the one hand, ﬁnancing a
failed project is ineﬃcient, so the principal ideally would
like the agent to disclose failure and to terminate ﬁnancing
upon failure. On the other hand, incentivizing disclosure
of failure is costly, as it leads to excessive agency rents.
In light of this trade-off, the optimal contract does not
always incentivize disclosure of failure and consists of two
distinct stages: an unconditional ﬁnancing stage followed
by a disclosure stage . During the unconditional ﬁnancing
stage, the principal does not incentivize disclosure of fail-
ure, so the project is potentially ﬁnanced and ineﬃciently
continued after failure. The unconditional ﬁnancing stage
can also be interpreted as contractual grace or proba-
tionary period during which the agent is not (always)
ﬁred after certain bad outcomes. Moreover, the agent i) is
not paid for failure, ii) receives low rewards for success,
and iii) incurs mild punishments for delays (beyond her
inﬂuence) as rewards for success decrease over time. The
unconditional ﬁnancing stage ends with a soft deadline at
which the principal elicits a truthful progress report from
the agent on whether the project has failed so far. The
principal ﬁnances the project over the next stage, i.e., the
disclosure stage, if and only if the progress report reveals
that the project has not failed yet.
During the disclosure stage, the principal incentivizes
disclosure of failure and ﬁnances the project until either
completion is reported or a hard deadline is reached.
Thus, ﬁnancing is terminated upon failure and therefore
performance-sensitive. During this stage, the agent re-
ceives high and time-decreasing rewards for failure and
success and incurs harsh punishments for delays that
include the threat of contract termination. The disclosure
stage ends with a hard deadline at which the principal
terminates ﬁnancing regardless of whether the project is
still proﬁtable to pursue. In summary, the optimal con-
tract consists of different (ﬁnancing) stages, and involves
tolerance towards failure through both i) pay for failure
and ii) a grace period. The provision (continuation) of
ﬁnancing becomes more performance-sensitive over time
and across stages. The agent’s incentives are backloaded
and so become stronger over time and across stages.
Our ﬁndings have implications for venture capital ﬁ-
nancing, whereby the principal represents the venture cap-
italist (VC) who ﬁnances a startup run by an entrepreneur
or founder (agent). According to our model, the provision
(continuation) of ﬁnancing becomes more performance-
sensitive over time and across stages. Speciﬁcally, opti-
mal VC contracts feature an initial unconditional ﬁnancing
stage during which the VC does not elicit disclosure of fail-
ure or, more broadly, soft information. This initial uncondi-
tional ﬁnancing period implies a relatively low likelihood
that a VC-ﬁnanced startup declares failure and is termi-
nated in the early stages/years of ﬁnancing, in line with the
empirical ﬁndings of Puri and Zarutskie (2012) . The model
also predicts that the entrepreneur’s incentives (to exert
effort) are backloaded and become stronger over time.
Venture capital contracts typically include vesting provi-
sions for the entrepreneur ( Kaplan and Strömberg, 2003 ),
consistent with the notion of backloaded incentives. Pro-
vided that incentives boost the likelihood of success, back-
loaded incentives imply that the (marginal) probability
of startup success should increase over time and across
stages, which is documented by Puri and Zarutskie (2012) .
Importantly, the entrepreneur should be compensated
for failure (i.e., liquidation or an acquisition/sale of the
startup that leads to relatively low payoff) only in the later
ﬁnancing stages. Kaplan and Strömberg (2003) indeed
document that in the later ﬁnancing stages, the founder
is more likely to have liquidation rights or claims that are
senior/par to VC cumulative investment. Then, in the later
ﬁnancing stages, these rewards for failure decrease over
time, which could be a consequence of staged ﬁnancing.
With staged ﬁnancing, the startup undergoes several
ﬁnancing rounds that repeatedly dilute the entrepreneur’s
stake in the startup, thereby reducing her rewards for
failure (and success) over time. In addition, as rewards for
failure decrease over time, the VC’s residual payoff upon
failure and so the VC’s downside protection increases
over time, in line with the ﬁndings of Bengtsson and
Sensoy (2011) .

Interestingly, the optimal contract for projects
that generate results quickly stipulates a relatively
long unconditional ﬁnancing stage but a short hard
deadline. This shape of the optimal contract sug-
gests that projects, generating results quickly, are
particularly suitable for a “spray and pray” invest-
ment approach in which VCs provide funding to
(many) startups over a relatively short horizon with
limited governance and oversight ( Ewens et al., 2018 ).
During the contract’s unconditional ﬁnancing stage,
the principal may ineﬃciently ﬁnance a failed project
with negative net present value (NPV), leading to over-
investment or over-provision of ﬁnancing. In contrast,
during the contract’s disclosure stage, incentive provision
may require premature termination of a project with pos-
itive NPV, leading to under-investment or under-provision
of ﬁnancing. As a result, within the optimal contract, there
can be instances of both under- and over-investment. The
model implies that over-investment (under-investment)
tends to arise for projects that (do not) generate results
relatively quickly. In the context of venture capital ﬁnanc-
ing, the model therefore predicts ineﬃciently high venture
capital investment in projects that generate results quickly
—s u c h as information technology projects ( Kerr et al.,
2014 ) —a n d ineﬃciently low investment in projects that
do not generate results quickly —s u c h as renewable
energy production ( Nanda et al., 2014 ).
The model also applies to more general types of ﬁrms.
In the context of executive compensation contracts, the
model highlights the tension that while instruments like
golden handshakes, golden parachutes, and severance pay
provide incentives for truthful disclosure of bad outcomes,
they may also provide incentives to generate bad outcomes
on purpose (e.g., an acquisition that is not desirable for
shareholders) and/or to leave the ﬁrm. As a result, these
instruments should not be in place in the early stages of
the contract (i.e., CEO tenure). Crucially, once in place, the
dollar value of these instruments should decrease over
time and the CEO should be provided strong incentives
through performance pay that incentivize the CEO not to
generate bad outcomes.
Finally, we analyze the role of monitoring and inspec-
tions in incentive provision. For this purpose, we assume
that the principal can inspect the project’s progress at a
cost. Upon inspection, the principal learns whether the
project has failed so far. That is, an inspection enables the
principal to detect whether the agent hides failure and
allows punishment of that misbehavior with termination.
The optimal contract (with monitoring) features several
unconditional ﬁnancing and disclosure stages, whereby
periodic inspections occur at deterministic dates and an
unconditional ﬁnancing stage is followed by a disclosure
stage. At the end of a disclosure stage, the principal
inspects the project and grants ﬁnancing for the next
stage — which is an unconditional ﬁnancing stage —i f
the inspection reveals that the project is still proﬁtable
to pursue. Importantly, inspections and contract termina-
tion are substitutes in incentive provision, generating the
prediction that venture capitalists with high expertise and
ability to oversee/inspect a startup’s operations tend to
provide ﬁnancing over a longer horizon. Our model also
provides an explanation for the positive relationship be-
tween VC monitoring and startup success ( Bernstein et al.,
2016 ): VCs who regularly inspect and monitor the startup
are less likely to terminate ﬁnancing prematurely, thereby
increasing the likelihood of eventual success. In addition,
our ﬁndings imply that VCs with more expertise and/or
a better monitoring skills elicit more frequent progress
reports, tend to incentivize disclosure of failure more, and
therefore tend to terminate ﬁnancing in failing ventures
earlier.

Relatedly, DeMarzo and Fishman (2007b,a) study dy-
namic ﬁnancial contracting in a discrete time setting.
Piskorski and Westerﬁeld (2016) ; Orlov (2018) , and
Malenko (2019) analyze incentive provision with optimal

Prat (2016) ; Varas et al. (2020) , and Marinovic and Szyd-

Our framework is similar to Green and Tay-
lor (2016) and Varas (2017) . Green and Taylor (2016) study
contracting for a multistage project when intermediate
progress is privately observed. They derive an optimal
contract that involves a period during which ﬁnancing is
guaranteed whereby the agent is rewarded for good but
never for bad outcomes. Varas (2017) studies managerial
short-termism in a dynamic model and ﬁnds that the
threat of termination and deferred payouts for success
provide eﬃcient incentives. Our analysis differs from
these papers along several dimensions. First, in our model
project development may lead to two types of adverse
outcomes, failure and completion delays, whereas in
Green and Taylor (2016) and Varas (2017) only completion
delays may arise. Second, our paper studies incentives
to truthfully disclose, i.e., not to hide or to fake, bad
outcomes. In Green and Taylor (2016) and Varas (2017) the
moral hazard problem is different in that the agent is

paper is to study incentive provision for disclosure of bad
outcomes in a dynamic environment so that the agent’s

future. As such, our analysis yields the novel implications
that to incentivize truthful disclosure, the rewards for
disclosing bad outcomes must decrease over time and it
is not optimal to incentivize disclosure of bad outcomes in
the early contract stages.

3.3. The optimal contract
When there is a chance that the principal observes (i.e.,
π > 0 ), a full disclosure contract requires to promise the
agent excessively high rewards for success, and therefore
is costly. As we show in the following, the optimal con-
tract then does not always incentivize disclosure of failure
when π > 0 .
Thus, suppose that the contract stipulates no rewards
for failure over [ t 0 , t 1 ) , in that βt = γt = 0 for t ∈ [ t 0 , t 1 ) .
Consequently, the agent never reports and never fakes
failure over [ t 0 , t 1 ) . As the contract does not incentivize
disclosure of failure over [ t 0 , t 1 ) , it is also not terminated
over [ t 0 , t 1 ) due to disclosure of failure. In other words, the
principal provides unconditional ﬁnancing over [ t 0 , t 1 ) . Cru-
cially, the provision of unconditional ﬁnancing generates
incentives not to fake failure (i.e., relaxes incentive con-
straint (7) ) and hence limits agency rents but comes at the
expense that the project may be continued and ﬁnanced
after failure, which is ineﬃcient. Although a contract could
stipulate unconditional ﬁnancing over several distinct time
intervals, we focus in the following discussion on the
latest interval in time denoted by [ t 0 , t 1 ) . Thereafter, we
verify that under the optimal contract there is only one
(connected) time interval with unconditional ﬁnancing.

who “never” privately observes failure (if failure occurs).

high rents W 0 ex-ante at t = 0 , irrespective of whether the

failure (if it occurs) and so “overpays” this agent under a

3.3.4. Discussion: uniqueness of the optimal contract
The optimal contract and in particular the choice of α
and γ are not unique, precisely because the optimal full
disclosure contract is not unique. Proposition 3 describes
the optimal contract that maximizes incentives, and its
generalization is presented in Proposition 6 in the Ap-
pendix. In what follows, we propose a model extension in
which the contract from Proposition 3 is (strictly) optimal
and the choice of γ is uniquely pinned down. Speciﬁcally,
we consider that when the agent hides failure from the
principal over [ t , t + dt ) , with probability λF dt , project
failure becomes publicly observed or detected, leading
to project termination and rewards for publicly observed
failure for the agent γt . Note that λF > 0 may reﬂect that
bad news and failure hidden by insiders can leak out to
the public (e.g., via whistleblowing), which for instance
was the case for Theranos.
We study the agent’s incentives to hide failure and to
delay disclosure after she has privately observed failure
at time t during the disclosure stage. Disclosure of failure
at time t yields βt dollars. If the agent discloses failure
at time t + dt, she receives private beneﬁts of shirking
φdt. With probability 1 − λF dt, the principal does not
observe (detect) failure over [ t , t + dt ) so that the agent
receives βt+ dt dollars from reporting failure at time t + dt.
With probability λF dt, the principal observes failure over
[ t , t + dt ) , leading to rewards for publicly observed failure
γt . Thus, the agent prefers to disclose failure at time t if
and only if

4. Analysis
4.1. Application —v e n t u r e capital ﬁnancing
The main application of our model is venture capital
ﬁnancing. In this context, the principal represents the
venture capitalist (VC) and the agent represents the en-
terpreneur(s) or founder(s) of the startup ﬁnanced by the
VC. As is well known, VCs tend to ﬁnance innovative and
complex projects that are subject to high failure risk and

agency conﬂicts between the founder(s) and the VC. Below,
we discuss several empirical implications of our model.
According to our model, optimal VC ﬁnancing contracts
do not elicit disclosure of failure or, more broadly, soft
information in the early ﬁnancing stages. Related, the pro-
vision (continuation) of ﬁnancing by the VC becomes more
performance-sensitive over time and across stages. In the
data, we do not observe a startup’s actual failure date but
only the date on which the startup “declares failure” (i.e.,
is sold/liquidated/shut down). As a result, we should —
all else equal —o b s e r v e in the data relatively low failure
rates for VC-ﬁnanced startup ﬁrms in the early years of
ﬁnancing. Consistent with our ﬁndings, Puri and Zarut-
skie (2012) ﬁnd that especially in the early stages (years)
of ﬁnancing, VC-ﬁnanced ﬁrms are less likely to declare
failure than comparable non VC-ﬁnanced ﬁrms (whereas
any differences in failure rates between VC-ﬁnanced and
non-VC ﬁnanced ﬁrms taper off in subsequent years). Our
analysis suggests that the reason underlying this outcome
is that in the early stages/years of ﬁnancing, VCs tend to
elicit disclosure of failure and soft information less than
non VC investors. Related, Puri and Zarutskie (2012) show
that at the time of failure, VC-ﬁnanced ﬁrms are larger
than comparable non VC-ﬁnanced ﬁrms in terms of em-
ployees. Our analysis also offers an explanation for this
result: because VCs do not always incentivize immediate
disclosure of failure, VC-ﬁnanced ﬁrms tend to receive ﬁ-
nancing longer after failure and so can grow longer before
declaring failure (liquidation) than non VC-ﬁnanced ﬁrms.
Interestingly, Puri and Zarutskie (2012) write in their
introduction: “These results are consistent with the inter-
pretation that there is a window over which VCs allow
ﬁrms to continue and grow, but once this period is over
VCs are just as likely to shut their ﬁrms down.” This initial
period resembles the unconditional ﬁnancing stage of
the optimal contract. Our model delivers novel empirical
predictions regarding the length of this unconditional
ﬁnancing stage. In particular, as we show in Section 4.3 ,
the unconditional ﬁnancing stage is relatively short when
agency conﬂicts ( φ) are large (e.g., due to high asset
intangibility or complexity of the project) or when the
project is more likely to produce (at least intermediate)
results quickly (i.e., 1 / /Lambda1is low). That is, the relative length
of the unconditional ﬁnancing stage, t 1 /T , increases with
φ and decreases with1 / /Lambda1.
The quantity t 1 /T —t h a t is, the length of the un-
conditional ﬁnancing stage t 1 relative to total contract
duration T —c a p t u r e s the principal’s propensity to con-
tinue ﬁnancing the project after its failure. Therefore, t 1 /T
is related to the measure of VC investors’ tolerance for
failure in Tian and Wang (2014) , that is, their willingness
to continue ﬁnancing underperforming ventures. Tian and
Wang (2014) empirically document a positive relationship
between VC investors’ tolerance for failure and innovation
(measured by the number of produced patents as well as
their citations). Our ﬁndings provide an explanation for
these ﬁndings that is based on agency conﬂicts regarding
the disclosure of failure. Because innovative projects (i.e.,
projects that are likely to produce innovative outcomes
such as patents) are complex and characterized by severe
agency conﬂicts (high φ), the optimal ﬁnancing contract
features a relatively long unconditional ﬁnancing stage
and so tends to incentivize disclosure of failure less, thus
increasing the likelihood that the project continues to
receive ﬁnancing after failure. 18
Another key prediction of our model is that the en-
trepreneur’s incentives (to exert effort) are backloaded
across stages and time (i.e., become stronger over time).
Kaplan and Strömberg (2003) document that venture
capital contracts commonly feature vesting provision for
the founders, consistent with the notion of backloaded
incentives. In addition, provided that stronger incen-
tives translate into a higher likelihood of success, the
model predicts that the marginal probability of success
is higher in the later ﬁnancing stages. 19 Moreover, as the
entrepreneur discloses failure in the later but not in the
early ﬁnancing stages, in the early ﬁnancing stages, the
VC might end up ﬁnancing failed startups that cannot
succeed anymore. That is, the pool of startups that receive
ﬁnancing from the VC improves over time and across
ﬁnancing stages and so does the marginal probability of
success. Indeed, Puri and Zarutskie (2012) document that
the marginal probability of successful exit via an IPO or
acquisition increases over time.
The model also predicts that during the later stages
of VC ﬁnancing (i.e., during the disclosure stage of the
optimal contract), it can be optimal to reward the founder
for failure. In practice, any exit that generates relatively
low payoffs could be interpreted as failure, such as the
liquidation, sale, or “low acquisition” of the startup. 20
Rewards for failure could be implemented by granting the
entrepreneur liquidation rights or claims that are senior or
par to the VC’s cumulative investment. According to our
model, such rewards for failure are more common in the
later stages of VC ﬁnancing, consistent with the empirical
ﬁndings of Kaplan and Strömberg (2003) . 21

More broadly, our model implies that in the early ﬁ-
nancing stages, startup founders are only paid for positive
outcomes, but in later stages, they may be paid for other
outcomes too. Interestingly, Ewens et al. (2020) document
that cash compensation for startup founders tends to
be higher and more prevalent in the later stages of VC
ﬁnancing and in particular after reaching certain mile-
stones. In the context of their paper, our ﬁndings imply
that startup CEOs should receive severance pay only in the
later ﬁnancing stages (or after certain milestones).
There are several contractual possibilities to implement
that the entrepreneur is not rewarded for failure in the
early ﬁnancing stages. Typically, the VC holds claims
that are senior to the founders’ claims in the startup,
such as preferred equity or (convertible) debt. These
“debt-like” claims provide downside protection to the VC
( Bengtsson and Sensoy, 2011 ). According to our model, the
VC’s claim tends to be “debt-like” in the early ﬁnancing
stages. In addition, our results suggest that venture debt
— which is increasingly common in the early ﬁnancing
stages ( Davis et al., 2020 ) —i s beneﬁcial for incentivizing
the entrepreneur to choose the optimal exit timing and, in
particular, not to exit too early.
Importantly, our model predicts that in the later stages
of VC ﬁnancing, the agent receives time-decreasing re-
wards for failure and success. Recall that rewards for
failure decrease over time to incentivize the agent not to
delay disclosure of failure. In practice, the entrepreneur’s
time-decreasing payoffs (for both failure and success)
could be a consequence of the repeated dilution of the
entrepreneur’s stake in the startup when the startup holds
a new ﬁnancing round to raise funds (which requires
investors to grant a stake in the startup). This mecha-
nism is similar to the dilution of early startup investors
in later ﬁnancing rounds ( Bengtsson and Sensoy, 2015 ).
The fact that VC ﬁnancing is provided in several stages
and ﬁnancing rounds implies the gradual dilution of the
entrepreneur’s stake and so can induce time-decreasing
rewards for failure in the later ﬁnancing stages. Hence, we
conclude that staged ﬁnancing endows the entrepreneur
with incentives not to delay disclosure of failure and, more
broadly, to choose the optimal exit timing.
Observe that ceteris paribus, failure rewards for the
entrepreneur decrease the residual payoff upon failure
that is left for the VC; thus, rewards for failure (for
the entrepreneur) are likely to correlate negatively with
investor downside protection. Notably, Bengtsson and Sen-
soy (2011) document a signiﬁcantly positive relationship
between VC downside protection (which can be seen as
the inverse of the entrepreneur’s rewards for failure) and
startup age. According to our ﬁndings, this relationship
could reﬂect that in the later ﬁnancing stages, the en-
trepreneur’s rewards for failure decrease and therefore VC
downside protection increases over time.
Moreover, the structure of the rewards for failure sug-
gested in Proposition 3 generates the empirical prediction
that the entrepreneur’s reward from failure tends to be
highest in the intermediate ﬁnancing stages. Assuming
that these rewards for failure shape the entrepreneur’s
incentives to exit the startup, we therefore should observe
relatively more low acquisitions and sales of the startup
in the intermediate ﬁnancing stages (which is yet to be
empirically tested).
4.2. Other applications
R&D ﬁnancing . Our model also has implications for
optimal R & D ﬁnancing within more general types of ﬁrms
as well as for the design of R & D projects and compensa-
tion contracts of R & D workers (insiders) or the CEO of a
company who controls on R&D expenditures/projects.
In particular, optimal ﬁnancing of R & D projects involves
several stages, whereby insiders make occasional progress
report and the continuation of ﬁnancing is contingent
on the outcomes of these reports. Crucially, it is optimal
for the ﬁnancier to elicit less frequent progress reports
at the early stages of project development and more
frequent progress reports at later stages. Over time, the
provision and continuation of ﬁnancing should become
more sensitive to reported progress and performance.
The compensation of R & D workers should involve
high rewards for success, penalties for delays in project
completion, and tolerance for failure via both i) pay for
failure and ii) a grace period with relatively low likelihood
of termination. Loosely speaking, R & D workers should
have an initial probationary (grace) period that corre-
sponds to the unconditional ﬁnancing stage in the contract
and their incentives should be backloaded. Interestingly,
Baranchuk et al. (2014) document that tolerance for failure
combined with backloaded incentives incentivizes CEOs
to pursue innovation and to increase R & D spending. Our
model suggests the reverse relation too: tolerance for
failure combined with backloaded compensation not only
induces innovation, but also is necessary to incentivize
disclosure of bad outcomes in ﬁnancing innovation.
Importantly, R & D workers should be rewarded and
paid for failure at later stages, but not for failure at early
stages. Note that backloaded incentives combined with
rewards for failure in the later stages are optimal to jointly
optimize the agent’s incentives for project development
and disclosure of failure. This mechanism leading to back-
loaded incentives/compensation is different to the one
in Sannikov (2014) or Marinovic and Varas (2019) where
backloaded compensation is necessary to provide incen-
tives for actions with persistent (i.e., long-run) impact on
ﬁrm performance.
Related to our paper, Manso (2011) ﬁnds that to incen-
tivize experimentation (which is crucial for R & D), it may
be optimal to reward failure. Note that the mechanism
leading to rewards for failure in our model is different
from the one in Manso (2011) , as rewards for failure are
needed to incentivize disclosure for failure. In addition,
considering a dynamic framework, we obtain the novel
ﬁndings that i) it is optimal to only reward failure that
occurs at a later stage and ii) if there are rewards for
failure they should decrease over time to incentivize the
agent not to delay disclosure of failure.
Executive compensation contracts . Our model also has
implications for CEO compensation in a more broad set-
ting. One can interpret the agent as the manager (i.e.,
CEO) of a ﬁrm while the principal represents the ﬁrm’s
investors or outside shareholders. Golden parachutes,

Importantly, our model highlights that while instru-

severance pay incentivize disclosure of failure, they also
provide incentives to fake or generate bad outcomes on

in incentive provision similar to the one in Inderst and

incentivize a “bad” CEO to quit but at the same time
severance pay undermines incentives for effort. The novel
feature in our model is that because the contract does not
always incentivize disclosure and consists of an uncondi-
tional ﬁnancing stage followed by a disclosure stage, our
results imply golden parachutes (golden handshakes) and
severance pay should not be in place in the early stages
of the CEO’s contract (i.e., CEO tenure) but only later on.
Once in place, the dollar size of these instruments should
decrease over time and the CEO should be provided strong
incentives through performance pay.

that generate results quickly (that is, t 1 /T decreases with
1 / /Lambda1). As T is hump-shaped in 1 / /Lambda1, projects generating
results quickly also have a short ﬁnancing horizon. Put
differently, the optimal contract for projects that generate
results quickly stipulates a relatively long unconditional
ﬁnancing stage but a short hard deadline. As a result, these
types of projects are particularly suitable for a “spray and
pray” investment approach in which VCs allocate funding
to (many) startups over a (relatively) short horizon with
limited governance and oversight so that the provision
of ﬁnancing is initially not very performance-sensitive
( Ewens et al., 2018 ).
The ﬁnancing deadline T decreases in φ, while the
relative length of the unconditional ﬁnancing stage t 1 /T
increases in φ. The absolute length of the unconditional
ﬁnancing stage t 1 is hump-shaped in φ. That is, unless φ
is suﬃciently large, t 1 increases in φ. 22 As a result, optimal
ﬁnancing contracts for projects that are subject to severe
agency conﬂicts involve a relatively long unconditional
ﬁnancing stage (and soft deadline) that is followed by
a relatively short disclosure stage (and hard deadline).
This ﬁnding also implies that the provision of ﬁnancing
is less performance-sensitive, when agency conﬂicts are
more severe. The intuition is that because the provision
of unconditional (not performance-sensitive) ﬁnancing
limits agency rents, unconditional ﬁnancing is especially
valuable when agency conﬂicts are severe and φ is high.
In other words, our model generates the novel and at ﬁrst
glance counter-intuitive result that because self-reported
progress is subject to moral hazard, it is optimal to provide
substantial unconditional ﬁnancing that is independent of
performance or reported progress, precisely when agency
conﬂicts in project development are severe (e.g., due to
high intangibility or complexity of the project).

5. Optimal contracts with monitoring and inspections
In this section, we introduce the possibility that the
principal can inspect (i.e., monitor) project progress at a
cost K > 0 . Upon an inspection at time t, the principal
learns without error whether the project has failed so
far. We consider inspections that occur at deterministic
times, and that inspections and their outcomes are pub-
licly observable and contractible subject to the following
limited commitment constraint: the principal cannot
commit to inspections that yield at the time of inspection
an expected post-inspection continuation payoff (strictly)
lower than the inspection costs K. 23 The intuition behind
this limited commitment constraint is that the principal
cannot commit to inspections that yield her a negative
continuation payoff (i.e., the principal’s continuation payoff
must be positive at all times). The limited commitment
constraint precludes that the principal inspects the project
after failure is reported. In optimum, the agent never fakes
failure and any reported failure is true, so an inspection
after reported failure would indeed reveal failure, trigger
termination, and yield zero post-inspection payoff for the
principal. In what follows, we present the heuristic solu-
tion with inspections and all solution details are deferred
to Appendix B.5 .
We study how (deterministic) inspections generate
incentives to disclose failure truthfully, i.e., truth telling
incentives. For this sake, let us consider that the contract
incentivizes disclosure of failure so that w t = βt . When
the principal inspects the project and learns that the agent
is hiding failure, she can punish the agent. The threat of
punishment generates truth telling incentives. Because of

horizon. Our results also deliver an explanation for the
positive relationship between VC monitoring and startup
success ( Bernstein et al., 2016 ). VCs who regularly inspect
and monitor the startup (e.g., because K is suﬃciently
small) are less likely to terminate ﬁnancing prematurely,
thereby increasing the likelihood of eventual success.
Removing the threat of termination also increases
the agent’s continuation payoff (i.e., the agent’s stake)
W t , thereby incentivizing the agent not to fake failure and
boosting incentives for project development. The additional
incentives for project development brought about by in-
spections reduce the need for excessively high rewards for
success to provide incentives for disclosure, which lowers
the cost of incentivizing disclosure of failure. As a conse-
quence, the optimal contract with monitoring is a full dis-

satisfy K ∈ ( K , F 0 ] , the optimal contract does not always
incentivize disclosure of failure so that w c < w 0 . Then,
the optimal contract consists indeed of several uncondi-

principal inspects the project at the end of a disclosure
stage and elicits a truthful progress report at the end
of an unconditional ﬁnancing stage. Finally, when the
monitoring costs are so large that monitoring is no more
optimal (i.e., when K > F 0 ), then the optimal contract is
described in Proposition 3 and does not always incentivize
disclosure of failure. Our results imply that venture cap-
italists with more expertise and/or a better monitoring
skills (as captured by lower costs of monitoring K) elicit
more frequent progress reports, tend to incentivize disclo-
sure of failure more, and so provide ﬁnancing in a more
performance-sensitive way.

6. Further results and robustness
6.1. Moral hazard versus adverse selection
In our model, moral hazard arises because effort is
hidden and costly. The severity of moral hazard is cap-
tured by the agent’s private beneﬁts from shirking φ. In
addition, failure is hard for the principal to observe or ver-
ify. Imperfectly observable failure induces another agency
problem that can be interpreted as an adverse selection
problem and its severity is captured by 1 − π. The reason
is that the principal provides truth telling incentives to the
agent under the assumption that the agent (already) has
privately observed project failure.
Proposition 3 shows that t 1 → 0 as π → 0 and t 1 → T
as π → 1 and Fig. 3 shows that t 1 /T increases in φ. That
is, the provision of unconditional ﬁnancing is valuable
when moral hazard is severe (i.e., when φ is large) but
adverse selection concerns are mild (i.e., when 1 − π
is low). In other words, the provision of unconditional
ﬁnancing is suitable for dealing with moral hazard but less
suitable for dealing with adverse selection. As a result, the
provision of ﬁnancing is more (less) performance sensitive
when adverse selection (moral hazard) is more severe.
The lower three panels of Fig. 4 plot the (scaled) prob-
abilities of over- and under-investment and the average
length of the ﬁnancing period against φ, capturing the
severity of moral hazard, while Fig. 5 plots the same quan-
tities against 1 − π, capturing the severity of adverse selec-
tion. Figs. 4 and 5 demonstrate that mild adverse selection
concerns (moral hazard problems) induce over-provision
of ﬁnancing (i.e., over-investment) whereas severe ad-
verse selection concerns (moral hazard problems) induce
under-provision of ﬁnancing (i.e., under-investment).
6.2. Robustness
Our model entails a number of assumptions that are
mainly designed to enhance simplicity and to facilitate
a clear analysis of the main forces in a tractable model.
Below, we discuss these assumptions and the robustness
of the results.
Discounting . Appendix C.2 provides the model solution
when principal and agent discount at r > 0 . We show
that discounting does not have any bearings on our key
ﬁndings: with discounting, the contract features two
stages —a n unconditional ﬁnancing stage followed by a
disclosure stage —a n d , during the disclosure stage, the
agent’s rewards for failure decrease over time.
Modeling of the agent’s effort . Existing dynamic con-
tracting papers —s u c h as Mason and Välimäki (2015) ;
Green and Taylor (2016) , and Varas (2017) —s t u d y how
to motivate the agent to complete a project. In these
papers, completion always corresponds to “success” and
the only bad outcomes that can arise are completion
delays. Notably, the agent is never tempted to hide project
completion/success, but —i n Green and Taylor (2016) and
Varas (2017) —t h e agent is tempted to fake success (i.e.,
good outcomes). The innovation in our paper is that it
considers the risk of project failure when the agent can
hide or fake project failure (i.e., bad outcomes).
We model the agent’s effort in a different way than
Green and Taylor (2016) and Varas (2017) . However,
regardless of the way of modeling the agent’s effort,
the project completion timing arrives under incentive
compatible contracts according to a Poisson process with
constant intensity both in our paper and in Green and
Taylor (2016) and Varas (2017) . 25 Appendix C.3 shows
that we obtain the same results employing an alternative
framework, in which the agent controls project completion
as in Green and Taylor (2016) and Varas (2017) while the
project is subject to failure risk.
Unobservable success . Throughout this paper, we have
assumed that success is perfectly observable and con-
tractible. This assumption intuitively reﬂects that the
agent is tempted to conceal bad outcomes rather than
good outcomes. Even though not modeled explicitly, dis-
closure of good outcomes could yield private beneﬁts to
the agent, e.g., related to the agent’s reputation or career
concerns, whereas disclosure of bad outcomes could yield
private dis-utility.
We show that our ﬁndings do not change substantially
when success is imperfectly observable to the principal
and the agent cannot fake success. That is, the agent can
hide success (i.e., delay disclosure of success and report

7. Conclusion
We study a dynamic contracting model in which a
principal hires an agent to develop an innovative project.
Crucially, project failure is hard for the principal to observe
or verify and the agent can hide or fake failure, leading to
a tension in incentive provision. The optimal contract con-
sists of two distinct stages: i) an unconditional ﬁnancing
stage and ii) a disclosure stage. During the unconditional
ﬁnancing stage, ﬁnancing is guaranteed and the contract
does not incentivize disclosure of failure. During the
disclosure stage, the contract incentivizes disclosure of
failure and the principal ﬁnances the project until either
a deadline is reached or the project is completed. Then,
the agent receives time decreasing rewards for success
and failure and incurs harsh punishments for delays. In
the optimal contract, the provision of ﬁnancing becomes
more performance sensitive following completion delays,
i.e., following poor performance.
The paper has several empirical implications for ven-
ture capital ﬁnancing. First, as VCs tend to incentivize
disclosure of failure less in the early ﬁnancing stages,
the failure rate of VC-ﬁnanced startups is relatively low
in the early ﬁnancing stages and years. Second, because
the entrepreneur’s incentives become stronger over time,
the marginal likelihood of startup success increases over
time and across stages. Third, the entrepreneur should
be reward for failure — i.e., any type of exit that yields

Fourth, in the later ﬁnancing stages, these rewards for

(e.g., innovative projects), the VC tends to incentivize
disclosure of failure less and so is more likely to continue
ﬁnancing the startup when it fails or underperforms.
Finally, the model also helps explain why venture capital

renewable energy), and predicts that projects, generating
results relatively quickly, are more suitable for ﬁnancing
via a spray-and-pray investment approach.
