3. Numerical policy functions
To examine the implications of the estimated parameters for firms’ optimal policies, we
compute the numerical policy functions {I/K,D/K, ∆P } = h(K,D, Φ,Z ) for investment
rate I/K , net leverage D/K, and product introductions ∆P . In the discussion that follows,
we focus on two sets of policy functions. First, we fix K andD at their average values in the
simulated sample and set Z = 1 as we want to focus on the economic forces driven by product
portfolio setting. Panel A of Fig. B.1 plots the policy functions {I/K,D/K, ∆P } = ˜h(Po|Pi
n)
for a firm with a low and high number of new products, i.e. i ∈ {l,h }. Second, in Panel B of
Fig. B.1 we fix K andD at their average values in the simulated sample and plot the policy
functions for the profitability shocks Z: {I/K,D/K, ∆P } = ˜h(Z|Φi), while varying Φ from
a low to a high value, i.e. i ∈ {l,h }.
The numerical policy functions in Panel A of Fig. B.1 show how firms optimally respond to
changing the product portfolio structure. In particular, the left graph in Panel A shows that
the policy function for product introductions can be characterized by an inaction region due
to a fixed cost of product introduction. That is, firms only tend to introduce new products
once the current stock of old products is suﬀiciently large, which means that they introduce
products at the highest rate when their product portfolios are older. The policy function
for product introductions has natural implications for investment and financing policy. The
middle graph in Panel A illustrates that investment tends to decrease as the share of old
products increases, consistent with the investment Euler equation.
The right graph in Panel A documents the precautionary savings motive induced by product
dynamics, as it indicates that firms’ financing policy depends on product portfolio structure.
Firms adopt higher leverage when the share of old products is low and then delever as product
portfolio age increases. This happens for two reasons. First, because firms have stronger
precautionary savings incentives and thus value preserving debt capacity more, because they

The right graph in Panel B illustrates that the effect of the profitability shock Z on leverage
also depends on product portfolio structure. When hit by a low shock realization, firms tend
to introduce less new products, invest less, and use the remaining proceeds to pay down debt,
resulting in lower leverage. Similarly, when the realization of the profitability shock is high,
firms use debt to fund investment and product introductions, which increases their leverage
ratios. These effects are magnified by product portfolio structure, as firms with lower product
portfolio age have higher leverage ratios, given that they have less incentives to preserve debt
capacity.
To conclude the discussion of the policy functions, we investigate whether the dynamics
induced by the product-level economic forces are economically important. To this end, we
perform a variance decomposition of investment and leverage. The results highlight that
product dynamics account for roughly 20–22% of the total investment and leverage variance. 2

Internet Appendix C. Structural estimation
In this section we provide additional results concerning the structural estimation of our model.
Subsection 1 presents the estimation diagnostics of Andrews, Gentzkow, and Shapiro (2017).
Subsection 2 provides the estimation results of the baseline model using an alternative prod-
uct portfolio age measure. Subsection 3 provides new evidence concerning the importance of
distinguishing between cash and negative debt in our sample of firms. Subsection 4 provides
the data-implied transition probabilities. Subsection 5 reports the model- and data-implied
moments and parameter estimates for all sample splits.
1. Estimation diagnostics
We compute the diagnostic measure of Andrews et al. (2017) to investigate whether the
model parameters are locally identified by the underlying moments. The key benefit of the
measure is that a reported high sensitivity means not only that the moment is sensitive to
the underlying parameter, but also that the parameter is precisely estimated. The results
are presented in Table C.1, in which each column corresponds to a structural parameter and
each row to a moment. The sensitivities in the table are trimmed at 0.4 in absolute value to
ease the presentation of key relationships, similar to Michaels, Page, and Whited (2018).
[Table C.1 about here.]
The results confirm the intuition behind the identification of the structural parameters. For
example, the standard deviation and persistence of the profit shock are sensitive to variance
and serial correlation of profitability, the depreciation rate δ is closely linked to the mean of
investment and the collateral constraint parameter ω is positively related to the mean of net
leverage. Importantly, the product-related moments are sensitive to product characteristics ξ
andη. It should be noted, however, that the elasticities are only local and, moreover, highly

sensitive to the numerical properties of the gradient. Because of that it might appear that
some moments are not informative about the underlying parameter while in reality they do
provide substantial identifying information. One example of that is the effect of the product

age: while the Andrews et al. (2017) sensitivities are smaller than 0.4 in absolute value (0.28
and -0.21, respectively), over a wider range of the parameter values they are substantial.
It should also be noted that the sign and magnitudes of the elasticities for product-related
moments are different, in line with the intuition outlined in Section 3 of the paper.

3. Distinguishing between cash and debt
An important assumption in the model concerns the fact that cash is treated as negative
debt, and thus the firm decides on the stock of net debt rather than cash and debt separately.
This assumption might be imperfect, as the distinction between cash and debt could play
an important role, e.g. for small and innovative firms (e.g. Gamba and Triantis , 2008).
To better understand whether the assumption affects our results, we examine the following
subsamples of our data: we first split firms into two samples based on their cash holdings
and then split each into two further subsamples based on leverage. We call the subsample
with below-median cash and below-median leverage as the ‘low’ distance subsample and
the subsample with above-median cash and above-median leverage as the ‘high’ distance
subsample. We then estimate our model on each subsample. The resulting estimates are
presented in Table C.3.
[Table C.3 about here.]
The results provide several insights. First, the two subsamples have similar moments related

to financing, despite using very different mix of debt and cash, as their mean net leverage
ratios are close. Second, the resulting structural estimates do not recover any major difference
between the subsamples. In particular, the collateral constraint paramter ω is virtually the
same and all the other parameters are statistically indistinguishable. This is true despite
the fact that the two subsamples have quite distinctive product portfolio characteristics, as
product portfolio age is higher in the subsample with higher distance between debt and cash.
At the same time, the result is not surprising given our sample consists primarily of large,
profitable and old companies, at least when compared to an an average Compustat firm, for
which the distinguishing between debt and cash should be less important.
