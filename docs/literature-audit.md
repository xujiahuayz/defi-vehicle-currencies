## Completion contract

Each paper is classified as load-bearing, contextual, venue exemplar, or more than one. A complete card records five distinct reading axes, not a generic prose summary:

- exact title, authors, year, publication/version and saved source;
- project roles and every bibliography key or manuscript claim that uses it;
- `Scientific`: question, contribution, theory or mechanism, data, measurement, identification, main estimates, boundary conditions and credible alternative readings;
- `Structure`: top-level and subsection architecture and the function of each major block;
- `Depth`: where the paper spends analytical, institutional and robustness effort, and what it treats lightly;
- `Breadth`: scope, literatures, mechanisms, objections, alternatives and boundary discipline;
- `Optics`: title, opening, claim calibration, tables, figures, captions, notation, prose density, page allocation and reviewer-facing credibility;
- exact page, section, table or equation supporting every project use;
- first reader, completion status and independent-reader status.

Allowed statuses are `discovered`, `abstract-screened`, `full-text-read`, `claim-verified` and `independently-re-read`. Keyword retrieval, text extraction and corpus-level counts never advance a paper beyond `abstract-screened` on their own.

### MilionisMoallemiRoughgardenZhang2022LVR
- Status: claim-verified
- Roles: load-bearing, mechanism, measurement
- Source: literature/papers/2022-MilionisMoallemiRoughgardenZhang2022LVR-preprint-automated-market-making-and-loss-versus-rebalancing.pdf
- Version: arXiv v5, 27 May 2024, 63 pages
- Uses: Supplies the loss-versus-rebalancing definition and the constant-product closed form used in Section 5, equations `eq:lvr` and `eq:lvrrate`, and in the rent-incidence finding; does not support the Myersonian liquidity-provision claims, which belong to a different paper
- Scientific: Builds a continuous-time risk-neutral model with an infinitely deep external market, a locally smooth AMM demand curve, passive LP capital, frictionless arbitrage and exogenous noise-trader fees. The rebalancing portfolio holds the AMM's risky-asset inventory but trades at the external price. Theorem 1 shows that the AMM's predictable loss relative to that portfolio is one half of instantaneous price variance times marginal liquidity and equals rebalancing-arbitrage profit. For the equal-weight constant-product invariant, instantaneous LVR divided by pool value is variance divided by eight. The one-pair empirical exercise uses the Uniswap v2 WETH-USDC pool from 1 August 2021 to 31 July 2022, Binance minute prices, and Dune pool events; it validates the accounting approximation but is not causal identification. Its sharp boundaries are the external-price benchmark, continuous trading, no arbitrage fees or gas in the baseline, passive liquidity, and a single large pool.
- Structure: Sections 1 to 3 move from the Black-Scholes analogy and contribution preview through institutional background to the model. Section 4 derives the generic LVR theorem and mechanism. Section 5 specializes it to Balancer, Uniswap v2, Uniswap v3 ranges and a limit-order boundary case. Section 6 validates the model empirically. Sections 7 and 8 connect the object to option replication, variance swaps and impermanent loss. Section 9 discusses oracle, auction and batch-auction design responses; Section 10 concludes. Appendices prove the theorem, derive curvature, give a binomial intuition and multidimensional extension, and disclose the data construction and SQL.
- Depth: Roughly half the main text is devoted to the mechanism, theorem, intuition and AMM examples before the eight-page empirical validation. The appendices spend twelve pages on proofs and extensions and four on data construction. The paper treats equilibrium LP supply, cross-pool competition, fees, gas, block discreteness, strategic liquidity adjustment and multi-venue routing lightly or as follow-on work.
- Breadth: Connects AMM market making to stale-quote sniping, adverse selection, Black-Scholes replication, delta hedging, European options, variance swaps, impermanent loss and mechanism design. It covers generic smooth AMMs plus weighted-product, constant-product and concentrated-liquidity examples, while explicitly declining claims about equilibrium liquidity levels and the response of supplied capital to market design.
- Optics: The title names one new object; the abstract and first two pages state the analogy, mechanism, formula class, empirical validation and design implication. The paper spends fourteen pages earning the theorem before naming the constant-product shortcut, then uses seven figures and one compact results table to make the abstraction concrete. Captions are unusually explanatory, equations carry the argument, and limitations are placed before the empirical section rather than hidden in robustness. The 63-page preprint is theory-led and denser than an empirical JFE template, so its architecture is a mechanism model benchmark, not a direct section-length target.
- Locations: The rebalancing definition is in Section 4, pages 17 to 19, equations (5) and (6); Theorem 1 and the generic LVR rate are pages 19 to 20, equations (7) and (8); the constant-product result is Example 3, pages 25 to 26, equation (16); the empirical integrated form is page 30, equation (23); model limitations are Section 3.1, pages 13 to 15; the validation sample and results are pages 30 to 36, Figures 3 to 6 and Table 1; the exact empirical construction uses Binance prices at Appendix C, pages 60 to 62, especially equation (45).
- Implication: Keep the corrected attribution and variance-over-eight formula, but do not imply that the citation validates variance measured from each pool's own marginal price. The cited estimand uses the external market price and the paper implements it with Binance data. External-price reconstruction or a transparent bound is therefore required before rent incidence freezes. Preserve the source's separation of fees, LVR and market risk, and its explicit limits; decline its single-pool empirical breadth as evidence for cross-pool or vehicle-rotation claims.
- First reader: glotl, full-text pass completed 2026-08-07
- Independent: not required; this source is load-bearing for one measurement formula but is not classified as a central paper-level framing source

### MakarovSchoar2020Arbitrage
- Status: claim-verified
- Roles: load-bearing, rival, empirical-design, contextual, venue exemplar
- Source: literature/papers/2020-MakarovSchoar2020Arbitrage-trading-and-arbitrage-in-cryptocurrency-markets.pdf
- Version: Published Journal of Financial Economics article, volume 135, 2020, pages 293 to 319; accepted 12 January 2019 and available online 11 July 2019
- Uses: Supports the introduction's cross-exchange segmentation claim, the Section 6 choice to give a contested measured object its own defence, the Korea-Japan-Europe magnitude and friction contrast, and the choice to report economic magnitudes in dollars as well as incidence. It does not support classifying DEX round trips, claiming atomic flash-loan capture, describing arbitrage and uninformed volume shares at an AMM pool, or equating route notional times a hypothetical gap with its executable arbitrage-profit object.
- Scientific: Uses tick data for 34 centralized exchanges in 19 countries from January 2017 through February 2018, with a 15-exchange Kaiko core, to document recurring cross-exchange bitcoin price deviations, their geographic segmentation, co-movement with buying pressure, relation to capital controls, cross-cryptocurrency falsification and order-flow price pressure. The arbitrage index is the minute-level maximum volume-weighted price divided by the minimum and then averaged daily. Potential profit is constructed at second frequency from the minimum of executable sell-initiated low-price volume and buy-initiated high-price volume, normally only above a 2% spread, with a capped more aggressive rule above 10%. Korea-US price differences averaged above 15% from December 2017 to early February 2018 and reached 40% on several days; Japan averaged about 10%, Europe about 3%, and the authors estimate at least $2 billion total potential profit over December through February. Pairwise capital-control exposure predicts spread-correlation with slope 0.29, t-statistic 3 and R-squared 0.07; the country premium-beta relation has slope 0.3, t-statistic 4.6 and adjusted R-squared 0.56. Crypto-to-crypto deviations on the same exchanges are an order of magnitude smaller than crypto-to-fiat deviations, strengthening the segmentation account. Factor and canonical-correlation decompositions show common signed volume strongly explains common returns, while exchange-specific signed volume and lagged price deviations explain slow local convergence. The paper is descriptive and mechanism-discriminating, not a causal estimate of capital controls; exchange access, cleaning, possible wash trading, short sample length and endogenous exchange choice remain boundaries.
- Structure: Section 1 spends four pages stating five numbered findings, magnitudes, mechanism conjecture, order-flow contribution, literature and roadmap. Section 2 gives exchange/data construction and cleaning; Section 3 establishes volume and return facts. Section 4 builds the arbitrage index from within-region to cross-region prices and executable-volume profits. Section 5 moves from spread correlation to buying pressure and capital controls; Section 6 uses fiat-versus-crypto and crypto-versus-crypto comparisons as a mechanism check. Section 7 develops the factor and VAR order-flow model. Section 8 is a standalone defence of the measured arbitrage object through strategy mechanics and constraints. Section 9 concludes. A two-page appendix records exchange rules, fees, transfer delays and the full cross-country correlation matrix.
- Depth: The article spends about five pages on data and baseline market facts, four on arbitrage measurement and magnitudes, four on cross-country correlation and capital controls, three on the crypto-crypto falsification, five on order-flow modelling, and two and a half on executability and constraints. Ten numbered tables including two appendix tables, eleven figures and eight equations carry most evidence. Its deepest work is measurement triangulation and economic feasibility, while formal identification of capital controls, trader identity, institutional change over time and welfare are treated lightly or explicitly left unresolved.
- Breadth: Connects international market segmentation, limits to arbitrage, capital controls, investor demand, convenience yield, market microstructure, price discovery, order flow, governance risk and crypto institutional detail. It covers within-country, cross-country, crypto-fiat and crypto-crypto comparisons at second, minute, five-minute, hourly and daily frequencies. It does not cover decentralized exchanges, automated market makers, path routing, flash loans, same-block atomic execution, liquidity-provider returns or vehicle currencies.
- Optics: The broad descriptive title matches the paper's two contribution blocks. The abstract is a single compact paragraph, while the first three introduction pages preview the findings as an ordered sequence with concrete percentages and dollars before offering the mechanism. Figures 2 to 5 establish the phenomenon visually before regression tables appear; Section 8 then answers the obvious referee objection that the gaps should be arbitraged away. Captions define samples and construction in full. Equations are confined to the order-flow section and do not interrupt the descriptive opening. At 27 journal pages, the paper earns breadth through a clear results ladder and a late standalone measurement defence, not through a generic robustness section.
- Locations: Published pages 293 to 296 contain the abstract, ordered contribution preview, 15%/40%/10%/3% differences and $2 billion magnitude; pages 296 to 298 and Table 1 document the data; pages 301 to 305, Figures 2 to 5 and Sections 4.1 to 4.4 define and measure the arbitrage index and volume-matched profit; pages 305 to 308, Figures 6 to 9 and Tables 4 to 5 report buying-pressure and capital-control evidence; pages 308 to 311 and Figures 10 to 11 give the crypto-crypto comparison; pages 311 to 315, equations (1) to (8) and Tables 6 to 10 give the order-flow analysis; pages 315 to 317, Section 8 and Appendix Table A1 state latency, short-sale, fee, governance and capital-control constraints.
- Implication: Preserve the paper's sequence of phenomenon, economic magnitude, mechanism discrimination and explicit executability defence. Keep the verified Section 6 comparison. Remove this citation from DEX round-trip and same-block flash-loan claims, and do not use it as evidence about AMM pool-flow composition. Label the project's route-size-times-improvement quantity as counterfactual execution improvement or savings, subject to exact-size quote support, not as Makarov-Schoar potential arbitrage profit. Match its optics by placing the key continuous magnitude and denominator early, then giving the constructed quote object a dedicated quantitative defence.
- First reader: glotl, full published-text pass completed 2026-08-07
- Independent: not required at this stage; the paper is load-bearing for a rival and measurement benchmark but is not the project's central vehicle-dominance theory source

### ErenMalamud2022DominantDebt
- Status: claim-verified
- Roles: central, theory, rival, contextual, venue exemplar
- Source: literature/papers/2022-ErenMalamud2022DominantDebt-dominant-currency-debt.pdf
- Version: Published Journal of Financial Economics article, volume 144, 2022, pages 571 to 589; accepted 9 May 2021 and available online 18 July 2021
- Uses: Supports the claim that dominance is role-specific and that debt-currency shares can change with forward-looking expectations at quarterly frequency; supplies a non-network rival mechanism for abrupt changes in a dominant currency's debt role; provides a JFE theory-plus-empirics architecture benchmark. It does not support the statement that debt denomination is derived from the same network or invoicing forces as Gopinath-Stein, daily pair-level FX turnover, renminbi competition, or a priced route-choice counterfactual.
- Scientific: Develops a borrower-driven capital-structure model for why large global firms choose one major currency for debt. With comparable issuance costs, the chosen currency is the one whose depreciation best hedges firm distress over the debt-maturity horizon, equivalently the currency with the largest relevant covariance with the stock market under the model's transformation; the result is independent of lenders' stochastic discount factor. A dynamic extension adds a leverage-ratchet result and predicts choice of the currency with the steepest nominal yield curve. The evidence is triangulated across two-year quanto-implied EUR/USD-S&P 500 covariance, two- and five-year inflation risk premia, quarterly dollar debt shares, historical dollar-stock covariances over three to 120 months, a 45-observation annual VAR, 706,924 bond issues by 61,910 firms in 121 countries, yield-curve slopes, and a preliminary post-COVID issuance comparison. A one-standard-deviation fall in the quanto risk premium is associated with roughly three percentage points more dollar issuance, about $38 billion at the sample mean; the maturity regressions imply one-standard-deviation longer maturity raises dollar issuance by 1.5 percentage points for non-banks and 3 points for banks in the broad samples. These are theory-consistent predictive relationships, not causal estimates: the quanto regression has 24 quarters, the IRP and yield-curve regressions 84, the annual VAR 45, historical long-horizon returns overlap, and the COVID post dummy is especially vulnerable to coincident shocks.
- Structure: A four-page introduction opens with the $12 trillion fact, the dollar's fall and post-crisis rise, three challenges to the safe-asset account, the debt-view mechanism, a complete result preview, and a role-by-role literature map. Section 2 gives the static capital-structure model in about two pages. Section 3 uses forward-looking risk premia and quarterly issuance in three pages; Section 4 uses historical covariances and a VAR in three. Section 5 moves to bond-level maturity choice in two pages. Section 6 addresses the static-model objection with a dynamic extension and yield-curve evidence in two pages. Section 7 treats COVID as an additional setting in two pages, and Section 8 concludes in one page. Data, construction and limitations are introduced beside the experiment that needs them instead of in one generic data or robustness section.
- Depth: The paper states two static theorems, three empirical propositions, a leverage-ratchet proposition and a dynamic theorem, then subjects their distinct predictions to six empirical exhibits. Its strongest micro design compares bonds issued by the same firm in at least two currencies in the same month and then restricts to firms from five deep-market jurisdictions; the non-bank firm-month estimate is imprecise until that jurisdiction restriction, while the bank estimate survives. The paper openly notes short quanto maturity, overlapping-return inference, small non-overlapping samples, sensitivity of the annual VAR to the starting year, incomplete firm hedging, comparable-market scope and the partial-equilibrium abstraction. It treats issuance-cost endogeneity, a causal monetary-policy design, debt/invoicing/banking feedback, equilibrium currency demand and network externalities as controls, contrasts or future work rather than results.
- Breadth: Connects dominant-currency roles in debt, trade invoicing, global banking, reserves and safe assets with capital structure, borrower hedging, default, inflation policy, term premia, convenience yield, corporate basis and leverage dynamics. The breadth stays disciplined around one question, why dollar debt dominates among otherwise comparable major currencies. The model is intended for large global firms and market-based or bank debt in deep currencies; it expressly does not explain emerging-market local-versus-dollar borrowing without material issuance-cost heterogeneity and does not model vehicle-currency routing.
- Optics: The two-word title names the exact economic object. Figure 1 makes the fall-and-rise phenomenon visible on the second journal page; the introduction then creates tension by showing why the conventional safe-currency account gets the risk and rate ordering wrong. The counterintuitive answer, firms choose the riskier currency because it hedges borrowers over their maturity horizon, is stated before formalism and recurs as the organizing mechanism. Six tables and seven figures each answer a named step, with full sample and construction notes. At nineteen journal pages the paper is compact because theory and empirical evidence alternate, not because content is thin. Its four-page introduction is longer than an empirical-only target but earns that space by previewing the full mechanism and by distinguishing the debt view from trade, safe-asset and vehicle-currency views.
- Locations: The question, conventional-view failures, borrower mechanism, result preview and literature distinctions are on journal pages 571 to 575, especially pages 572 to 574. Theorem 2.1 and the covariance condition are pages 575 to 577, equations (6) to (8); Theorem 2.2 and inflation-risk-premium link are page 577, equation (9). Forward-looking evidence, Propositions 2 and 3, and the quarterly share regressions are pages 577 to 581, Figures 2 to 3 and Tables 1 to 2. Historical horizons, overlap correction, the covariance decomposition and VAR are pages 581 to 583, equations (12) to (13), Figures 4 to 5 and Table 3. Bond-level maturity design and Table 4 are pages 583 to 585. Proposition 4, Theorem 6.1 and yield-curve evidence are pages 585 to 587, equation (14), Figure 6 and Table 5. The COVID comparison and its stated preliminary status are pages 586 to 588, Figures 7 and Table 6. The abrupt-beliefs claim and boundaries are in the conclusion on page 588.
- Implication: Correct the introduction: Eren and Malamud deliberately derive dominant debt denomination from borrower risk and yield-curve forces without network effects, pricing complementarity or safety demand, so “the same forces” is false. This source cannot substantiate daily FX turnover, renminbi competition or missing route quotes; those measures may still enter if supported elsewhere or introduced and validated here as novel. Use the paper as a sharp rival and framing precedent: dominance is a continuous role-specific share, its mechanism can differ across debt, invoicing and vehicle use, and expectations can move that share quickly even when network accounts predict slow change. For this paper's optics, open with the measured degree of vehicle dominance and the fact that needs explanation, state the surprising mechanism early, and make each later experiment answer one prediction or rival instead of collecting robustness by data source.
- First reader: glotl, full published-text pass completed 2026-08-07
- Independent: pending; this is a central source and requires a second full-text read before the literature gate closes

### venue:li-ye-zheng-refusing-best-price
- Status: full-text-read
- Roles: venue, empirical-design, candidate-rival
- Source: ../defi-dominant-currency/lit/jfe-exemplars/li-ye-zheng-refusing-best-price.pdf
- Version: Published Journal of Financial Economics article, volume 147, 2023, pages 317 to 337
- Uses: Venue benchmark for a counterintuitive title, proprietary-data defence, magnitude-first opening, order-type taxonomy, mechanism triangulation, fee-shock design, clientele heterogeneity, market-design evolution and policy boundaries. It is also a candidate scientific precedent for treating routing maturation as a rival to vehicle persistence, but it is not yet cited and does not itself establish a DEX-aggregator effect.
- Scientific: Uses proprietary NYSE message-level order data for 109 stratified common stocks from January 2010 through March 2011. Unlike standard trade and order-book data, the source records failed submissions, reserve-order instructions and orders routed away from the NYSE, allowing the authors to classify whether an order accepts or refuses inter-exchange routing. Non-routable orders account for 57% of continuous volume. Sixty-two percent of Reg NMS routing reaches the same gross price and a worse net price after fees; Rule 610 causes 69% of routings and about 90% of those routings deliver no gross improvement. Two maker-rebate increases raise DNS market share by 2.28 and 1.22 percentage points overall, with difference-in-differences effects 2.62 and 2.55 percentage points larger for narrow-spread than wide-spread stocks. Speed-race wins, quote aggressiveness and non-fill opportunity costs support a clientele account: liquidity-providing HFTs avoid routing fees, while traders facing larger directional opportunity costs accept routing to secure execution. The fee changes and spread interaction identify a response of order-type use more credibly than the clientele identity, which remains inferred because trader IDs are unavailable. The paper studies one exchange's order intake over fourteen months, so later order-type evolution and policy claims are mechanism-based extensions, not estimates from the main panel.
- Structure: The abstract and four-page opening establish the paradox, magnitudes, explanation and three contribution blocks. Section 2 defends the proprietary message data by naming the observation and variable truncations in public data. Section 3 builds a functional taxonomy of eight economically important order types before any causal test. Section 4 measures whether routing improves gross and net prices and isolates Rule 610 from Rule 611. Section 5 uses cross-sectional spread exposure, two fee changes and difference-in-differences. Section 6 triangulates clientele through queue races, stale-quote cancellation, quote aggressiveness and opportunity costs from one second to thirty days. Section 7 traces how later order types solve the frictions uncovered in the sample and then evaluates policy alternatives. A one-page conclusion restates the chain; the appendix moves the distinct latency mechanism for liquidity-taking orders out of the main narrative.
- Depth: The article spends three pages earning its data advantage and taxonomy, two pages decomposing the routing paradox, two on the fee mechanism, four on clientele discrimination and three on order-type evolution and policy. Eight main tables, three figures and two appendix tables turn the same mechanism through volumes, net prices, fee shocks, speed, quote placement, fill risk and product innovation. Robustness is argument-local: hidden versus displayed reserve orders isolate the regulatory channel; two fee shocks replicate the same interaction; opportunity costs use fourteen horizons; and alternative hypothetical fill assumptions bound the DNS comparison. The weakest links are HFT identity without trader IDs, event-window exposure to other contemporaneous changes and policy extrapolation beyond the sample.
- Breadth: Connects exchange fragmentation, smart routing, maker-taker fees, tick-size constraints, queue priority, hidden liquidity, high-frequency trading, adverse selection, order non-execution, product innovation and regulation while keeping one object fixed: why traders refuse an apparently best displayed price. It distinguishes gross quote, net execution, routing latency, fill probability and order-submitters' objectives instead of compressing them into one efficiency measure. The institutional detail is U.S. equities and Reg NMS; AMM routing, gas, multi-hop execution, inventory risk and DEX aggregators are outside its data.
- Optics: The title states the puzzle in five words and makes the reader ask why refusing the best price can be rational. The opening gives the 57% and 62% facts before institutional detail, then resolves the paradox with fees and heterogeneous non-fill costs. A taxonomy table makes a complex market legible before the mechanism tests. Each later section answers one question from the opening, and figures explain institutional logic instead of decorating estimates. Long table notes make the proprietary objects auditable. The paper is equation-light but analytically dense; its credibility comes from decomposing the measured object and confronting alternative motives, not from a large robustness appendix.
- Locations: Journal pages 317 to 321 state the puzzle, contributions, institutional setting and data advantage; pages 321 to 324 and Tables 2 to 3 give the routing destinations and order taxonomy; pages 324 to 326, Table 4 and Figure 2 separate gross from net improvement and Rule 610 from Rule 611; pages 326 to 327 and Table 5 report the fee-shock tests; pages 327 to 331 and Tables 6 to 8 identify the clientele mechanism and horizon-specific opportunity costs; pages 331 to 334 and Figure 3 trace order-type evolution and policy alternatives; pages 334 to 336 and Tables A.1 to A.2 conclude and isolate the latency mechanism.
- Implication: Add routing maturation to the F-G-H rival set, but do not encode it as mechanically increasing efficiency over calendar time. Separate route availability and connectivity from net execution after pool fees, gas, latency or state staleness, and from the user's value of immediate fill. The all-path cost surface can test whether feasible alternatives improve over time; it cannot by itself identify router choice or aggregator causality. Any aggregator-era test therefore needs validated router identity or a defensible architecture timing design. For presentation, use the paper's puzzle-first discipline: show the degree of vehicle dominance and the priced declined alternative early, define gross and net execution explicitly, give the route taxonomy before regressions, and let each mechanism test resolve one alternative explanation.
