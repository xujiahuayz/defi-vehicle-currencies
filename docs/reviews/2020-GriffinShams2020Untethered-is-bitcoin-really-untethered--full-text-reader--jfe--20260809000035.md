### Griffin and Shams (2020), “Is Bitcoin Really Untethered?”

- Status: Main article read sequentially in full, including exhibits, notes, conclusion, and references, across journal pp. 1913–1964 (PDF pp. 1–52); source-set read is incomplete because the stated Internet Appendix, replication code, and disclosure statement were not supplied or read.
- Roles: Load-bearing claim-verification source on stablecoin-mediated cryptocurrency price pressure; historical evidence on Tether’s vehicle-currency and cross-exchange settlement role; published empirical-method and research-communication exemplar.
- Source: John M. Griffin and Amin Shams, “Is Bitcoin Really Untethered?,” The Journal of Finance 75(4), August 2020, pp. 1913–1964, DOI 10.1111/jofi.12903; saved artifact `2020-GriffinShams2020Untethered-is-bitcoin-really-untethered.pdf`, 52 PDF pages.
- Version: Final published open-access article, not a working paper or accepted manuscript; initial submission July 6, 2018, accepted October 5, 2019, published August 2020 (p. 1962).
- Companions: Incomplete: p. 1964 identifies Appendix S1 (“Internet Appendix”), replication code, and a disclosure statement as online supporting information; none was included in the supplied artifact or read. The main text relies materially on Internet Appendix Sections I–II, Tables IA.I–IA.XIV, and Figures IA.1–IA.13 (pp. 1926, 1930–1934, 1938–1949, 1954–1961). No source-discovery record establishing the absence of corrections or corrigenda was supplied.
- Uses: Supports calibrated claims that Tether functioned as an important exchange vehicle during 2017, that concentrated blockchain flows from Bitfinex toward Poloniex and Bittrex followed Bitcoin declines and predicted short-horizon appreciation, and that these patterns are consistent with strategic price support. It may support only suggestive—not definitive—claims that Tether was unbacked or that observed trading constituted manipulation, because backing is not observed directly and the causal design depends on a contestable exclusion restriction.
- Scientific: The paper asks whether Tether was “pulled” by cash-backed investor demand and arbitrage or “pushed” into cryptocurrency markets to support prices (H1A–H1B and H2A–H2E, pp. 1923–1925). Its principal contribution is to combine full Bitcoin and Tether transaction histories with exchange prices and wallet-clustering methods to reconstruct cross-exchange capital flows. Data exceed 200 GB from more than 10 sources: prices span March 2016–March 2018, main hourly tests use March 1, 2017–March 31, 2018, Bitcoin transactions extend from January 2009, and Tether transactions from October 2014 (pp. 1925–1926). Measurement clusters Bitcoin wallets through the same-input heuristic, assigns exchange identities from public and investor-supplied addresses, allocates multi-recipient transactions proportionally, and combines opposite-direction Bitcoin and Tether flows using equations (1)–(3) (pp. 1930–1934). The central descriptive and predictive results are that 81% of Bitfinex-to-Poloniex/Bittrex Tether flows use one deposit address at each recipient exchange, 52% of returning Bitcoin reaches the 1LSg Bitfinex address (pp. 1934–1935), and a 100-Bitcoin increase in lagged flow predicts an 8.134-basis-point increase in average three-hour returns after authorization and negative returns (Table II, pp. 1937–1938). The 95 highest-flow hours are associated with 58.8% of Bitcoin’s buy-and-hold return and an average 64.5% of six altcoins’ returns; bootstrap replacement exercises show these outcomes are extreme relative to randomly selected hours (pp. 1941–1944, Figures 6–7). Mechanism discrimination uses timing, authorization conditioning, other-account and other-exchange placebos, matched prior-return events, return reversals, round-number behavior, reserve-wallet flows, Tether-USD demand proxies, and arbitrage spreads. The strongest causal claim comes from instrumenting flow with being just below a $500 threshold within 72 hours of authorization: Table VI reports 26.42 basis points per 100 Bitcoin overall and 65.44 basis points for 1LSg flows after authorization (pp. 1951–1952). That interpretation is fragile because round-number proximity can affect returns directly through technical trading or anchoring, the running variable is an endogenous asset price, and first-stage Wald statistics for decomposed 1LSg specifications fall to 7.639, 3.291, and 4.277. End-of-month evidence is consistent with reserve replenishment but does not observe bank reserves: high-issuance months have −6.15% Bitcoin and −7.62% top-five-index interactions (Table VII, pp. 1955–1957), while the simple Bitcoin relation becomes insignificant after excluding the two largest issuance months (p. 1954). Standard errors are generally heteroskedasticity/autocorrelation-adjusted, but low adjusted R-squared values, many related specifications, noisy demand proxies, timing error between blockchain settlement and exchange trades, and unavailable appendix material constrain certainty.
- Structure: The abstract and introduction frame the pulled-versus-pushed question, summarize the full result chain, and place cryptocurrency within historical bubbles and fraud (pp. 1913–1919). Section I supplies bubble, Bitcoin, exchange-hack, and Tether history before formalizing H1A–H2E (pp. 1919–1925). Section II documents prices and blockchains, the clustering algorithm, network maps, flow definitions, cross-chain validation, and deposit-account concentration (pp. 1925–1935). Section III moves from predictive regressions to event studies, bootstrap economic magnitudes, and asymmetric return reversals (pp. 1936–1947). Section IV discriminates pushed from pulled explanations through round-price thresholds and IV estimates, end-of-month reserve implications, Tether-USD demand tests, and cross-exchange arbitrage (pp. 1947–1961). Section V concludes with manipulation and regulatory implications (p. 1961), followed by submission metadata, references, and the supporting-information inventory (pp. 1962–1964).
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in blockchain entity resolution and two-ledger flow construction (Section II.B–II.E, pp. 1930–1935), followed by layered short-horizon price tests and account/exchange decompositions (Section III, pp. 1936–1947). Institutional detail on Tether, Bitfinex, banking access, reserve representations, and contemporaneous disclosures is substantial (Section I.C, pp. 1921–1923). Identification receives its deepest treatment in the round-threshold design (Section IV.A, pp. 1948–1952), but the exclusion restriction is asserted through placebos rather than secured institutionally. Reserve-backing analysis is comparatively indirect and sample-limited (Section IV.B.1, pp. 1953–1957). Formal theory is light: the pushed and pulled mechanisms generate directional hypotheses but no structural model or quantified equilibrium counterfactual. Much validation and robustness is delegated to the unread Internet Appendix.
- Breadth: The paper connects cryptocurrency market structure to speculative bubbles, fraud, price pressure, market manipulation, behavioral anchoring, network effects, capital flows, intermediary balance-sheet effects, and cross-exchange arbitrage. It considers legitimate cash demand, arbitrage, buy-the-dip behavior, ordinary return reversal, volatility, technical trading, exchange-wide activity, alternative accounts, other Tether exchanges, noisy Tether-price proxies, and reserve liquidation. Declared boundaries include inability to measure the complete Tether price effect across trading channels (p. 1917), limits of blockchain transparency (p. 1919), imprecision in aggregate impact calculations (pp. 1941, 1945), small-sample end-of-month inference (pp. 1953–1954), and noise and illiquidity in demand proxies (p. 1958). Additional credible readings remain: the concentrated addresses may represent an intermediary or coordinated liquidity provider rather than the ultimate beneficial trader; reactive liquidity provision can produce post-decline flows and reversals; and common news, within-exchange activity, or technical-threshold demand could violate causal assumptions.
- Optics: The interrogative title uses “Untethered” as an effective double meaning, while the abstract presents a compact exhibit ladder from timing and price effects through account concentration, round prices, autocorrelation, and reserve implications (p. 1913). The opening’s extended bubble-and-fraud analogy supplies stakes but also primes a nefarious interpretation before identification (pp. 1913–1919). The paper devotes roughly six pages to framing and hypotheses, ten to data and measurement, twenty-five to results, and one to the conclusion, signaling empirical depth. Figures 1–4 establish networks and actors before Tables II–IV address prediction and Figures 5–7 establish magnitude; Section IV then escalates to mechanism and causal claims. Captions are unusually self-contained, equations (1)–(3) make the constructed flow measure auditable, and prose repeatedly links tests to preregistered-looking hypotheses. Claim calibration is uneven: caveats are explicit, but “causing,” “manipulation,” “unbacked,” and “one entity” sometimes exceed what address inference, predictive timing, and the threshold instrument alone establish. Table II’s significance legend appears to print `**p < 0.001` where `***` is intended (p. 1937). The ownership/conflict disclosure on p. 1913 is visible, but the separately listed disclosure statement is absent from the artifact (p. 1964), limiting a complete optics assessment.
- Locations: Vehicle-currency and exchange-network role—pp. 1914–1916, Figure 1 (p. 1916), Figure 2 (p. 1922), Table I Panel A (p. 1927), Section II.C (pp. 1931–1932); hypotheses—Section I.D, pp. 1923–1925; data and measurement—Section II.A–II.E, pp. 1925–1935, especially equations (1)–(3), p. 1933; concentration—Figure 4 and Section II.E, pp. 1934–1935; predictive effects—Table II, pp. 1937–1938, Table III, pp. 1939–1940, Figure 5, p. 1942; economic magnitude and bootstraps—pp. 1941–1945, Figures 6–7, pp. 1943–1944; asymmetric reversals—Table IV, pp. 1946–1947; threshold behavior and identification—Figure 8, p. 1949, Table V, p. 1950, Table VI and identification discussion, pp. 1951–1952; reserve-backing implications—Figure 9, p. 1954, Table VII, pp. 1955–1957; demand and arbitrage alternatives—Table VIII, pp. 1959–1960, Section IV.C, pp. 1960–1961; limitations—pp. 1917, 1919, 1941, 1945, 1953–1954, 1958; conclusion—p. 1961; companion inventory—p. 1964.
- Implication: The admissible scientific takeaway is strong evidence of a concentrated, reactive Tether/Bitcoin flow pattern with substantial short-horizon predictive content and mechanism-consistent price support during the 2017 boom. The artifact does not by itself establish that Tether was issued without backing, identify the beneficial actor, or cleanly quantify a causal contribution to the full boom. As research communication, its most reusable feature is the cumulative triangulation from institutional hypotheses through cross-ledger measurement, actor decomposition, placebos, event timing, and mechanism tests; its principal caution is that a persuasive exhibit ladder can make suggestive reserve and manipulation evidence appear more causally settled than the design warrants.
