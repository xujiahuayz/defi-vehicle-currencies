### Carletti, De Marco, Ioannidou, and Sette (2021), “Banks as Patient Lenders: Evidence from a Tax Reform”

- Companions: Complete: the article’s cited Internet Appendix is bundled and was read at PDF pp. 50–54, containing Figs. IA.1–IA.2 and Tables IA.1–IA.3. No additional supplement, correction, or corrigendum is identified in the article or publication record.
- Uses: Supports the qualified claim that an Italian tax-induced substitution of household bank bonds with withdrawable term deposits changed the composition, rather than the aggregate quantity, of bank lending toward credit lines and loans longer than five years, especially for lower-risk firms (Secs. 4–5; Tables 2–3, 6, and 9). It also supplies mechanism-discrimination evidence concerning liquidity synergies, depositor discipline, guarantees, and deposit-market power (Secs. 5.2.2–5.2.4; Figs. 7–8; Tables 8–10; Appendix Table A1), but not clean causal identification of demandability itself. Use as a JFE communication exemplar only after checking the definitive published version.
- Scientific: The question is how greater reliance on deposits, holding total retail funding and investor class approximately fixed, affects bank lending. The institutional shock is Italy’s 2011 harmonization of household tax rates: deposit interest taxation fell from 27% to 20%, while private-security taxation, including bank bonds, rose from 12.5% to 20% (Sec. 2, PDF pp. 7–9). Data combine monthly supervisory bank-province deposits, quarterly household security holdings adjusted with security prices, Bank of Italy Credit Register bank-firm exposures above €30,000, bank balance sheets, CERVED firm accounts, and province characteristics (Sec. 3, PDF pp. 9–11; Table 1, PDF p. 40). Funding identification uses bank-province difference-in-differences, comparing a bank across provinces with different predetermined 2009 household bond holdings, with province and bank-time fixed effects (Eq. 1, PDF pp. 11–12). Lending identification instruments bank-level term-deposit growth with the deposit-share-weighted bond holdings of provinces in which the bank operated, then compares lending by differently exposed banks to the same firm using firm fixed effects and predetermined bank controls (Eqs. 2–3, PDF pp. 16–18). Table 3 shows bonds fall, deposits rise, and combined household bonds plus deposits do not change significantly. Baseline 2SLS estimates imply that a one-unit increase in log term-deposit growth raises credit-line growth by 0.064 (t=3.16) and growth in term loans longer than five years by 0.159 (t=3.89), while total credit and all term credit do not change significantly; multiplying by the reported 0.36 standard deviation gives approximately 2.3- and 5.7-percentage-point effects (Table 6, PDF p. 45). Baseline first-stage F-statistics are 22.08 and 40.97 for these outcomes. Effects survive controls for LTRO funding, exclusion of single-province banks, and a two-year post-window (Table 7, PDF p. 46; Table A3, PDF pp. 29–30), although the pre-reform placebo has first-stage F-statistics of only 2.18–3.83 and therefore provides little informative 2SLS falsification. Risk heterogeneity places increases in credit lines and long-term loans among low-risk firms and shows reductions in total term credit to high-risk firms (Table 9, PDF p. 48), but relevant interacted-model F-statistics fall to 7.29 and 7.27, weakening mechanism inference. Higher bank capitalization attenuates the credit-line response as the liquidity-synergy account predicts, but one Table 8 first stage is also weak at 8.11 (PDF p. 47). Later stressed-bank event plots show roughly 15% total-deposit and 20% term-deposit declines without an analogous retail-bond break (Fig. 8, PDF pp. 23–24 and 39); this is selected, descriptive evidence from 2015–2017, not causal evidence that anticipated runs produced the 2011–2012 lending response. The exclusion restriction remains contestable because geographic exposure may proxy for bank franchise, depositor wealth, or bank-specific regional opportunities; bond holdings correlate 0.89 with province GDP and 0.77 with population (Fig. IA.2, PDF p. 51), and Table 5 reports a normalized NPL difference of 0.59. Per-capita exposure, province controls, region-time effects, outlier exclusions, and restricted samples mitigate but do not eliminate this concern (Tables IA.2–IA.3, PDF pp. 53–54). The reform also jointly changes demandability, maturity, insurance/seniority, liquidity, and relative returns, so borrower-risk heterogeneity and later runs discriminate among interpretations but do not causally isolate demandability from all contractual attributes. The large-account evidence is bank-level, annual, and includes all investor types, limiting the claim that the reform’s household inflow itself was predominantly uninsured (Sec. 3, PDF pp. 9–10). No multiplicity adjustment is reported across the many outcomes and heterogeneity tests. External validity is bounded to unusually retail-bond-dependent Italian banks during a sovereign crisis and to firms with multiple qualifying bank relationships.
- Structure: PDF p. 1 gives title, authors, abstract, keywords, and acknowledgments. Section 1 (PDF pp. 2–7) frames competing demandability, guarantee, and market-power theories; previews the reform, two-stage design, results, mechanisms, and literatures. Section 2 (PDF pp. 7–9) explains the reform and aggregate funding patterns. Section 3 (PDF pp. 9–11) defines datasets, samples, and measurements. Section 4 (PDF pp. 11–16) develops the bank-province design, assesses parallel trends, estimates deposit-bond substitution, and examines bond maturity, seniority, and bank heterogeneity. Section 5.1 (PDF pp. 16–19) develops the bank-level instrument and within-firm lending design. Section 5.2.1 (PDF pp. 19–21) presents baseline lending estimates and robustness; Sec. 5.2.2 (PDF pp. 21–22) examines deposit-credit-line liquidity synergies; Sec. 5.2.3 (PDF pp. 22–24) contrasts demandability with guarantees using borrower and bank risk; Sec. 5.2.4 (PDF pp. 24–25) presents later stressed-bank runs. Section 6 concludes on PDF p. 25. The article appendix (PDF pp. 26–29) tests deposit-market power, nonfinancial-firm deposits, and a longer window; references occupy PDF pp. 30–31; Figs. 1–8 and Tables 1–10 occupy PDF pp. 32–49. The Internet Appendix (PDF pp. 50–54) covers the reverse 1996 reform, geographic exposure, bondholder characteristics, per-capita treatment, province controls, and sample restrictions.
- Depth: Effort is concentrated in institutional exposition, administrative-data linkage, two linked identification stages, fixed-effect absorption, maturity decomposition, and robustness to crisis-period confounds. Measurement depth is good for loan type, maturity, bond maturity/seniority, and security valuation, but thinner for deposit insurance status, early-withdrawal terms, borrower-risk validation, and the selected stressed-bank sample. The baseline lending estimates receive credible first-stage and within-firm support; mechanism sections rely more heavily on interactions, descriptive balance-sheet movements, and theory-implied signs, with weaker instruments in several specifications. Formal theory is imported rather than developed, and no welfare or general-equilibrium estimates are attempted.
- Breadth: The paper connects delegated monitoring and runnable debt, bank liquidity provision, maturity transformation, government guarantees, deposit-market power, deposit runs, monetary-policy transmission, and tax effects on bank capital structure (Introduction, PDF pp. 2–7). It addresses flight to quality, ECB LTRO funding, bank capitalization, local lending opportunities, market power, alternative exposure scaling, geographic outliers, bank type, and event-window length. Declared and effective boundaries are a single Italian household tax reform, a crisis-period lending window, unusually high retail-bond dependence, multi-bank firms, and later selected bank-stress episodes; it does not establish effects for ordinary monetary-policy deposit inflows, small insured depositors, single-bank borrowers, or non-crisis settings.
- Optics: The outcome-oriented title makes “patient lenders” memorable but partially embeds the long-maturity finding. The abstract follows a compact question–identification–result–mechanism sequence and appropriately uses “consistent with” for theory discrimination. The introduction is substantial but functional, moving from competing mechanisms to the source of variation, two-stage design, estimates, and literature positioning. The exhibit ladder is strong: aggregate motivation (Figs. 1–3), bank-province treatment and timing (Figs. 4–5; Tables 2–4), bank comparability and lending effects (Fig. 6; Tables 5–7), then mechanism evidence (Figs. 7–8; Tables 8–10). Captions are generally self-contained and notation is limited to four equations. Claim calibration deteriorates in the mechanism discussion: “strong empirical support,” “key evidence,” and the conclusion that demandability is effective “when not neutralized” exceed what selected runs and interaction patterns identify. The saved artifact also contains reviewer-facing inconsistencies: the Table 6 narrative calls the long-term/total-credit ratio significant, whereas the printed baseline estimate is 0.032 with t=1.52; Table 6’s caption points to Eq. 3 although the lending equation is Eq. 2 and Eq. 3 defines the instrument; the event-study omitted month differs between Sec. 4.2 and the Fig. 5 caption; and Sec. 5.2.4 calls the stress sample seven banks but names six.

- Implication: The defensible scientific takeaway is narrow but important: when Italian households were induced to replace retail bank bonds with withdrawable term deposits without materially expanding banks’ total retail funding, more-exposed banks reallocated lending toward credit lines and loans exceeding five years, disproportionately to safer firms. The design supports a causal interpretation for this lending-composition response more strongly than it supports the paper’s causal mechanism claim. Demandability is a plausible and well-developed reading, but government guarantees are not cleanly ruled out and demandability is not separately manipulated from maturity, liquidity, insurance, and return changes.
