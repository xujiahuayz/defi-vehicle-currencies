- Uses: Supports the theoretical claim that stronger aggregate ESG tastes lower green firms’ expected returns and CAPM alphas (Section 2.1, Proposition 1 and Corollaries 1–2, Eqs. 9–10, pp. 553–554); that unexpected strengthening of customer or investor ESG concerns can make green assets outperform despite lower expected returns (Section 3.2, Eqs. 40–42 and Proposition 6, pp. 556–557); that the market and an ESG factor deliver two-factor pricing (Section 2.3, Propositions 4–5, Eqs. 27–30, pp. 555–556; Section 3.1, Eqs. 31–35, p. 556); that taste dispersion, not the average taste alone, creates ESG portfolio tilts and determines industry size (Corollary 4, p. 555; Section 4.5, Eqs. 51–54 and Fig. 5, pp. 559–561); that climate hedging can further lower green expected returns (Section 5, Proposition 7 and Eqs. 56, 58–60, pp. 561–562); and that ESG tastes can increase modeled social impact through greener operations and investment reallocation (Section 6, Propositions 9–10, Eqs. 61–67 and Figs. 6–7, pp. 563–565). It is also a published JFE exemplar for a theory paper that moves from equilibrium primitives to pricing, calibration, extension, and real effects.
- Scientific: The paper asks how heterogeneous preferences for sustainable holdings affect equilibrium asset prices, portfolios, the ESG-management industry, climate-risk premia, and firms’ real behavior. Its baseline is a one-period normal-return economy with exponential utility, a continuum of atomistic investors, common relative risk aversion, observable firm greenness \(g_n\), and nonpecuniary holding benefits \(d_i g_n\) (Section 2, Eqs. 1–8, pp. 553–554). Market clearing produces expected returns equal to CAPM returns minus a taste term, so greener stocks have lower expected returns and alphas (Eqs. 9–10); deviations of individual tastes from the wealth-weighted mean generate three-fund separation and opposing green/brown tilts (Eqs. 14–20, pp. 554–555). A scaled ESG-portfolio return prices the cross-section with the market, while customer-demand shocks and shifts in investor tastes generate its realizations (Eqs. 31–42, pp. 556–557). The quantitative exercise is a calibration, not estimation: it imposes two investor types, equal market weights, orthogonality and factor-covariance restrictions, market volatility 20%, market premium 8%, four willingness-to-sacrifice values from 1% to 4%, and ESG-factor volatility equal to 20% of market volatility (Section 4.1 and Eq. 43–45, pp. 557–558; Section 4.5, p. 560). It yields exact comparative statics rather than sampling uncertainty: return differences equal \(-2\lambda\Delta\) (Eq. 46), ESG-investor alpha equals \(-2\lambda(1-\lambda)\Delta\) (Eq. 48), surplus lies between \(\Delta/2\) and \(\Delta\) (Eq. 50), and industry size is hump-shaped in the ESG wealth share (Eq. 54 and Fig. 5). The climate extension adds a nontraded utility shock and climate betas but does not empirically establish which firms hedge climate risk; the paper explicitly calls this an empirical question and relies on cited evidence before studying the perfect-negative-correlation special case (Section 5.2, pp. 561–562). The real-effects extension assumes value-maximizing managers, quadratic adjustment costs, and an alpha-to-cost-of-capital channel; it proves positive impact inside that model but supplies no firm-level data or causal identification (Section 6, pp. 563–565). Important approximations are the payoff-discounting linearization in Eq. 37 (footnote 7, p. 557) and \(\log(1+x)\approx x\) behind Eq. 65 (pp. 563, 570). Credible alternative readings are that ESG factor pricing is mechanically risk-based but economically taste-driven (Eqs. 29–30, pp. 555–556), realized green outperformance can reflect unanticipated taste shifts rather than positive ex ante alpha (Proposition 6, p. 557), and aggregate-impact preferences alone do nothing without coordination because infinitesimal investors do not internalize their impact (Proposition 11, Eq. 68, p. 565).
- Structure: The abstract states six headline implications (p. 550). Section 1 motivates the scale of sustainable investing, previews every result, positions the model against climate-risk, cost-of-capital, sustainable-investing, coordination, and engagement literatures, and provides the roadmap (pp. 550–553). Section 2 builds the baseline economy, derives expected returns, investor portfolio tilts, three-fund separation, and market-plus-ESG two-factor pricing (pp. 553–556). Section 3 constructs an empirically implementable ESG factor and decomposes its shocks into customer and investor channels (pp. 556–557). Section 4 imposes a two-investor calibration and studies return differences, market correlations, alphas, investor surplus, and ESG-industry size (pp. 557–561). Section 5 adds nontraded climate risk, derives four-fund separation, and analyzes when green stocks hedge climate shocks (pp. 561–562). Section 6 endogenizes firm investment and greenness, decomposes social impact, and distinguishes utility from holdings from utility over aggregate impact (pp. 563–565). Section 7 consolidates predictions and identifies empirical opportunities and historical-sample limitations (p. 566). The Appendix supplies derivations for the principal equilibrium, calibration, climate, and real-effect equations (pp. 566–570), followed by references (pp. 570–571).
- Depth: Analytical depth is concentrated in equilibrium derivation and market clearing (Section 2 and Appendix pp. 566–568), factor construction and shock decomposition (Section 3 and Eq. 42 derivation, pp. 556–557, 567–568), closed-form calibration results (Section 4 and Appendix pp. 568–569), and the climate and firm-choice extensions (Sections 5–6 and Appendix pp. 569–570). The paper repeatedly translates formal results into economic intuition after propositions and uses seven figures to expose comparative statics. Institutional detail, measurement validity, empirical implementation, parameter sensitivity, statistical uncertainty, and causal identification are light: \(g_n\) is treated as observable without choosing or validating an ESG measure; the factor-construction discussion supplies regressions and possible taste-shift proxies but no implementation (Section 3.1–3.2, pp. 556–557); and the real-effects claims remain model implications rather than tested effects.
- Breadth: The paper spans asset pricing, portfolio choice, factor construction, sustainable-investment industry organization, climate hedging, cost of capital, corporate investment, endogenous greenness, and externalities. It distinguishes holding tastes from preferences over aggregate impact (Sections 2 and 6.3), customer from investor shocks (Section 3.2), ex ante from ex post performance (pp. 552, 557), and taste-based from fundamental-risk interpretations of factor premia (pp. 555–556, 562). The introduction compares its mechanism with exclusion and reduced risk sharing, customer loyalty and pricing power, investor unawareness, financing constraints and coordination, and shareholder engagement (pp. 552–553). Declared boundaries include a single-period setting, normality and exponential utility, common risk aversion, market ESG neutrality, atomistic uncoordinated investors, value-maximizing managers, and no direct engagement; Section 6.3 and the conclusion explicitly leave coordination, engagement, joint climate-impact uncertainty, channel magnitudes, empirical testing, and historical applicability open (pp. 565–566).
- Optics: The direct title names the object and equilibrium lens. The abstract is prediction-dense and moves in sequence from expected returns to shocks, factor pricing, industry size, and social impact (p. 550). The opening uses the growth of sustainable assets to establish stakes, then previews essentially the entire paper before reviewing adjacent literatures (pp. 550–553), giving readers a clear contribution map before notation begins. Claims are generally calibrated to the model—especially the explicit warning that realized returns do not cleanly identify ex ante effects (p. 552) and the acknowledgment that green-versus-brown climate hedging is empirical (p. 562)—although phrases such as “sustainable investing produces positive social impact” are broader than the paper’s purely theoretical identification unless the model qualifier is retained. The exhibit ladder is disciplined: Figs. 1–5 visualize the calibrated return, correlation, alpha, surplus, and industry-size comparative statics (pp. 558–561), while Figs. 6–7 decompose firm and aggregate impact (pp. 564–565); there are no tables. Captions identify the plotted quantity, parameters, and panels, with Figs. 6–7 especially self-contained. Eleven propositions, four corollaries, numbered equations, and an integrated proof appendix create reviewer-facing traceability. Notation is dense but stable, and equations are followed by interpretation. - Implication: The paper is admissible as a theoretical foundation for distinguishing lower green expected returns from realized green outperformance, interpreting ESG factor premia, linking preference dispersion to portfolio specialization, and describing cost-of-capital channels through which ESG demand may affect investment and greenness. It is not evidence that any particular ESG score validly measures \(g_n\), that the proposed factor prices observed returns, that calibrated magnitudes describe an actual ESG industry, or that sustainable investing causally changes firms; those uses require separate measurement and empirical identification. Any project claim about “positive social impact” should state that the result is conditional on the paper’s equilibrium, adjustment-cost, and value-maximization assumptions and should cite Section 6 rather than the abstract alone.
