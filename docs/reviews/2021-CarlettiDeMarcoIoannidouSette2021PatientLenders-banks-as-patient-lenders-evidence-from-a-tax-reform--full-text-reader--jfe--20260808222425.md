### Carletti et al. (2021), “Banks as patient lenders: Evidence from a tax reform”

- Uses: Supports a carefully qualified claim that Italy’s 2011 household investment-income tax reform induced substitution from retail bank bonds into deposits without materially increasing combined bond-plus-deposit funding (Section 2, journal pp. 9–11; Tables 2–4, pp. 14–16), and that reform-induced term-deposit growth changed the composition rather than the total level of credit toward credit lines and loans exceeding five years (Section 5.2.1, Table 6, pp. 18–19). It supports mechanism-consistent—not definitive mechanism-identifying—evidence for liquidity-production synergies and depositor discipline (Table 8 and Fig. 7, pp. 21–22; Tables 9–10 and Fig. 8, pp. 22–24). It is also a published JFE exemplar of an identification-first empirical paper whose exhibit sequence moves from institutional shock and first stage to lending outcomes, mechanisms, and robustness.
- Scientific: The question is how greater reliance on deposit rather than retail-bond funding affects bank lending, holding investor class and approximately total funding fixed (Introduction, journal pp. 6–9). The contribution is a two-stage empirical design linking a tax-induced household portfolio substitution to bank credit composition and using borrower and bank heterogeneity plus later stress episodes to distinguish demandability from government guarantees and deposit-market power. Data combine monthly supervisory bank-province deposits, quarterly SHS/CSDB security holdings and prices, Italian Credit Register bank-firm exposures above €30,000, bank balance sheets, CERVED firm accounts, and province characteristics; the main lending sample contains 315,708 relationships involving 107,654 firms and 482 banks (Section 3, pp. 11–12; Tables 1 and 6, pp. 12 and 19). Measurement distinguishes demand and term deposits, bonds, credit lines, term loans below or above five years, and firm risk using an ordinal “Altman Z-score,” with firms scoring at least seven classified as risky (Section 5.1, p. 18; Table 9, p. 23); the paper gives limited validation of that risk threshold. Funding identification uses bank-time and province fixed effects with predetermined province household bond holdings in Eq. (1), while lending identification instruments bank term-deposit growth with exposure constructed from 2009 branch geography and province bond holdings in Eqs. (2)–(3), adding firm fixed effects and predetermined bank controls (pp. 12–13, 17–18). A one-standard-deviation difference in province bond holdings predicts 27.1% larger annual total-deposit increases and 67.5% larger annual term-deposit increases in level specifications, with corresponding growth effects of about 1.5% and 3.5% annually (Table 2 and discussion, pp. 13–15); bonds fall while combined deposits plus bonds do not change significantly (Table 3, p. 16). A one-standard-deviation increase in term-deposit growth, approximately 36%, is estimated to raise credit-line growth by 2.3 percentage points and loan growth at maturities above five years by 5.72 points, without significantly changing total credit (Table 6 and discussion, p. 19). Effects are concentrated among low-risk firms; the reported magnitudes are approximately +1.72 points for credit lines and +6.48 points for long-term loans to low-risk firms, versus about −7 points for term lending to risky firms (Table 9 and discussion, pp. 22–23). Baseline first-stage F-statistics are 22.08–40.97, but several interaction specifications fall below ten—7.29 and 7.27 in Table 9 and 8.11 in Table 8—weakening the strongest mechanism claims. Standard errors are clustered by province for funding regressions and two-way by bank and firm for lending regressions; inference is presented mainly through t-statistics and significance levels rather than confidence intervals. Causal interpretation remains conditional on the exclusion restriction that predetermined branch geography and local household bond portfolios affect post-reform lending only through deposit growth; firm fixed effects remove common borrower demand but not bank-firm-specific shocks or unobserved bank traits correlated with geographic footprint. Dynamic and untreated-firm tests strengthen the funding first stage (Fig. 5 and Table A2, pp. 15 and 25), while the pre-reform lending placebo has a very weak first stage, F = 2.18–3.83, and therefore supplies limited evidence from its null coefficients (Table 7, pp. 20–21). The selected stressed-bank evidence is descriptive, occurs in 2015–2017 rather than the reform window, and cannot by itself identify the 2011 lending mechanism (Fig. 8, pp. 23–24).
- Structure: The abstract states shock, design, lending result, and mechanism interpretation (journal p. 6). Section 1 frames a contest among demandability, government-guarantee stability, and market-power theories; previews the data, two-step design, estimates, mechanisms, and literature contributions (pp. 6–9). Section 2 explains the reform and uses aggregate cross-country and historical patterns to motivate the shock while confronting flight-to-quality and ECB-intervention alternatives (pp. 9–11; Figs. 1–3). Section 3 documents datasets, samples, variables, and summary statistics (pp. 11–12; Table 1). Section 4 develops the bank-province difference-in-differences design, assesses parallel trends, establishes bond-to-deposit substitution, and studies maturity, seniority, and bank heterogeneity (pp. 12–16; Eq. 1, Figs. 4–5, Tables 2–4). Section 5 constructs the bank-level instrument and within-firm lending design (pp. 16–18; Eqs. 2–3, Table 5), presents baseline lending and identification checks (pp. 18–21; Fig. 6, Tables 6–7), then separates liquidity synergies, demandability versus guarantees, borrower risk, bank capitalization, and later stressed-bank runs (pp. 21–24; Tables 8–10, Figs. 7–8). Section 6 concludes with lending and policy implications (p. 24). Appendix A tests deposit-market power as an additional channel and includes firm-deposit and longer-window checks (pp. 24–26; Eq. A.1, Tables A1–A3), followed by references on p. 26.
- Depth: Institutional and identification effort is concentrated in the tax reform’s timing, incidence, within-investor substitution, bond maturity, province exposure, bank internal capital markets, and borrower fixed effects (Sections 2, 4.1–4.3, and 5.1). Measurement depth is strongest for funding products and loan maturity but lighter for depositor identity, actual insurance status, credit-line utilization, pricing, new originations versus outstanding balances, and the firm-risk score. Robustness is extensive on parallel trends, untreated firm deposits, bond maturity and seniority, flight to quality, LTRO funding, single-province banks, pre-period placebos, market power, and a two-year outcome window (Figs. 5–6; Tables 4, 7, A1–A3), although several supporting Internet Appendix checks remain unaudited. There is no original formal model; mechanism predictions are imported from established theories and tested through heterogeneity and balance-sheet comovement. Direct evidence on depositor discipline is comparatively light because the run analysis is a selected, later-event comparison without formal identification.
- Breadth: The paper spans financial-intermediation theories of liquidity production, demandable-debt discipline, maturity transformation, guarantees, deposit-market power, bank runs, monetary-policy transmission, regional liquidity shocks, and taxation of bank capital structures (Introduction, pp. 6–9). It directly addresses flight to quality, contemporaneous sovereign stress, ECB LTRO funding, bank capitalization, geographic specialization, borrower demand, funding-market power, bond seniority, and bond maturity. It retains market power as an independent complementary mechanism rather than claiming demandability is exclusive (Appendix A, pp. 24–26). Declared and effective boundaries are Italy’s unusual household ownership of bank bonds, the sovereign-debt-crisis environment, multi-bank firms with both credit lines and term loans, a short 2010–2012 main window, and effects on credit quantities and maturity rather than loan prices, performance, defaults, or welfare. Credible alternative readings include portfolio reallocation caused by unobserved bank exposure correlated with branch geography, crisis-era bank risk management rather than depositor discipline specifically, and differential borrower-bank matching not fully absorbed by firm fixed effects.
- Optics: The title converts a technical funding-composition result into the memorable “patient lenders” frame, although patience is inferred from longer maturity and safer borrower allocation rather than directly measured. The abstract is compact and calibrated on the identified funding and lending results but states the theoretical interpretation more firmly than the mechanism evidence warrants (p. 6). The opening efficiently creates a theory contest, states the endogenous-funding problem, introduces the reform, gives the €75 billion substitution and balance-sheet shares, then previews design and estimates before positioning the literatures (pp. 6–9). The exhibit ladder is strong: aggregate motivation in Figs. 1–3; parallel trends and first stage in Figs. 4–5 and Tables 2–4; balance and lending estimates in Tables 5–7; liquidity and risk mechanisms in Fig. 7 and Tables 8–10; direct run illustration in Fig. 8; and compact appendix checks in Tables A1–A3. Captions are generally self-contained and identify samples, variables, fixed effects, clustering, and significance conventions. Equations are few and functional, though notation and compressed double-column prose are dense. Most page space is devoted to identification and mechanism tests rather than institutional description or conclusion, fitting a causal empirical JFE presentation. Reviewer-facing credibility is built through repeated placebo and alternative-channel tests, but phrases such as “strong empirical support,” “benefits emanate,” and taxation as a “powerful financial stability tool” (pp. 22, 24) exceed what heterogeneity tests and unreported welfare effects establish. The stress section says “seven Italian banks” but names only six—Monte dei Paschi, Banca Carige, Banca Etruria, Banca Marche, CariFerrara, and CariChieti (p. 23)—an auditable reporting inconsistency.

- Implication: The admissible project claim is that, conditional on the geographic-exposure instrument’s exclusion restriction, an Italian tax-driven substitution from household-held bank bonds to demandable term deposits caused banks to recompose lending toward credit lines and loans beyond five years, especially for observably safer firms, without increasing total credit. The paper should not be cited as direct causal proof that run threats discipline banks, that deposits are universally less stable than bonds, or that the reform improved financial stability: those are interpretations supported by heterogeneous responses and descriptive later stress events, not independently identified outcomes.
