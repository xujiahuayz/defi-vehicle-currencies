### Eren and Malamud (2021), Internet Appendix to “Dominant Currency Debt”
- Status: Complete full-text read of all 39 PDF pages, including proofs, equations, tables, figures, notes, extensions, and references.
- Roles: Independent second reader; theory-proof supplement; empirical-robustness record; dynamic-capital-structure derivation; emerging-market extension; JFE research-communication exemplar.
- Source: Egemen Eren and Semyon Malamud, “Internet Appendix: Dominant Currency Debt,” May 16, 2021, saved as 2021-ErenMalamud2022DominantDebtAppendix-supplement-internet-appendix-to-dominant-currency-debt.pdf, 39 pages.
- Version: Internet Appendix dated May 16, 2021, explicitly identified on p. 1 as the companion to “Dominant Currency Debt” published in the Journal of Financial Economics; the artifact is supplementary material, not the standalone published article, and supplies no volume, issue, pagination, or DOI.
- Uses: Supports claims that unhedged foreign-currency exposure can be privately optimal under debt overhang (Proposition A.1, p. 2); supplies the Kuhn–Tucker derivation of dollar-only borrowing and its alternative-currency representation (Theorem 2.1 and Lemma A.2, pp. 3–4); proves the monotonic relation used for inflation-risk premia (Theorem 2.3 and Lemma A.3, p. 5); documents bank/non-bank and backward-looking robustness exercises (Tables B.1–B.2, pp. 6–7; Section C, pp. 8–13); develops the dynamic-capital-structure extension (Section D, pp. 14–30); and derives and tests predictions for local-currency versus dollar debt in emerging markets (Section E, pp. 31–38). It cannot by itself verify claims whose definitions, baseline estimates, or full model setup appear only in the absent main paper.
- Scientific: The appendix develops the mechanism that debt issuers favor currencies whose exchange-rate payoffs covary advantageously with bad firm-value states, because limited liability and debt overhang shift hedging benefits toward creditors. Proposition A.1 establishes a model-specific no-hedging result, not a general empirical prohibition on hedging (p. 2). Theorem 2.1’s dollar-dominance condition is derived from Kuhn–Tucker comparisons of currency-specific issuance costs and risk-adjusted exchange-rate payoffs (pp. 3–4), while Theorem 2.3 uses a covariance argument under zero idiosyncratic shocks to relate the inflation-risk premium monotonically to a loading parameter (p. 5). The empirical material is descriptive and prediction-consistent rather than causally identified: quarterly OLS links dollar issuance shares to forward-looking quanto and inflation-risk premia with robust standard errors, trends, and issuance controls (Tables B.1–B.2, pp. 6–7); horizon regressions, a 30-observation VAR(2), impulse responses, and non-overlapping samples assess exchange-rate safe-haven patterns (Section C, pp. 8–13); and a 17-country cross-section relates local-currency-to-dollar debt ratios to residual inflation comovement and volatility (Eqs. E.1–E.4 and Table E.1, pp. 33–35). The strongest forward-looking result is the negative association of dollar share with the euro-dollar quanto risk premium and euro inflation-risk premium; dollar inflation-risk-premium coefficients are mostly insignificant (pp. 6–7). In the emerging-market test, residual inflation comovement is positive and precisely estimated, approximately 6.0–6.5, whereas residual inflation volatility has the predicted negative sign but is insignificant, −1.784 with standard error 1.479 (Table E.1, p. 35). Uncertainty is reported through robust or Newey–West standard errors and confidence bands, but small samples are material: 24 or 84 quarterly observations in Section B, 30 annual VAR observations in Table C.1, as few as nine observations at a five-year non-overlapping horizon, and 16–17 countries in Table E.1 (pp. 6–7, 11, 13, 35). Key boundaries include zero idiosyncratic shocks in the Theorem 2.3 proof (p. 5); technical survival and no-new-debt-at-default-threshold conditions plus small-parameter approximations in the dynamic model (pp. 17–27); and equal issuance costs, sufficiently small shock variances, interior mixed-currency issuance, and ℓ near one for Proposition E.1 (p. 31). Plausible alternative readings are that issuance-cost advantages, market depth, economy size, institutional openness, or common global conditions generate the observed currency shares; the appendix acknowledges several of these, especially for sterling, but does not identify the proposed mechanism against them causally (pp. 9, 31–35).
- Structure: Page 1 identifies the artifact and publication relationship. Section A, pp. 2–5, supplies the no-hedging proposition, proofs of main-paper Theorems 2.1 and 2.3, an alternative-currency pricing-kernel formulation, and the covariance lemma. Section B, pp. 6–7, splits the forward-looking issuance regressions into banks and non-banks. Section C, pp. 8–13, moves through bilateral exchange-rate horizon regressions (C.1 and Fig. C.1), the yen-versus-pound comparison (C.2 and Fig. C.2), an MSCI World VAR and impulse responses (C.3, Table C.1, and Fig. C.3), and non-overlapping horizon regressions (C.4 and Fig. C.4). Section D, pp. 14–30, derives the dynamic-capital-structure problem, default threshold, policy derivatives, Kuhn–Tucker conditions, small-debt approximation, logarithmic limiting term, and Proposition 6.1. Section E, pp. 31–38, introduces the local-versus-dollar extension, states Proposition E.1, translates it into two empirical hypotheses, reports the country cross-section, and proves the extension through Theorem E.2. Page 39 contains the references.
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in Sections A and D: pp. 2–5 establish the static optimality conditions, while pp. 14–30 devote seventeen pages to the dynamic value function, implicit policy derivatives, default threshold, approximations, and issuance/repurchase conditions. The emerging-market extension also has substantial formal depth in Theorem E.2 and the small-variance covariance derivation (pp. 36–38). Empirical depth is lighter: Section B reports compact regressions without additional diagnostics (pp. 6–7); Section C adds alternative exchange rates, indices, VAR responses, and non-overlapping observations but remains vulnerable to overlap, low power, and very small annual samples (pp. 8–13); Section E documents measurement construction and controls but relies on a single small country cross-section and discretionary outlier exclusion (pp. 32–35). Institutional detail, variable validation, missing-data treatment, leverage composition, and sensitivity to richer country covariates are limited.
- Breadth: The appendix spans corporate debt denomination, hedging, exchange-rate risk, inflation-risk and quanto premia, safe-haven behavior, dynamic capital structure, and emerging-market local-currency borrowing. It examines banks versus non-banks, four bilateral dollar rates, S&P 500 versus MSCI World benchmarks, overlapping versus non-overlapping horizons, pound versus yen debt shares, and local-versus-dollar debt across 21 initially listed emerging markets, narrowed to 17 for regressions (pp. 6–13, 31–35). It addresses incomplete hedging, alternative international currencies, issuance costs, economy size, nominal rates, inflation, financial openness, and global/domestic equity controls. Declared boundaries are unusually visible, but competing mechanisms are mostly acknowledged rather than discriminated; the cross-sectional evidence is explicitly outside the paper’s primary global-currency focus (p. 31).
- Optics: The title page immediately labels the document as an Internet Appendix and ties it to the JFE publication, preventing confusion with the main article (p. 1). There is no substantive abstract or introduction; the document opens directly with theory support, appropriate for supplementary material but unsuitable as a self-contained entry point. The exhibit ladder is functional: two subgroup regression tables, bilateral and alternative-index figures, a yen-pound descriptive comparison, a VAR table and impulse responses, non-overlapping estimates, then an emerging-market figure and regression table (pp. 6–13, 32, 35). Captions generally specify variables, horizons, samples, uncertainty procedures, and sources, especially Figs. C.1, C.3, and C.4 (pp. 9, 12–13); Fig. C.2 and Fig. E.1 are more descriptive (pp. 10, 32). Claims are usually calibrated with “qualitatively similar,” “in line with,” and explicit insignificance or small-sample caveats (pp. 8, 11, 33–34), although statements that firms “would strictly prefer” a currency lean heavily on the maintained model and proxy validity (pp. 8–9). Notation is dense and occasionally inconsistent or typographically rough—spacing errors, malformed symbols, “parantheses,” “det,” and awkward sentences appear on pp. 3–5, 11, 16, 30, and 38—making Section D particularly difficult to audit. Page allocation strongly favors derivation over empirical exposition: 22 of 39 pages cover formal proofs and dynamic theory, while robustness tables and figures receive concise presentation.

- Implication: This artifact materially strengthens the audit trail for the paper’s theoretical dominance condition, dynamic extension, and stated robustness exercises, and it is useful as a JFE exemplar of moving long proofs and secondary tests outside the main article. Its evidence supports mechanism-consistent associations and comparative statics, not causal proof that risk properties create dollar debt dominance. Any project claim should therefore cite the exact proposition or exhibit and preserve the appendix’s assumptions, weak or insignificant results, and small-sample limitations; claims about baseline magnitudes, variable definitions, or the complete contribution still require the published main paper.
