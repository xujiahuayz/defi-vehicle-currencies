- Scientific: The scientific question is how fragmenting trade across exchanges changes strategic price impact, risk-sharing efficiency, information aggregation, and liquidity-trader costs. The core mechanism is cross-exchange inference: a trader’s optimal demand on one venue depends on what its price reveals about other venues, summarized in the baseline by the price-correlation coefficient ρ_E (Eq. B5, p. 6). Allocative efficiency is ranked by the distance of Eα_E from one (Lemma 2, pp. 4–5); Eα_E rises from (N−2)/(N−1) toward N/(N−1), so fragmentation initially improves risk sharing but can produce over-adjustment, with the efficient exchange count characterized in Eq. C2 and the following solution (p. 10). At the same time, price impact rises with fragmentation (Appendix C1, pp. 8–9), an individual venue becomes less informative while the vector or sum of prices becomes more informative (Appendix C3, pp. 10–11), and expected liquidity-trader payments rise (p. 11). When aggregate strategic inventory is observable, price impact instead equals 2b/(N−2), independent of E (Eqs. D3–D6, pp. 13–14). The dynamic construction preserves the static efficient exchange count but raises within-period price impact through intertemporal effects (Eqs. E18–E25, pp. 20–27); Brownian inventory shocks yield the same incentives up to an additive constant (Appendix E2, pp. 27–28). Endogenous liquidity supply preserves the mechanism only under stated parameter conditions (Eqs. F1–F4 and Propositions 2–3, pp. 29–32). Private payoff information adds an adverse-selection/inference term to price impact (Eqs. G2–G9, pp. 34–38), allows multiplicity for large E, and leaves nonzero informational trading in the large-E limit (Propositions 5–6, pp. 39–42). Arbitrary covariance can yield one to three equilibria and changes the limiting Eα_E according to endowment–liquidity covariance (Theorem 6, Eqs. H11–H18, pp. 43–49). This is an analytical model: there are no data, empirical measurements, estimated effects, standard errors, predictive tests, or causal identification. Randomness is parametric rather than estimated. Results are conditional on quadratic costs, symmetric affine strategies, denominators requiring N>2, and model-specific Gaussian/information assumptions; additional restrictions include ζ>0 for sufficiency (pp. 1–4), full support for uniqueness in Appendix D (p. 13), finite-variance and no-Ponzi conditions in the dynamic model (pp. 15, 22–26), downward-sloping demand in Appendix G (p. 33), and positive-definite covariance in Appendix H (p. 43). Fragmentation therefore cannot be read as unconditionally welfare-improving or as an empirically identified effect.
- Structure: Appendix A establishes the general verification theorem and admissible-deviation argument (pp. 1–4). Appendix B proves the baseline efficiency lemma and main-text Theorem 1 through equilibrium equations, existence, uniqueness, and comparative statics (pp. 4–8). Appendix C proves the Section IV propositions on price impact, optimal E, informativeness, and payments (pp. 8–11). Appendix D derives and verifies the Section V equilibrium when aggregate endowment is observed (pp. 12–14). Appendix E gives a six-step dynamic-programming proof—Bellman equation, candidate value, coefficient solution, unrestricted-domain argument, transversality, and final verification—followed by the Brownian-shock equivalence (pp. 14–28). Appendix F endogenizes local liquidity-trader orders and derives conditional welfare comparisons (pp. 28–32). Appendix G introduces private payoff signals, derives conditional-normal projections and equilibrium equations, then studies existence, limits, and welfare (pp. 32–42). Appendix H permits an arbitrary covariance structure, derives conditional moments and equilibrium equations, and supplies existence, uniqueness, limit, efficiency, payment, and monotonicity results (pp. 43–52).
- Depth: Analytical depth is greatest in the admissible-strategy verification (Eqs. A1–A7, pp. 1–4), the dynamic Bellman and transversality argument (Eqs. E1–E25, pp. 14–27), and the conditional-projection calculations for private information and arbitrary covariance (pp. 34–38 and 44–49). Baseline comparative statics receive compact algebraic proofs (pp. 5–11), while the observable-aggregate and endogenous-liquidity variants are substantially shorter (pp. 12–14 and 28–32). Several robustness claims receive lighter treatment: Appendix G omits Theorem 4 part 3’s computation and repeatedly invokes an unstated argument analogous to Theorem 1 (pp. 38, 40–42); Appendix H omits coefficient-matching algebra (p. 48), and Propositions 9 and 11 are dispatched as “analogous” proofs (p. 52). Institutional detail, measurement validity, empirical identification, calibration, and quantitative robustness are absent. Proposition 10 also has an audit-relevant internal sign discrepancy: its displayed statement uses a negative α_E N(η+μ_Xμ_Q) term, while the concluding calculation uses a positive term (p. 52).
- Breadth: The appendix covers static and dynamic trading, allocative efficiency, strategic price impact, venue-level and aggregate price informativeness, liquidity-trader transfers, endogenous local order supply, private asset-payoff information, correlated endowments and liquidity demand, equilibrium multiplicity, and discrete optimal venue counts. It distinguishes allocation, information, and payment effects rather than collapsing them into a single welfare claim. Declared boundaries include symmetric affine equilibria throughout; Gaussian projection arguments in the baseline extensions; local liquidity traders without cross-exchange access (pp. 28–30); monotone decreasing demand in the private-information model (p. 33); conditional large-E or large-c results (pp. 31–32, 39–42); and covariance restrictions needed for a valid joint distribution (p. 43). It does not address entry costs, exchange design, heterogeneous strategic technologies, non-affine equilibria, empirical magnitudes, or general-equilibrium venue formation.

- Implication: The appendix supplies substantial analytical support for a qualified mechanism claim: fragmentation changes cross-venue inference and strategic responsiveness, can improve risk sharing up to a finite optimum, but simultaneously raises price impact and liquidity-trader payments and may induce over-adjustment. Dynamics preserve the model’s optimal venue count while increasing impact; private information and correlated primitives materially alter limiting allocations and may create multiplicity. It supports theoretical mechanism discrimination, not causal or quantitative claims about actual markets.
