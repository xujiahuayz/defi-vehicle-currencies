### Griffin and Shams (2020), Internet Appendix for “Is Bitcoin Really Un-Tethered?”
- Status: Incomplete source-set read: all 45 supplied appendix pages were read sequentially, including figures, tables, notes, equations, and reference, but the main article was not supplied or read; appendix-level claims are auditable, while paper-level completion cannot be claimed.
- Roles: Published empirical-methods and robustness companion; load-bearing source for wallet labeling, exchange-cluster validation, construction of 1LSg flow, authorization-to-flow timing, and alternative explanations for the documented flow-return relation.
- Source: John M. Griffin and Amin Shams, Internet Appendix for “Is Bitcoin Really Un-Tethered?,” Journal of Finance supporting information, DOI 10.1111/jofi.12903, saved as 2020-GriffinShams2020UntetheredAppendix-supplement-internet-appendix-to-is-bitcoin-really-untethered.pdf, 45 PDF pages.
- Version: Published Internet Appendix supporting the Journal of Finance article; the saved filename identifies 2020, although the appendix itself prints no date. It is neither a standalone article nor a working paper.
- Companions: Incomplete: the required main article, “Is Bitcoin Really Un-Tethered?,” is explicitly identified on pp. 1 and 10 but was not supplied or read. The supplied artifact names no additional supplement, correction, or corrigendum, but it does not establish that none exists.
- Uses: Supports verification of exchange-wallet labels through independently labeled cross-blockchain flows (Section I.A–C, pp. 3–5; Figure IA5, p. 16; Figure IA14, pp. 25–26), the linked-wallet/1LSg construction (Section I.D–E, pp. 5–8; Figures IA7–IA9, pp. 18–20), and delayed movement following Tether authorization (Section III, p. 10; Figures IA3–IA4, pp. 14–15). It also supplies robustness on altcoin and exchange returns, reversals, end-of-month behavior, Tether-dollar rates, and arbitrage spreads (Tables IAIII–IAXIV, pp. 30–45). It does not independently establish Tether’s dollar backing, beneficial ownership of the linked wallets, intent to manipulate, or a causal price effect.
- Scientific: The appendix asks whether blockchain labels and the concentrated 1LSg transaction channel are credible and whether flow patterns survive alternative market explanations. It combines Omni-layer Tether transfers, clustered Bitcoin addresses, public address labels, exchange volume and prices, hourly returns, and issuance events, chiefly from March 1, 2017 through March 31, 2018 (Figures IA3, IA6, IA10–IA15; Tables IAIII–IAIV), with monthly tests extending to March 2016–March 2018 (Tables IAVIII–IAIX). Exchange labels are validated by unusually high matched flow correlations, adjusted R², and similar magnitudes (Equation IA1 and Figure IA5, pp. 4, 16); Poloniex and Bittrex inflows explain own-exchange volume with adjusted R² of 0.30 and 0.23, versus near-zero placebos, and both reach 0.48 with daily data (p. 5; Figure IA14, pp. 25–26). The 1LSg linkage rests on concentration and comovement: 90% of Bitfinex-to-Poloniex Tether uses 1J1d, 72% of Bitfinex-to-Bittrex Tether uses 1AA6, 66% of returning Bitcoin uses 1LSg, and matched correlations are 0.69 and 0.64 (pp. 5–6; Equations IA2–IA3; Figure IA8, p. 19). Measurement is formalized in Equations IA4–IA7 (pp. 7–8), but Equation IA6 prints NetTetherFlow twice even though the prose describes averaging Bitcoin and Tether flows, creating a material transcription ambiguity. Identification remains observational: VAR ordering, event timing, matched events, fixed effects, placebo exchanges, lagged controls, and alternative rate/spread specifications discriminate mechanisms but do not randomly assign issuance or flow. Tables IAX–IAXIII (pp. 37–44) generally find flows related more consistently to lagged BTC-USD returns than to Tether-USD returns; Table IAXIV (p. 45) finds some cross-exchange-spread associations but very low adjusted R². Thus the evidence is consistent with price-support trading concentrated in linked wallets, while reverse causality, endogenous liquidity demand, arbitrage, omitted market conditions, and unidentified common control remain credible readings.
- Structure: Pages 1–2 state the appendix’s purpose and enumerate all exhibits. Section I (pp. 3–8) explains exchange identification, cross-blockchain and exchange-volume validation, linked-wallet inference, reverse flows, and formal flow definitions. Section II (p. 9) provides representative addresses for 20 exchange clusters. Section III (p. 10) studies authorization-to-transfer timing with a five-lag VAR; p. 11 contains the sole reference. Figures IA1–IA15 (pp. 12–27) move from blockchain topology and timing to label validation, 1LSg linkages, price events, placebos, volume matching, and recipient concentration. Tables IAI–IAXIV (pp. 28–45) progress from a raw transaction example through return, reversal, end-of-month, rate, and cross-exchange-spread tests.
- Depth: Effort is concentrated on institutional measurement and validation: public-label sourcing, cross-chain matching, address-level concentration, own-exchange volume placebos, representative addresses, and explicit net-flow equations (pp. 3–9). Robustness is extensive but mostly variations on OLS/event-time associations (pp. 29–45). Statistical uncertainty is reported through confidence intervals, robust or HAC standard errors, t-statistics, fixed effects, and 10,000 bootstrap draws (Figure IA3, p. 14; Figure IA12, p. 23; Tables IAII–IAXIV, pp. 29–45). Lighter areas are the underlying Bitcoin clustering algorithm, which is deferred to the main paper (p. 3), ownership validation beyond correlated flows, multiple-testing treatment across many coins/specifications, sensitivity to label errors, off-chain exchange activity, and structural identification of manipulation.
- Breadth: The appendix spans blockchain measurement, exchange microstructure, liquidity flows, return predictability, cross-asset transmission, reversals, end-of-month effects, stablecoin pricing, and arbitrage alternatives. It examines Bitcoin and six major altcoins (Table IAIII, p. 30), exchange-specific versus market-wide returns (Table IAIV, p. 31), 1LSg versus other Poloniex/Bittrex and other Tether-exchange flows (Figure IA12, p. 23; Tables IAV and IAXIII–IAXIV, pp. 32, 43–45), and Tether-USD measures constructed three ways (Tables IAX–IAXII, pp. 37–42). Declared empirical boundaries are the selected observable blockchains, identifiable exchanges and wallets, and chiefly the 2017–2018 boom; the design cannot observe internal exchange ledgers, fiat funding, customer identities, wallet-control changes, or later stablecoin regimes.
- Optics: The title immediately labels the artifact as an Internet Appendix, and p. 1 supplies a concise four-part roadmap followed by a complete exhibit inventory on pp. 1–2. The communication strategy is replication-forward: representative addresses appear on p. 9, wallet relationships are visualized in Figure IA7 (p. 18), and captions usually restate samples, variables, panels, and estimators. The exhibit ladder is coherent—network maps, timing, validation, events, then regression tables—but figures occupy 16 pages (pp. 12–27) and tables 18 pages (pp. 28–45), making the artifact exhibit-heavy relative to its ten pages of prose and methods. Notation is generally explicit but typographically dense; Equation IA6’s duplicated NetTetherFlow term (p. 8), “figure” for Table IAIX (p. 36), and the continuation of Figure IA14 onto an essentially captionless p. 26 weaken auditability. Claims about common control are calibrated as “strongly suggest” and “highly likely” (pp. 6–7), appropriately short of proof, whereas Figure IA13’s auditor-firing statement (p. 24) is rhetorically charged and uncited within this appendix.
- Locations: Purpose and inventory, pp. 1–2; label construction and public sources, Section I.A, p. 3; exchange-level cross-validation, Section I.B and Equation IA1, pp. 3–5, Figure IA5, p. 16; volume placebos, Section I.C, p. 5, Figure IA14, pp. 25–26; linked-wallet evidence, Section I.D, pp. 5–7, Equations IA2–IA3, p. 6, Figures IA7–IA9, pp. 18–20; formal flow definitions and the Equation IA6 anomaly, Section I.E, pp. 7–8; representative exchange addresses, Section II, p. 9; issuance timing and VAR Equation IA8, Section III, p. 10, Figures IA3–IA4, pp. 14–15; lagged-return asymmetry, Figure IA6, p. 17 and Table IAII, p. 29; price-event and matched-event evidence, Figures IA10–IA12, pp. 21–23; altcoin and exchange effects, Tables IAIII–IAIV, pp. 30–31; compounded-return and reversal tests, Tables IAV–IAVII, pp. 32–34; end-of-month tests, Tables IAVIII–IAIX, pp. 35–36; Tether-USD alternatives, Tables IAX–IAXIII, pp. 37–44; cross-exchange-spread alternative, Table IAXIV, p. 45.
- Implication: The appendix materially strengthens confidence that the authors identified exchange clusters and a distinctive linked-wallet channel, and it documents that the flow-return pattern is concentrated rather than a generic feature of all Tether venues. It strengthens measurement validity and mechanism discrimination, not causal identification: any project claim should say that the evidence is consistent with coordinated price-support activity and difficult to explain solely by measured Tether-dollar deviations or generic volume, while avoiding claims that ownership, intent, unbacked issuance, or manipulation is proven. The Equation IA6 discrepancy must be resolved against the main article or replication materials before reproducing the 1LSg measure.
