### Cong, Li, and Wang (2022), “Token-based platform finance”

- Status: Full-text read complete for the saved 20-page published article; the separately hosted supplementary material containing Appendices A–D is referenced but absent from the saved PDF, so appendix-dependent claims cannot be independently audited from this artifact.
- Roles: Published JFE venue exemplar; load-bearing theory and mechanism source for dynamic token-based platform finance, token overhang, endogenous token supply, blockchain-enabled commitment, and fee-financed investment; unsuitable as empirical or causal evidence.
- Source: Lin William Cong, Ye Li, and Neng Wang, “Token-based platform finance,” Journal of Financial Economics 144 (2022), 972–991, DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.10.002; saved as `2022-CongLiWang2022TokenPlatform-token-based-platform-finance.pdf`, 20 PDF pages.
- Version: Final published journal version; received September 8, 2020, revised May 4, 2021, accepted June 1, 2021, and available online October 10, 2021 (p. 972). The article subsumes earlier drafts titled “Token-Based Corporate Finance” and “Tokenomics and Platform Finance” (p. 972).
- Uses: Supports the theoretical claim that costly externally financed token repurchases create an endogenous issuance wedge and underinvestment even in a liquid token market (Section 3.1, pp. 978–979, Proposition 1 and Corollary 1, Eqs. 9–10); supports the mechanism claim that rule-based issuance can mitigate time inconsistency and token overhang, subject to policy calibration and more frequent buybacks (Section 5.1, pp. 985–988, Eqs. 34–37, Proposition 5, Fig. 6); supports fee financing as a model-based alternative that relaxes token-financing pressure while reducing participation and demand (Section 5.2, pp. 987–990, Eqs. 38–42, Fig. 7); provides a JFE exemplar for organizing a technically dense dynamic-theory paper.
- Scientific: The paper asks how a post-launch platform should issue, distribute, repurchase, and burn payment tokens while financing productivity growth, and what commitment value blockchain may provide. Its contribution is a continuous-time Markov model jointly determining investment, token supply, token price, and user adoption. Platform productivity follows a contribution-scaled diffusion (Eqs. 1–2, p. 976); heterogeneous users receive convenience yield with network effects and choose participation and token balances (Eqs. 3–6, pp. 977–978); the entrepreneur issues tokens for investment or payout and uses costly external funds for buybacks (Eqs. 7–8, p. 978). Market clearing generates endogenous token pricing and adoption (Propositions 2–3, Eqs. 11–19, pp. 979–980). The central mechanism requires three ingredients: costly buybacks, heterogeneous user surplus that owners cannot extract, and uncertain investment outcomes (pp. 978–979). Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 establish a wedge \(-V_M/P\) between owners’ marginal token cost and market price, producing “token overhang”; this is mechanism discrimination within the model, not causal identification. Parameterization reduces the state to normalized supply \(m=M/A'\) and solves coupled differential equations numerically (Section 4.1, pp. 981–982, Eqs. 20–33, Proposition 4). Figures 2–5 report calibrated comparative statics rather than estimated effects; parameters are borrowed or chosen for qualitative illustration, and robustness is asserted rather than displayed in the saved main text (p. 982). Commitment simulations show sizable value gains for token growth \(\mu_M=2\), but \(\mu_M=1\) can reduce value near the payout boundary, so blockchain commitment is not uniformly beneficial (pp. 987–988, Fig. 6). Boundary conditions include perfect token liquidity, risk-neutral pricing, exogenous contributor compensation and investment-efficiency shocks, quadratic adjustment costs, post-launch operation, and imperfect real-world blockchain commitment (pp. 973, 976–982). No dataset, measurement exercise, standard errors, statistical uncertainty, empirical identification, or causal estimate is provided.
- Structure: The abstract states the model, optimal supply regions, issuance wedge, underinvestment, and commitment result (p. 972). Section 1 motivates tokenized platforms, previews four mechanisms and applications, and positions the paper across token, platform, corporate-finance, monetary, blockchain, stablecoin, and gig-economy literatures (pp. 973–976). Section 2 builds the agents, productivity technology, user problem, entrepreneur objective, token accounting identity, and Markov-equilibrium definition (pp. 976–978). Section 3 derives optimal investment and supply, aggregate demand, adoption, pricing, and the durable-goods comparison (pp. 978–981). Section 4 parameterizes and reduces the model, states the equilibrium solution, then interprets platform value, investment, price, adoption, and comparative statics (pp. 981–986; Figs. 2–5). Section 5 studies predetermined token growth and user fees as responses to underinvestment (pp. 986–990; Figs. 6–7). Section 6 summarizes the mechanisms and implications (p. 990). References occupy pp. 990–991; separately hosted Appendices A–D and supplementary material are cited throughout but not included.
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in equilibrium construction and state reduction: the HJB and policy wedge (Section 3.1), user demand and market clearing (Sections 3.2–3.3), homogeneity transformation and free-boundary conditions (Section 4.1), and commitment/fee extensions (Section 5). Economic interpretation is unusually extensive around the issuance wedge, precaution, and time inconsistency (pp. 978–979, 983–988). Institutional depth in the saved article is lighter and largely illustrative—named platforms, burning practices, token caps, stablecoins, and fee examples appear mainly in notes and pp. 973–975, 985, and 990. Measurement, estimation, empirical validation, welfare aggregation, governance implementation, and formal robustness are light or absent. Several derivations, institutional examples, comparative statics, and the foundation for convenience yield are deferred to unavailable Appendices A–D (pp. 974, 977, 981, 985–986).
- Breadth: The model spans platform economics, dynamic corporate finance, monetary/token valuation, durable-goods monopoly, blockchain commitment, stablecoins, crowdsourcing, and the gig economy (pp. 973–976). It incorporates endogenous adoption, network effects, investment uncertainty, payout, repurchase, burning, and fees, while considering competition and user heterogeneity parametrically (Sections 4.1, 4.4, and 5). It discusses alternatives including fixed supply, discretionary versus predetermined issuance, collateralized stablecoins, token bonds, and user fees (pp. 981, 985–990). Declared boundaries include post-launch platforms, decentralized contributions rather than founders’ hidden effort, tokens as payment rather than dividend claims, consensus-protocol neutrality, imperfect blockchain commitment, non-optimized illustrative fees, quadratic investment costs, and a deliberately omitted capped-supply extension (pp. 973–976, 981–982, 986, 989–990). Credible alternative readings are that the main inefficiency is generated by the assumed repurchase-financing cost and surplus leakage, and that the commitment result demonstrates value from exogenous policy restriction rather than a blockchain-exclusive capability.
- Optics: The short functional title directly names the object and domain. The abstract follows a compact question–model–policy–friction–implication sequence and calibrates most statements as model results, although “Blockchain technology mitigates underinvestment” is broader than the later parameter-dependent evidence (p. 972 versus pp. 987–988). The introduction uses phenomenon-first motivation, states the normalized-supply state variable early, then advances a numbered contribution ladder before an extensive literature map (pp. 973–976). The exhibit ladder is disciplined: Fig. 1 supplies the institutional flow map; Fig. 2 establishes value, investment, issuance cost, and marginal productivity value; Fig. 3 covers price and adoption; Figs. 4–5 provide mechanism comparative statics; Figs. 6–7 evaluate remedies. Captions are self-contained about plotted objects and parameters, though the text must supply economic interpretation. Notation is dense but progressively defined, with propositions, corollaries, and lemmas furnishing reviewer-facing checkpoints. Prose is technical yet interpretation-heavy; pages 973–976 devote substantial space to mechanism and positioning, pages 976–982 to setup and solution, pages 982–986 to baseline results, and pages 986–990 to extensions and conclusion. Credibility benefits from explicit limiting assumptions and a non-monotone commitment result, but claims of robustness and real-world applicability outrun the evidence visible in the saved main article.

- Implication: Cite this paper for a coherent theoretical channel linking external financing frictions to token issuance, investment, and endogenous adoption—not as evidence that observed platforms underinvest or that blockchain causally improves investment. Any project claim that commitment unambiguously raises platform value must be narrowed: Fig. 6 shows gains depend on normalized supply and the committed growth rate, with low-growth commitment reducing value near the payout boundary. Claims relying on institutional examples, derivations, or robustness in Appendices A–D require retrieval of the supplementary artifact before closure.
