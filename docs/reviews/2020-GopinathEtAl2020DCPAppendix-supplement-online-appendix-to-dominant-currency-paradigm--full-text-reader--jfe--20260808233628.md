- Scientific: The appendix asks whether the dollar’s dominant role in international pricing and trade survives measurement checks, subsample splits, competing currency controls, firm-level evidence, and structural discipline. The macro data comprise importer-reported UN Comtrade HS6 values and quantities for 55 countries, generally over 1989–2015, covering 91.2% of world imports and 91.5% of exports in 2015; noncommodities exclude HS chapters 1–27 and 72–83, and chained Fisher indices aggregate filtered unit values (A.1.1, PDF pp. 2–3; Table 10, PDF pp. 4–5). BLS comparisons generally validate movement in the constructed indices but reveal excess volatility for some Asian series and a material Germany discrepancy caused by transport-vehicle unit values (A.1.3, Figs. 1–2, PDF pp. 3, 6–8). Macro panel evidence is descriptive and predictive rather than causally identified: regressions use fixed effects, exchange-rate lags, macro controls, weighting variants, and clustered uncertainty, but no instrument or plausibly exogenous dollar shock. Dollar price pass-through remains large across advanced/emerging flows (0.287–0.858 contemporaneously in Table 13), while dollar trade-volume coefficients are usually negative but heterogeneous (Table 14, PDF p. 10). Post-2002 country regressions estimate contemporaneous dollar pass-through of 0.106 into CPI and 0.284 into PPI; invoicing-share interactions are 0.181 and 0.237, respectively, with small-cluster corrected intervals, and the authors explicitly caution that magnitudes are imprecise with country and time fixed effects (Eq. A.1 and Table 15, PDF pp. 9–11). Without time fixed effects, dollar coefficients dominate euro coefficients after observed global controls: dollar trade-volume elasticities are −0.695 unweighted and −0.573 trade-weighted (Table 17, PDF p. 18), and remain −0.880 and −1.988 after adding franc and yen rates (Table 18, PDF p. 19). These results discriminate among currency predictors but remain vulnerable to the interpretation that broad dollar movements proxy omitted global or financial shocks. Colombian customs data cover the universe of firms at firm–product–country frequency during 2005–2015; transaction currency is observed for exports from 2007, with 98.28% of all export invoices and 98.39% of manufactured-export invoices denominated in dollars (A.3 and Table 20, PDF pp. 21–22). Dollarized-economy price pass-through is roughly 0.70–0.86 for exports and 0.97–1.04 for imports (Table 21, PDF p. 23); for nondollar partners, adding the dollar sharply reduces bilateral-rate coefficients while leaving dollar coefficients around 0.53–0.67 for prices (Table 22, PDF p. 24), and dollar volume elasticities are near −1 in many specifications (Tables 23–24, PDF pp. 25–26). Firm–industry–country fixed effects do not resolve common-shock endogeneity, and annual micro regressions cluster by year over a short sample without the small-cluster correction used in Table 15. The structural exercise calibrates steady-state targets and estimates nine parameters by minimum distance against eleven moments with inverse-variance diagonal weighting (A.5, PDF pp. 27–28); moment matching is close in Table 26, and Table 27 shows that dominant-currency pricing reproduces the observed attenuation of bilateral pass-through conditional on the dollar better than extreme producer- or local-currency pricing. Structural uncertainty, parameter standard errors, sensitivity to weighting/targets, and formal comparison of model fit are not reported here, so this is mechanism-consistent evidence rather than decisive mechanism identification.

- Depth: Effort is deepest in measurement defense and empirical robustness: the appendix discusses unit-value bias, HS-vintage transitions, reporting-currency conversion, country coverage, BLS validation, subsample stability, competing currencies, added controls, differentiated goods, and macro-to-micro replication (PDF pp. 2–26). Identification depth is lighter: the macro exercises establish conditional associations and predictive content, not causal effects, and invoicing shares may capture persistent country characteristics. Structural depth is compressed into calibration targets, a nine-parameter minimum-distance fit, eleven moments, and pricing-regime comparisons (PDF pp. 27–29); derivations reside in the absent main article, unit-value filtering details are deferred to Boz et al. (2019), and no parameter uncertainty, sensitivity analysis, or overidentification assessment appears here.
- Breadth: The evidence spans 55 advanced and emerging economies, 39 countries with invoicing shares, bilateral prices, quantities, terms of trade, CPI, PPI, four benchmark currencies, multiple time windows, and a universe of Colombian traders. It considers unit-value composition bias, financial-crisis timing, global controls, invoicing heterogeneity, strategic-complementarity/demand controls, differentiated products, and dominant-, producer-, and local-currency pricing. Declared boundaries are noncommodity trade for the main macro indices; annual data primarily from 1989–2015; post-euro comparisons from 2002–2015; Colombian firm data from 2005–2015 with export invoicing observed only from 2007; and manufactured samples excluding petrochemicals and metals, with Rauch differentiated-goods subsamples (PDF pp. 2–3, 12, 15, 21–26).
- Optics: As a supplement, the artifact has no abstract or argumentative introduction; its opening immediately supplies data provenance and measurement defenses (PDF pp. 1–2). The exhibit ladder is reviewer-facing and cumulative: sample disclosure and external validation (Tables 10–11, Figs. 1–2), macro heterogeneity and alternative currencies (Tables 12–19, Figs. 3–10), Colombian invoicing and regressions (Tables 20–24), then structural targets, moment fit, and pricing-regime comparisons (Tables 25–27). Captions usually identify samples, controls, weighting, clustering, and significance conventions, although several figures depend on main-paper baselines and notation such as H, R, eij, and invoicing shares is not fully reintroduced. Prose is dense but functional, with most page space allocated to exhibits: data/validation occupy PDF pp. 2–8, macro extensions pp. 9–20, micro evidence pp. 21–26, and structural estimation pp. 27–29. Claim calibration is strongest where the authors call trade results “predictive” and caution about imprecise inflation pass-through (PDF pp. 11–12), but phrases asserting a dollar “effect” or that other currencies “do nothing” should still be read as conditional association. Minor production issues include the duplicated “Figure Figure 6” caption (PDF p. 16); the pervasive split-character artifacts in the supplied text appear extraction-related rather than evidence about the published typography.

- Implication: The appendix can substantiate carefully bounded claims that Comtrade unit-value indices receive external validation, dollar movements have greater conditional predictive power for prices and trade than bilateral or other major currency movements, invoicing shares covary with pass-through, and Colombian micro moments are more consistent with dominant-currency pricing than with the two extreme alternatives. It does not by itself support causal language about dollar appreciation reducing world trade or uniquely identify the invoicing mechanism.
