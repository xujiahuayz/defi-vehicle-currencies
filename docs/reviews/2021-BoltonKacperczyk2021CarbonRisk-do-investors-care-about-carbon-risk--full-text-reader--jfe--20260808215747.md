### Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021), “Do investors care about carbon risk?”

- Status: Source-set incomplete: the published article was read sequentially in full, including all 16 tables, seven figures, four equations, notes, conclusion, and references across saved PDF pages 1–33 (journal pages 517–549), but the repeatedly cited Online Appendix was not supplied or read; companion-dependent claims therefore remain unaudited.
- Roles: Independent full-text second reader; load-bearing claim verification; published-versus-working-paper check; JFE venue exemplar for empirical asset-pricing communication.
- Source: Patrick Bolton and Marcin Kacperczyk, “Do investors care about carbon risk?”, Journal of Financial Economics 142 (2021), 517–549, DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.05.008; saved as 2021-BoltonKacperczyk2021CarbonRisk-do-investors-care-about-carbon-risk.pdf.
- Version: Published version of record; received October 21, 2019, revised September 24, 2020, accepted October 23, 2020, and available online May 14, 2021 (p. 517). This is not an accepted manuscript or working paper.
- Companions: Incomplete: an Online Appendix containing Tables A.1–A.15 is invoked for reporting selection, reduced-control specifications, return timing, ownership, alternative industry classifications, matching balance, crisis exclusion, Paris subsamples, disclosed-versus-estimated emissions, aggregate emissions, transformations, and legacy-sample tests (pp. 525, 530, 533, 536–539, 541, 543–544, 547–548), but was not supplied or read. Mahalanobis-matching results are described as qualitatively similar but not shown (p. 544), and an imputed-emission-growth analysis is available only upon request (p. 547). No source-discovery record establishing the presence or absence of corrections or corrigenda was supplied.
- Uses: Supports the claim that, in the authors’ 2005–2017 US sample, total scope 1–3 emissions and annual changes in emissions are conditionally positively associated with contemporaneous monthly returns, whereas emission intensity is not (Table 8, pp. 532–533; Figs. 4–6, pp. 534–536). Supports the narrower claim that conventional factor returns do not absorb the estimated characteristic premia (Table 10, pp. 540–541), and that institutional underweighting is associated with scope 1 intensity and concentrated in salient oil and gas, utility, and transportation industries (Tables 11 and 13, pp. 542 and 544). It is also a useful JFE exemplar for a hypothesis-led introduction, extensive measurement section, and exhibit-driven empirical sequence (pp. 517–548). It does not by itself support a causal claim that carbon risk raises expected returns or that investor divestment causes prices to change.
- Scientific: The paper asks whether corporate carbon emissions enter the cross-section of US stock returns and institutional holdings, positioning three interpretations: compensation for carbon risk, inefficient underpricing or “carbon alpha,” and sin-stock-style divestment (pp. 518–520). It matches Trucost EDX emissions to FactSet returns, fundamentals, and ownership for 3,421 US-listed firms over 2005–2017 (pp. 521–529). Measures distinguish scope 1 direct emissions, scope 2 purchased-energy emissions, and scope 3 upstream supply-chain emissions; each is represented by logged total emissions, annual growth, and emissions per sales (pp. 519, 521–523). Scope 3 is estimated through an input-output model and excludes downstream product-use emissions; estimated observations are common, provider agreement is materially weaker for estimates than reports, and coverage expands sharply in 2016 (pp. 521–526; Tables 2–4 and Fig. 2). The main pooled regressions use monthly returns, annual emissions, firm controls, year-month fixed effects, optional industry fixed effects, and standard errors clustered by firm and year (Eq. 1, p. 530; Table 8, pp. 532–533). With industry effects, coefficients on logged scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions are 0.164 (SE 0.036), 0.167 (0.048), and 0.312 (0.071); corresponding growth coefficients are 0.627 (0.144), 0.321 (0.120), and 1.186 (0.314), while all intensity coefficients are insignificant (Table 8). The authors summarize one-standard-deviation monthly effects as 15, 24, and 33 basis points for emission levels and 26, 18, and 31 basis points for changes (p. 519). Mechanism discrimination removes earnings-announcement-day returns (Table 9, p. 538), conditions estimated monthly premia on nine factor returns (Eq. 2 and Table 10, pp. 538–541), compares holdings across investor classes (Eq. 3 and Table 11, pp. 539–542), excludes salient industries (Tables 12–13, pp. 543–544), studies Paris Agreement subsamples and a matched event design (Eq. 4 and Tables 14–15, pp. 543–546), and imputes 1990s emissions (Table 16, p. 547). These are observational association and partial mechanism-discrimination exercises, not causal identification of required risk compensation: emissions can proxy for omitted operating scale, commodity exposure, regulatory sensitivity, or product-market risk; removing announcement-day returns does not remove all cash-flow news; and failure of known factors to explain a characteristic slope does not establish covariance with a priced carbon factor. The reported fading of coefficients after emissions become public, discussed from missing Table A.3 (pp. 535–538), is also compatible with gradual repricing or limited attention rather than a persistent equilibrium premium. The Paris design has one common event, residual matching imbalance, no parallel-trends evidence in the article, significance only for scope 1 levels, and a post-2015 result that disappears in the legacy sample according to missing Table A.6 (pp. 543–546). Ownership evidence discriminates against divestment as the sole explanation because returns load on levels and changes while holdings load on intensity, but holdings regressions do not causally identify ESG preferences. There is an internal attribution discrepancy: the introduction names insurance companies, pension funds, and mutual funds as underweight scope 1 intensity (p. 519), whereas Table 11 and the results text identify insurance companies, independent advisers, and pension funds; the investment-company or mutual-fund coefficient is positive and insignificant (pp. 539–542).
- Structure: Pages 517–520 introduce the policy setting, formulate the carbon-risk, carbon-alpha, and divestment hypotheses, summarize findings, and position the climate-finance literature. Section 2 (pp. 521–529) builds the sample: Section 2.1 defines emissions and documents coverage and industries through Tables 1–6 and Figs. 1–3; Sections 2.2–2.4 define return, factor, and ownership variables. Section 3 opens with emission determinants (Section 3.1, pp. 530–531; Table 7), then presents the main return regressions and timing tests (Section 3.2, pp. 530–538; Table 8, Figs. 4–6, and Table 9), factor adjustment (Section 3.3, pp. 538–541; Table 10), ownership and divestment (Section 3.4, pp. 539–541; Table 11 and Fig. 7), salient-industry categorization (Section 3.5, pp. 541–544; Tables 12–13), investor awareness and Paris/1990s exercises (Section 3.6, pp. 542–547; Tables 14–16), and a synopsis of companion robustness tests (Section 3.7, pp. 547–548). Section 4 gives a short conclusion on p. 548; references occupy pp. 548–549.
- Depth: Effort is concentrated on constructing and explaining emissions measures, separating scopes and levels from intensity, documenting industry and time-series variation, and iterating the central association through controls, fixed effects, earnings adjustments, factor regressions, ownership splits, industry exclusions, subperiods, and imputation. The nine-page data section (pp. 521–529) is unusually substantial and gives the measurement architecture real visibility. Identification depth is lighter: the core evidence remains contemporaneous pooled association; the event and historical-imputation designs require strong assumptions; annual emissions are repeated across monthly observations; and firm-by-year clustering relies on only 13 year clusters. Much of the decisive timing, selection, and robustness evidence is outsourced to the unread Online Appendix.
- Breadth: The paper spans cross-sectional asset pricing, climate-policy risk, technological transition risk, commodity exposure, market inefficiency, limited attention, sin-stock exclusion, sparse industry categorization, institutional ownership, disclosure, and climate-finance research (pp. 518–520, 523–525, 538–547). It considers three emission scopes, three exposure constructions, nine conventional factors, six investor categories, salient versus nonsalient industries, Paris-era attention, and a 1990s placebo-like comparison. Declared boundaries are US-listed firms covered by Trucost during 2005–2017, incomplete firm disclosure, estimated emissions, omission of downstream scope 3, and weak post-Paris power (pp. 521–523, 543–547). It does not estimate policy-induced cash-flow losses, a structural carbon price, or a traded carbon factor, and does not establish external validity outside the US sample or later climate-policy regimes.
- Optics: The interrogative title is memorable but broader than the actual US 2005–2017 design. The abstract efficiently states the question, principal measures, controls, holdings result, and a calibrated “consistent with” interpretation (p. 517). The opening moves from conventional return predictors to Paris-era salience, then gives three rival hypotheses before revealing data and results (pp. 517–520), a strong reviewer-facing architecture. The exhibit ladder is deliberate: descriptive measurement and sample exhibits first, then determinants, baseline returns, cumulative premia, earnings adjustment, factors, ownership, industry exclusions, attention, and historical imputation. Captions are generally detailed and variables are centralized in Table 1, while four simple equations keep notation light. Credibility is aided by reporting economic magnitudes, null intensity results, sample-expansion caveats, and limits of the Paris comparison. Auditability is weakened by cross-reference slippage: Figs. 4–6 say their controls follow Table 7 although the relevant return specification is Eq. 1/Table 8 (pp. 534–536); p. 525 points the alternative industry-classification check to Table A.4 whereas Section 3.7 assigns it to Table A.9 (p. 547); and the discussion around pp. 543–544 shifts between Table 15 and Table A.7. The double-column article devotes roughly four pages to framing, nine to data, eighteen to results and robustness, less than one to conclusion, and two overlapping pages to references; prose is dense but signposted, with exhibits carrying most quantitative detail.

- Implication: Cite the paper as evidence that Bolton and Kacperczyk document a robust conditional association between total or changing corporate emissions and contemporaneous stock returns in their Trucost-covered US sample, alongside scope-1-intensity-based institutional screening concentrated in salient industries (Table 8, pp. 532–533; Tables 11–13, pp. 542–544). Phrase the economic interpretation as consistent with investors pricing carbon risk, matching the abstract’s calibration (p. 517), not as established causal compensation for a priced systematic factor. Any reliance on lag timing, sample selection, alternative classifications, disclosure splits, or legacy-sample robustness must remain provisional until the Online Appendix is saved and read.
