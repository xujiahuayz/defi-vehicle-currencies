### Gopinath et al. (2020), Dominant Currency Paradigm

- Status: Published article read sequentially in full, including all tables, figures, notes, conclusion, and references; source-set review remains incomplete because the explicitly cited online appendix and article-page materials were not supplied or read.
- Roles: Independent second read; load-bearing theory and empirical claim verification for dominant/vehicle-currency research; research-communication exemplar from the American Economic Review, not a JFE venue exemplar.
- Source: Gita Gopinath, Emine Boz, Camila Casas, Federico J. Díez, Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, and Mikkel Plagborg-Møller, “Dominant Currency Paradigm,” American Economic Review 110(3), March 2020, pp. 677–719, DOI 10.1257/aer.20171201; saved as 2020-GopinathEtAl2020DCP-dominant-currency-paradigm.pdf, 43 PDF pages.
- Version: Final published version. The author note states that it combines the working papers Casas et al. (2016), “Dominant Currency Paradigm,” and Boz, Gopinath, and Plagborg-Møller (2017), “Global Trade and the Dollar” (p. 677); these precursors should not be treated as the reviewed version.

The article directs readers to AEA-hosted additional materials and disclosure statements (p. 677) and repeatedly relies on an online appendix: simulation details (p. 689); A.1, Comtrade construction, unit-value validation, and macro-data sources (p. 697); A.2.1, country-group decompositions (pp. 698, 700, 703, 708); A.2.2, CPI/PPI pass-through (pp. 698, 707); A.2.3, global-cycle controls and euro comparisons (pp. 706, 708); A.2.4, Swiss-franc and yen controls (pp. 707–708); A.2.5, added controls, samples, persistence, and invoicing-weight robustness (pp. 699–700, 703, 706, 708); A.3, Colombian data (p. 708); A.4, Colombian micro robustness (pp. 709–710); and A.5, model parameterization and PCP/LCP comparisons (pp. 712–713). No correction or corrigendum determination can be made from the supplied artifact alone.
- Uses: Supports the formal claim that, with prices rigid in a common dominant currency, bilateral terms-of-trade are locally exchange-rate-insensitive and import prices and quantities respond principally to the importer–dominant-currency rate (Section I.D, Proposition 1 and equations 16–19, pp. 686–689). Supports descriptive and predictive—not causal—claims that the dollar dominates bilateral exchange rates in global price pass-through and trade-volume regressions (Section II.C–D, Tables 3–4 and Figures 4–6, pp. 699–703), that US imports are unusually exchange-rate-insensitive (Section II.E, Figure 7 and Table 5, pp. 703–705), and that broad dollar appreciation predicts lower third-country trade (Section II.F, equation 22 and Figure 8, pp. 705–707). Provides Colombian micro-level corroboration and model-based mechanism discrimination (Section III, Tables 6–9 and Figures 10–13, pp. 709–716). It does not establish that dollar invoicing is exogenous, causally identifies the effect of invoicing choice, or explain endogenously why the dollar became dominant (footnote 8, p. 689; conclusion, p. 717).
- Scientific: The paper asks whether international price and quantity adjustment is better described by a dominant currency paradigm combining dominant-currency pricing, strategic pricing complementarities, and imported inputs than by producer- or local-currency pricing (pp. 678–682). Its theoretical contribution is a three-region dynamic New Keynesian model with Kimball demand, Calvo price and wage rigidity, roundabout production, incomplete international asset markets, and alternative invoicing regimes (equations 1–19, pp. 682–690). Proposition 1 derives sharp short-run predictions; calibrated simulations show asymmetric monetary-policy transmission and stronger global spillovers from dominant-country shocks (Figures 1–3, pp. 691–695). Empirically, the paper constructs chained Fisher bilateral price and volume indices from HS6 UN Comtrade data for over 2,500 ordered country pairs, 1992–2015, covering 91 percent of world trade and excluding broadly defined commodities (pp. 696–697), then uses Colombian firm–10-digit-product–country customs observations to address aggregation and composition concerns (pp. 708–710). Dyad and year fixed-effect regressions produce a contemporaneous noncommodity terms-of-trade coefficient of 0.0369, SE 0.00863, without PPI controls and −0.00938, SE 0.0130, with them (Table 2, p. 698); adding the dollar rate reduces unweighted bilateral price pass-through from 0.757 to 0.164 while the dollar coefficient is 0.781 (Table 3, p. 700); analogous unweighted volume coefficients move from −0.119 to −0.0310 while the dollar coefficient is −0.186 (Table 4, p. 703). The implied US import-volume elasticity is approximately −0.121 + 0.124 = 0.003 (Table 5, p. 705), and a uniform 1 percent dollar appreciation predicts about a 0.6 percent contraction in rest-of-world trade within a year (Figure 8, p. 707). Colombian price pass-through begins near one and declines gradually, while dollar rates displace bilateral rates in annual price and quantity specifications (Figure 10 and Tables 6–7, pp. 710–712); calibrated counterfactuals fit these dynamics better under DCP, with imported inputs and variable markups materially improving export-price pass-through fit (Figures 11–13, pp. 713–717). Identification is reduced-form and predictive: exchange-rate innovations and invoicing shares are not instrumented, importer-year fixed effects cannot be used in the key volume regressions, and the authors explicitly state that the coefficients are not structural demand elasticities (pp. 701–702) and that the aggregate dollar result is not structural (p. 707). Credible alternatives include offsetting PCP/LCP shares, flexible-price strategic-complementarity models, global-cycle or safe-haven confounding, bilateral-rate collinearity, composition and unit-value bias, and sample-period dependence; the article addresses these through additional price evidence, time effects or cycle proxies, alternative currencies, microdata, and simulations, but several decisive checks reside only in the unread online appendix (pp. 698–708). Persistence is particularly qualified: full-sample volume effects largely disappear after one to two years, whereas persistent effects arise in the 2002–2015 subsample (p. 703).
- Structure: The abstract and introduction define the three-feature paradigm, contrast it with PCP/LCP, enumerate four predictions and headline estimates, position the contribution, and outline the paper (pp. 677–682). Section I builds households, production, and pricing (Sections I.A–C, equations 1–15, pp. 683–686), derives testable implications (Section I.D, equations 16–19, pp. 686–689), then closes and calibrates the model and compares monetary-policy transmission in small and large open economies (Section I.E, Table 1 and Figures 1–3, pp. 689–695). Section II constructs the global data (II.A, pp. 696–697), tests terms-of-trade stability (II.B, pp. 697–698), price pass-through (II.C, pp. 699–701), volume elasticities (II.D, pp. 701–703), US asymmetry (II.E, pp. 703–705), rest-of-world trade and inflation (II.F, pp. 705–707), and robustness (II.G, p. 708). Section III introduces Colombian microdata, reports reduced-form results (III.A, Figure 9–10 and Tables 6–7, pp. 708–712), and matches those results with calibrated DCP/PCP/LCP counterfactuals and component shutoffs (III.B, Tables 8–9 and Figures 11–13, pp. 711–716). Section IV summarizes policy implications and the exogenous-invoicing boundary (pp. 716–717); references occupy pp. 718–719.
- Depth: Analytical depth is concentrated in the explicit price-setting, imported-input, and strategic-complementarity mechanisms and their monetary-policy simulations (pp. 682–695). Empirical depth is concentrated in the progression from global dyadic prices to quantities, US asymmetry, aggregate third-country trade, Colombian microdata, and model-generated counterfactuals (pp. 695–716). Measurement receives a candid discussion of unit-value bias and a stated BLS validation, but implementation details are deferred to online Appendix A.1 (p. 697). Causal identification is light by design: the paper estimates conditional associations and forecasting relationships rather than isolating exogenous invoicing or exchange-rate variation (pp. 701–702, 707). Mechanism discrimination is stronger than a purely descriptive exercise because PCP, LCP, DCP, and component shutoffs generate divergent dynamics, but it remains conditional on a calibrated model whose estimation details are in unread Appendix A.5 (pp. 712–715). Much of the robustness burden is similarly deferred to the online appendix (p. 708).
- Breadth: The paper integrates international New Keynesian monetary economics, exchange-rate pass-through, trade elasticities, pricing-to-market, imported-input production, currency invoicing, global trade fluctuations, and monetary spillovers. It confronts PCP, LCP, a 50–50 mixture interpretation, flexible-price variable-markup models, the euro as an alternative dominant currency, advanced/emerging-market heterogeneity, crisis-period dependence, safe-haven currencies, composition bias, and differentiated-product restrictions (pp. 681–682, 698–708). Declared boundaries are annual global data, country-level rather than bilateral invoicing-share proxies, one-country micro evidence from heavily dollarized Colombia, noncommodity/manufacturing emphasis, reduced-form rather than structural volume elasticities, exogenous currency choice, and limited power to trace trade responses from identified monetary-policy shocks (pp. 697, 700, 702, 708–709; footnote 8, p. 689; p. 717).
- Optics: The title names a replacement “paradigm,” and the abstract efficiently moves from three primitives to two datasets and four numbered empirical findings with magnitudes (p. 677). The opening first stages PCP and LCP, introduces the three empirical departures motivating DCP, lists its predictions, reports headline estimates, and only then maps the literature and paper (pp. 677–682). The exhibit ladder is unusually disciplined: Table 1 and Figures 1–3 establish model contrasts; Tables 2–5 and Figures 4–8 test successive global predictions; Figure 9, Tables 6–9, and Figures 10–13 move to micro corroboration and mechanism fit (pp. 691–717). Captions generally identify samples, weighting, controls, confidence intervals, and clustering, while impulse-response graphics make dynamic contrasts legible. Notation is dense but stable, with equations and a named proposition preceding empirical specifications. Claim calibration is mixed: the article explicitly labels volume and aggregate-dollar estimates predictive and nonstructural (pp. 702, 707), yet uses stronger language such as invoicing shares “cause” pass-through changes (p. 700) and competing paradigms being “soundly rejected” (p. 716), neither warranted as a standalone causal statement. Page allocation signals reviewer-facing seriousness: approximately fourteen pages for model and simulations, thirteen for global evidence, eight for Colombian evidence and matching, two for conclusion, and two for references.

- Implication: This is strong load-bearing support for treating vehicle-currency invoicing as a determinant of which exchange rate predicts trade prices and quantities, and for modeling a dominant currency as a source of asymmetric third-country spillovers. Project language should preserve the paper’s evidentiary level: “predicts,” “is associated with,” or “is consistent with DCP,” not “causes,” unless relying specifically on the model’s conditional mechanism. The paper cannot by itself establish endogenous dollar dominance, a causal invoicing effect, or universal long-horizon trade contraction. Any central use should remain provisionally open until the online appendix is saved and read.
