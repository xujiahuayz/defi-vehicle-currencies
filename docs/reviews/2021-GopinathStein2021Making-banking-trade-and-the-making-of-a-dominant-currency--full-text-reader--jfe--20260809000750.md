- Uses: Supports a model-based claim that invoice-currency use and demand for safe assets in that currency can reinforce one another and generate dominant-currency equilibria (abstract p. 783; introduction pp. 785–791; Sections IV–V, pp. 805–822; Propositions 4–5, pp. 809–817). Supports the narrower mechanism that heavy dollar invoicing raises hedging demand for dollar claims and can require cheaper dollar borrowing, foreign-currency collateral creation, and balance-sheet mismatch (Sections II–III, pp. 791–805; Propositions 1–3, pp. 795–804; Figures I–II, pp. 797, 804). Supports only descriptive, not causal, evidence that import-invoicing currency shares covary with domestic-bank foreign-currency liability shares (Section VI.B, pp. 824–826; Figure VII, pp. 825–826). It does not establish that a digital or DeFi currency will become dominant; Libra is presented only as a possible application of the mechanism (pp. 789, 828).
- Scientific: The question is how a currency can jointly dominate trade invoicing and global finance, confer low expected returns on its safe assets, and induce foreign borrowers to accept currency mismatch (pp. 783–791). The contribution is a unified general-equilibrium feedback: sticky dollar prices make dollar claims better consumption hedges; high demand for those claims pushes their price above that of otherwise safe local-currency claims; banks and firms then use increasingly unsuitable local-currency collateral to manufacture dollar claims, requiring a dollar funding advantage; exporters invoice in dollars to align revenues with that cheap funding, further increasing dollar-safe-asset demand (pp. 793–798, 800–805). Propositions 1–3 derive the UIP premium, endogenous invoicing, and mismatch; Propositions 4–5 derive multiple and asymmetric dominant-currency equilibria under stated feedback and cutoff conditions (pp. 795–817). The empirical component combines country-level invoicing shares from Gopinath (2015) with BIS Locational Banking Statistics; after excluding the United States, euro-area members, Brazil, and India, the principal sample has 10 countries and R-squared 0.72, while a narrower loans-and-deposits/nonbank-counterparty measure has eight countries and R-squared 0.82 (pp. 824–826, Figure VII). These are correlations without a causal design, reported regression coefficients, standard errors, confidence intervals, or demonstrated robustness in the article. Measurement requires importers to deposit locally and Treasury-like holdings to be bank-intermediated (p. 824). Important model boundaries include sticky preset prices, inelastic import demand, mean-variance utility, segmented asset access, exogenous exchange-rate distributions, collateral constraints, globally integrated hard-currency deposit markets, and a bang-bang invoicing choice (pp. 790–796, 798–802, 806–807, 812–815). The paper explicitly excludes high-frequency UIP violations (p. 798 n.11), leaves monetary policy and exchange rates outside the baseline (pp. 798–799), and treats equilibrium selection through history heuristically rather than through a formal dynamic model (pp. 820–821). A credible alternative reading of Figure VII is that geography and trade integration with the euro area jointly determine invoicing and bank funding; the authors themselves note this pattern (p. 826). The hedging objection is addressed through collateral-intensive derivatives contracting, but that friction is discussed rather than embedded in the baseline model (pp. 804–805).
- Structure: Section I opens with five facts—trade invoicing, bank funding, corporate borrowing, reserves, and low dollar returns—then states the feedback mechanism, literature position, preliminary evidence, and staged roadmap (pp. 783–791). Section II builds the one-EM model with exogenous dollar invoicing, separating investors, importers, banks, and market clearing; it derives the UIP violation and mismatch threshold and then discusses exchange rates and the bank–firm interpretation (pp. 791–800; Propositions 1–2; Figure I). Section III endogenizes exporter invoicing, derives the financing motive and four parameter regions, and handles the FX-hedging objection (pp. 800–805; Proposition 3; Figure II). Section IV closes the cross-country feedback loop with a continuum of EMs and derives multiple equilibria (pp. 805–811; Proposition 4; Figures III–IV). Section V introduces symmetric dollar and euro candidates, distinguishes stable and unstable dual-currency equilibria, derives single-currency dominance, supplies a numerical example, and discusses history and unequal fundamentals or safe-asset supplies (pp. 811–822; Proposition 5; Table I; Figures V–VI). Section VI applies the mechanism to monetary history and renminbi internationalization, presents the eight- and ten-country descriptive evidence, and summarizes the companion reserve model (pp. 822–827; Figure VII). Section VII concludes with persistence, discontinuous displacement, and digital-currency applications (pp. 827–828), followed by supplementary-material and data statements and references (pp. 828–830).
- Depth: Analytical effort is concentrated in the staged model, comparative statics, equilibrium cutoffs, and stability distinctions across Sections II–V (pp. 791–822), with five propositions, nine numbered equations, six theory figures, and a calibrated numerical illustration. Institutional interpretation is substantial around bank safe-asset production, corporate mismatch, constrained hedging, historical dollar–sterling transition, and reserve management (pp. 798–805, 820–827). Identification, statistical inference, and measurement validation are light: the article’s direct empirical contribution is one small cross-country correlation exercise (pp. 824–826). Formal proof depth cannot be audited because proofs are delegated to unread Online Appendix A.1–A.5.2.
- Breadth: The article connects trade invoicing and pass-through, international safe assets, banking and collateral creation, corporate currency mismatch, UIP, vehicle currencies, reserve holdings, monetary history, renminbi internationalization, and private digital currencies (pp. 783–790, 822–828). It considers exogenous versus endogenous invoicing, one versus many EMs, one versus two candidate global currencies, symmetric versus asymmetric fundamentals, stable versus unstable equilibria, unequal safe-asset supplies, hedging, and bank-versus-corporate risk bearing (pp. 791–822). Declared boundaries include no formal central-bank reserve choice in this article, no active monetary policy, no high-frequency UIP account, and no endogenous currency liquidity or stability for novel currencies (pp. 788, 798, 826–828).
- Optics: The title names both the proposed inputs and the outcome; the abstract efficiently states four model results and the unifying claim (p. 783). The introduction establishes relevance through five quantitatively anchored facts before giving the intuition, contribution relative to two literatures, preliminary-evidence qualification, and roadmap (pp. 783–791). Claims are generally calibrated as theoretical mechanisms, possible equilibria, “preliminary evidence,” and interpretive lenses, although the concluding language about abrupt future currency displacement remains model-contingent rather than empirically estimated (pp. 789, 827–828). The exhibit ladder follows the model’s construction: Figure I establishes invoicing-to-premium and borrowing; Figure II adds endogenous invoicing; Figures III–IV add strategic complementarity; Figure V maps equilibrium regions; Table I and Figure VI instantiate them numerically; Figure VII supplies the sole direct empirical association (pp. 797–826). Captions are concise and depend heavily on surrounding prose; notation is dense but introduced incrementally. Roughly pp. 791–822 are devoted to theory, pp. 822–827 to applications and evidence, and pp. 827–830 to conclusion, disclosures, and references. Reviewer-facing credibility comes from explicit propositions, parameter cutoffs, objections framed as remarks, appendix proof pointers, and a replication DOI, but independent auditability is weakened here by the missing appendix and replication files.

- Implication: For research on vehicle currencies or DeFi-denominated trade and finance, the paper supplies a precise candidate mechanism: transactional denomination can create hedging demand for same-currency safe claims, while cheaper same-currency funding can feed back into denomination choices and produce path-dependent dominance. The admissible use is theoretical motivation or mechanism comparison, anchored in Propositions 1–5 and pp. 791–822. The paper does not by itself verify this loop causally, demonstrate it for crypto or stablecoins, or show that scale alone is sufficient; its digital-currency discussion expressly takes liquidity and value stability as unresolved prerequisites (p. 828).
